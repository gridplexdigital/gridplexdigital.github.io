LESSON 069 / 123 · Game Theory & Cooperation

The Prisoner’s Dilemma in everyday life

People can make individually sensible, self-protecting choices that leave everyone worse off. The structure of incentives often matters far more than anyone's stated intentions.

⚡ 2-Minute Field Note 📖 Looking for the full study? Read the 12-minute deep dive with book quotes →

The Core Mental Model: Defection Traps

In a Prisoner's Dilemma, each actor has a dominant personal incentive to protect themselves by betraying the other ("defecting"). When both act defensively, both suffer a far worse fate than if they had coordinated to trust each other ("cooperating").

Why Preaching Trust Fails

Exhorting people to "be more trusting" in a one-shot interaction is asking them to be exploited. If the counterpart defects, the cooperative person suffers maximum damage. Cooperation requires restructuring the payoffs.

The Power of Repeated Play

When parties anticipate dealing with each other repeatedly—what game theorists call the shadow of the future—cooperation spontaneously emerges through clear reciprocity.

⚡ Tactical Heuristic: Axelrod's Tit-for-Tat Rule

In ongoing relationships, practice the four winning principles: 1. Be nice (never defect first); 2. Be provocable (push back immediately if exploited); 3. Be forgiving (restore cooperation immediately once the other party reforms); and 4. Be crystal clear (let your boundaries be completely predictable).

A QUESTION TO CARRY

Where in your life or workplace are you and a counterpart trapped in mutual suspicion because neither side can afford the risk of making the first cooperative move?
