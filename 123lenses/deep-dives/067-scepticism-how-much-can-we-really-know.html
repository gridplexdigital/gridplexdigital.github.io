Scepticism: How Much Can We Really Know?

Scepticism is not cynical nihilism or the denial of practical reality. It is a rigorous, therapeutic mental discipline designed to liberate the mind from dogmatic overconfidence by systematically inspecting the hidden assumptions beneath everything we claim to know.

In the fourth century BCE, a Greek painter and philosopher named Pyrrho of Elis accompanied the army of Alexander the Great on its monumental conquest across Persia and into the Indus River valley. In India, Pyrrho encountered the gymnosophists—naked ascetic yogis and Buddhist sages who had abandoned worldly possessions, family obligations, and theological disputes to practice radical mental stillness. Pyrrho returned to Greece transformed. He observed that human misery does not originate in physical reality, but in our unexamined, passionate attachment to opinions. Humans become violently enraged, go to war, and torture themselves because they cling dogmatically to beliefs that cannot be definitively proven. Pyrrho realized that every argument can be countered by an equally persuasive opposing argument—a balance of reasons known as isostheneia. Instead of agonizing over which unprovable dogma to champion, Pyrrho proposed an audacious cognitive pivot: practice epoche, the deliberate, systematic suspension of judgment. The moment you stop insisting that you possess absolute truth, the psychological fever breaks. A profound, unshakeable tranquility—which the ancient Greeks called ataraxia—naturally fills the void left by discarded certainties. Classical scepticism was born not as an academic parlour game of hair-splitting doubt, but as a practical, therapeutic art of living.

1. The Pyrrhonian Path: Sextus Empiricus and Agrippa's Trilemma

The definitive record of ancient scepticism survives through the medical writings of Sextus Empiricus, a second-century Roman physician who codified the traditions of the Pyrrhonian school in his Outlines of Pyrrhonism. Sextus observed that philosophers divide into three distinct camps:

  1. The Dogmatists: Thinkers (such as Aristotle or the Stoics) who claim they have discovered the final, indisputable truth.
  2. The Academic Sceptics: Thinkers who assert dogmatically that truth is permanently unknowable—a claim that is itself a self-contradictory dogma.
  3. The Pyrrhonists (True Sceptics): Perpetual seekers who continue investigating reality without declaring that knowledge has been permanently achieved or permanently foreclosed, holding every conviction lightly.

To demonstrate why dogmatic certainty is logically impossible, the ancient sceptic Agrippa formulated what modern epistemologists call Agrippa’s Trilemma (or the Münchhausen Trilemma). Whenever you make a foundational truth claim, and an interlocutor asks you to justify it, you are trapped in one of three inescapable logical dead ends:

  • Infinite Regress: You justify Claim A with Reason B; you justify Reason B with Reason C; and so on ad infinitum. Because the chain of justification has no final terminus, the claim is never fully grounded.
  • Circular Reasoning: You justify Claim A by appealing to Claim B, but Claim B secretly relies upon Claim A (e.g., claiming a sacred scripture is infallible because the scripture itself states it was dictated by an infallible deity).
  • Axiomatic Dogmatism: To halt the infinite regress, you arbitrarily declare a starting assumption to be self-evident, demanding that others accept it on blind faith.

Agrippa’s Trilemma reveals that no system of human thought—whether metaphysics, political ideology, or economic doctrine—can provide an absolute, self-contained, unassailable logical foundation. Recognizing this structural limit is the beginning of genuine intellectual maturity.

2. Methodological Doubt: René Descartes and the Dream Argument

In the seventeenth century, French polymath René Descartes took the tools of ancient scepticism and deployed them toward a radically different goal. In his 1641 masterwork, Meditations on First Philosophy, Descartes set out to rebuild Western science upon an unshakeable bedrock of certainty.

Descartes realized that much of what he had believed since childhood was false or dubious. To clear away this intellectual rot, he devised Methodological Doubt: treating any belief that carried the slightest possibility of doubt as if it were completely false.

Descartes systematically dismantled his cognitive architecture through three escalating stages:

  • The Unreliability of the Senses: Our sensory organs regularly mislead us: a straight stick looks bent when submerged in water, and distant square towers appear round. A prudent thinker never fully trusts an informant that has deceived them even once.
  • The Dream Argument: How can you prove with absolute certainty that you are currently awake reading these words, and not asleep in bed dreaming this vivid encounter? The sensory clarity of a dream often matches waking life with total fidelity.
  • The Evil Demon (Malin Génie): Suppose there exists an omnipotent, malicious entity dedicated to deceiving your mind, causing you to believe that 2 + 3 = 5, or that an external physical world exists, when in fact nothing exists at all.

Having stripped away the external world, his physical body, and sensory inputs, Descartes arrived at an irreducible point that withstood all doubt: even if a demon is deceiving him, he must exist in order to be deceived. The act of doubting is an act of thinking. Thus emerged his famous formulation: Cogito, ergo sum (I think, therefore I am). While Descartes used doubt as a temporary scaffold to build an ambitious rationalist philosophy, his radical skepticism exposed how thin the ice of human sensory confidence truly is.

3. David Hume and the Problem of Induction

A century after Descartes, Scottish Enlightenment philosopher David Hume delivered the most devastating sceptical blow to human reason in his 1748 treatise, An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding. Hume turned his critical gaze upon induction—the foundational engine of all empirical science and everyday decision-making.

Inductive reasoning assumes that patterns observed repeatedly in the past will continue to hold in the future: because the sun has risen every morning for billions of years, we assume it will rise tomorrow; because dropped stones fall to the ground, we assume gravity will operate in ten seconds.

Hume asked a disarmingly simple question: What rational proof guarantees that the future must resemble the past?

You cannot prove it through deductive logic, because there is no logical contradiction in imagining that tomorrow gravity might invert or the sun might fail to rise. Nor can you prove it by appealing to past experience, because doing so is a textbook circular argument: you are using past experience to prove that past experience is reliable!

Hume demonstrated that human beings do not navigate the world through pure, clean reason; we navigate through habit, custom, and animal expectation. When we observe Event A followed by Event B, our senses perceive only correlation and temporal succession—we never directly observe the invisible physical thread of "necessary causation."

Yet Hume did not advocate that humans abandon science or curl into a ball of nihilistic paralysis. Instead, he championed Mitigated Scepticism: recognizing that while our scientific theories and common-sense beliefs are provisional habits rather than eternal metaphysical certainties, they remain indispensable, practical tools for living. Scepticism should make us humble, curious, and tolerant, not paralyzed.

4. Historical Case Studies in Sceptical Breakthroughs

Case A: The European Black Swan Shock (Western Australia, 1697)

For more than two thousand years, Western thinkers used the phrase "a black swan" as a standard philosophical proverb denoting a logical impossibility. Aristotle, the Roman satirist Juvenal, and medieval scholastic philosophers cited the statement "All swans are white" as an unassailable empirical truth: millions of white swans had been observed across Europe, Russia, and the Middle East for millennia, with not a single contrary instance ever recorded.

Then, in January 1697, Dutch explorer Willem de Vlamingh sailed into the mouth of a river in Western Australia (the Swan River) and observed flocks of large, elegant birds gliding across the water: they were entirely, unmistakably black (Cygnus atratus).

In a single afternoon, two thousand years of inductive European empirical consensus was pulverized. As Nassim Nicholas Taleb highlighted centuries later, the black swan is history's greatest metaphor for the fragility of unexamined induction: no number of confirmatory observations can ever prove a universal rule, but a single counter-example destroys it in an instant. The true sceptic maintains an open mind, constantly looking for the black swan that could overturn the conventional consensus.

Case B: Michel de Montaigne and the War on Religious Dogmatism

During the late sixteenth century, France was torn apart by the French Wars of Religion—a thirty-year bloodbath between Catholic and Protestant factions that claimed over three million lives. Both sides were absolutely, uncompromisingly certain that they possessed divine truth, justifying the burning of heretics, the massacre of whole towns, and the torture of neighbors in the name of piety.

In his secluded stone tower in the countryside near Bordeaux, nobleman Michel de Montaigne retreated from the madness to write his immortal Essays. Montaigne had ancient sceptical maxims carved into the oak beams of his library ceiling, and struck a personal bronze medallion bearing a pair of balanced scales and his lifelong motto: Que sais-je? (What do I know?).

Montaigne observed that human beings are quick to kill one another over theological technicalities that vary from village to village. He pointed out that if an inquisitor had been born in Constantinople, he would have been a devout Muslim; had he been born in Athens, an ancient pagan. Human convictions are overwhelmingly accidents of birth, geography, and childhood habit. By confronting his contemporaries with the reality of their own cognitive limitations, Montaigne pioneered modern humanism, religious tolerance, and political moderation. Scepticism was the only antidote to ideological slaughter.

5. Actionable Heuristics for Practicing Modern Scepticism

  1. The "Epoche" Pause Before Certainty: Whenever you feel a wave of righteous indignation, certainty, or panic washing over you in response to a breaking news headline or social media provocation, invoke Pyrrhonian epoche. Deliberately suspend judgment for forty-eight hours. Remind yourself: "I do not possess the full context; early reports are almost always distorted; let the data settle."
  2. The Agrippa Assumption Screen: When presented with a confident business strategy, political manifesto, or investment thesis, trace the chain of justification backward. Ask: "What unproven baseline assumption is holding up this entire argument? Does this chain end in circular reasoning or dogmatic assertion?" Exposing the baseline assumption reveals the true fragility of the proposal.
  3. Practice Humean Mitigated Scepticism: Treat all knowledge as provisional, working software rather than sacred scripture. When someone asks your opinion on an uncertain topic, frame your response in terms of evidence and confidence levels: "Based on current data, the working hypothesis is X, but it remains vulnerable to new findings regarding Y."
  4. Montaigne’s Mirror Test: Whenever you find yourself despising someone who holds an opposing political, philosophical, or religious viewpoint, ask yourself: "If I had been born to their parents, raised in their neighborhood, educated in their schools, and endured their life traumas, would I hold the exact same view?" Intellectual empathy is the natural child of epistemic humility.

6. Guided Self-Reflection

Examine your intellectual habits through the lens of scepticism:

  • What is a core belief I hold today that I have never rigorously interrogated or subjected to doubt?
  • Am I acting as an arrogant dogmatist in my professional domain, assuming that past success guarantees future predictability?
  • When was the last time I experienced the liberating relief of saying out loud: 'I simply do not know enough to have an opinion on this'?
  • How much of my daily emotional friction is caused by demanding absolute certainty from a world that is inherently probabilistic and messy?
