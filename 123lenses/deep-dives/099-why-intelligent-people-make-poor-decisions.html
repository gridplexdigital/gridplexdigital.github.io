LESSON 099 / 123 · Decision Science & Cognitive Biases

Why intelligent people make poor decisions

High raw intelligence measures mental processing horsepower, not epistemic direction. When brilliant minds err, they do not merely stumble—they build sophisticated intellectual fortresses around their mistakes.

📖 15-Minute Deep Read (2,951 words) ⚡ Short on time? Switch to the 2-minute field note summary →

In 1917, two young cousins in Yorkshire produced a set of five photographs purporting to show real winged fairies dancing in their garden. The Cottingley Fairies were an obvious trick of cardboard illustrations, paper cutouts, and hatpins. Yet one of the most ardent public defenders of the photos was Sir Arthur Conan Doyle—a physician, celebrated investigator, member of the British Society for Psychical Research, and the creator of Sherlock Holmes, the world's universal archetype of ruthless inductive logic. Doyle did not defend the fairies in spite of his intellect; he defended them using his intellect. He published an entire book, The Coming of the Fairies, deploying pages of elaborate chemical arguments explaining why photographic emulsions could not be faked and ridiculing photographic technicians who pointed out the obvious cut marks on the fairy wings.

Eight decades later, in the summer of 1998, Long-Term Capital Management (LTCM)—a Greenwich hedge fund whose founding partners included Myron Scholes and Robert C. Merton, co-recipients of the Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences for revolutionary option pricing models—lost $4.6 billion in less than four months. Their risk algorithms were triumphs of stochastic calculus, yet they rested on the fatal assumption that financial markets behave like stable Gaussian bell curves. When the Russian government defaulted on its domestic debt, triggering an unprecedented flight to liquidity, the mathematical models collapsed. The Federal Reserve was forced to orchestrate a $3.6 billion consortium bailout to prevent systemic failure across Wall Street.

In 1986, the Space Shuttle Challenger disintegrated 73 seconds into flight, killing all seven crew members. When Nobel physicist Richard Feynman sat on the presidential investigative commission, he discovered that NASA's senior engineering administrators—men with graduate degrees in aerospace mechanics—had calculated the probability of shuttle engine failure at 1 in 100,000 launches. Working engineers on the floor estimated it closer to 1 in 100. The management had systematically ignored 14 previous flight warnings showing heat damage to the rubber O-rings in cold temperatures. As Feynman famously concluded in his personal appendix: "For a successful technology, reality must take precedence over public relations, for nature cannot be fooled."

These episodes are not isolated anomalies. History, business, science, and governance are saturated with brilliant people who authored catastrophic blunders. To understand why this happens, we must dismantle the prevailing cultural myth of modernity: the assumption that high intelligence is synonymous with sound judgment.

1. The Dysrationalia Gap: Intelligence vs. Rationality

In modern educational, professional, and corporate institutions, cognitive ability is treated as the supreme virtue. Standardized assessments—from the SAT, GRE, and GMAT to Mensa tests and civil service examinations—measure algorithmic processing capacity: working memory, processing speed, spatial rotation, and deductive logic.

Yet over three decades of empirical research led by cognitive scientist Keith E. Stanovich at the University of Toronto demonstrated that standard intelligence tests leave the cognitive machinery of rationality almost completely untouched.

"Intelligence is a measure of cognitive capacity, whereas rationality is a measure of how well that capacity is used to achieve one's goals and form beliefs consistent with evidence. ... Dysrationalia is the inability to think and behave rationally despite having adequate intelligence. Just as someone can have a normal IQ and suffer from dyslexia, a person can have an exceptionally high IQ and suffer from severe dysrationalia."

Keith E. Stanovich, What Intelligence Tests Miss: The Psychology of Dysrationalia (Yale University Press, 2009)
Primary Source

Stanovich developed a dual-process taxonomy that divides the mind into three levels:

  • The Autonomous Mind (System 1): Fast, unconscious, automatic heuristics evolved for rapid survival in ancestral environments.
  • The Algorithmic Mind: Fluid intelligence, working memory capacity, and raw computational horsepower. This is what IQ tests measure.
  • The Reflective Mind: Epistemic regulation. The disposition to question assumptions, calibrate subjective confidence against baseline statistics, evaluate disconfirming data, and recognize when the autonomous mind is feeding us an emotional illusion.

The crucial insight is that an individual can possess an extraordinary algorithmic mind while possessing an impoverished reflective mind. A supercomputer executing corrupted software will simply execute catastrophic errors at blinding speed.

"Mindware refers to the rules, knowledge, procedures, and strategies that can be retrieved by the reflective mind to aid in decision-making and belief formation. ... There are two distinct mindware problems: mindware gaps, where a person has never been taught the requisite probabilistic or scientific tools; and contaminated mindware, where the mind has acquired beliefs and dogmas that actively inoculate themselves against contrary evidence."

Keith E. Stanovich, The Rationality Quotient: Toward a Test of Rational Thinking (MIT Press, 2016)
Primary Source

2. The Fortress of Justification: The "Myside" Bias

One might assume that higher cognitive capacity would enable an individual to spot flaws in their own thinking. In experimental settings, the opposite frequently occurs.

In landmark research conducted at Harvard University's Project Zero, cognitive scientist David Perkins tested people of varying IQ levels on complex social, economic, and moral questions. He asked subjects to brainstorm arguments on both sides of contentious issues.

"People with high IQs do not explore issues more broadly or consider more counterarguments than people with average IQs. What they do is build an exceptionally well-defended fortress around their initial impression. Intelligence acts as an amplifier of justification rather than an engine of exploration. Once a smart person adopts an initial point of view, their cognitive speed is deployed almost entirely in mining for justifications that support that view."

David Perkins, Outsmarting IQ: The Emerging Science of Learnable Intelligence (Free Press, 1995)
Primary Source

Perkins identified what he termed makes-my-point reasoning. When average individuals adopt a flawed thesis, they struggle to defend it when questioned; their arguments quickly run into circular logic or obvious factual contradictions, forcing them to confront their confusion.

A highly intelligent person, however, possesses the verbal agility, selective memory retrieval, and rhetorical sophistication to construct plausible, convoluted narratives to explain away any anomaly. They can rationalize bad investments, toxic relationships, cultish ideologies, and failing organizational strategies with dazzling eloquence. They are far harder to rescue from an error precisely because their intellectual defenses are so formidable.

3. The Evolutionary Purpose of Reason: Persuasion, Not Truth

Why would our cognitive architecture evolve in this counter-productive manner? If survival depends on understanding reality, why would intelligence amplify rationalization rather than objective truth-seeking?

Cognitive scientists Hugo Mercier and Dan Sperber provided a breakthrough answer through their Argumentative Theory of Reason. Human reason did not evolve to enable solitary truth-seekers to deduce abstract cosmic principles in isolation. It evolved in small, hyper-social hominid groups where status, resource allocation, mating opportunities, and physical safety depended on the ability to justify one's actions to peers and persuade others during conflict.

"Reasoning was not designed to pursue the truth in isolation. It evolved for social interaction—to justify our actions to others and to convince them in argumentation. The smarter a person is, the better they are at constructing arguments that confirm their preexisting allegiance, and the more capable they are of dismantling opposing arguments, regardless of where the truth lies. The myside bias is not a bug or a cognitive flaw; it is a feature designed to make us formidable debaters within our tribe."

Hugo Mercier & Dan Sperber, The Enigma of Reason (Harvard University Press, 2017)
Primary Source

Seen through this evolutionary lens, the behavior of brilliant minds becomes intelligible. When a CEO, academic, or politician is confronted with disconfirming evidence, their autonomous brain does not interpret the data as an interesting intellectual puzzle; it interprets it as an existential threat to status, reputation, and tribal belonging. The algorithmic mind immediately mobilizes as a defense attorney, using its vast computational power to discredit the critic, question the data, and invent sophisticated counter-theories.

4. The Seduction of Narrative Fluency: The WYSIATI Trap

A third major vulnerability of the intelligent mind is the illusion of narrative coherence. In Thinking, Fast and Slow, Daniel Kahneman identified the foundational heuristic known as WYSIATI: What You See Is All There Is.

"Confidence is a feeling, which reflects the coherence of the information and the cognitive ease of processing it. It is wise to take admissions of uncertainty seriously, but declarations of high confidence mainly tell you that an individual has constructed a coherent story in his mind, not necessarily that the story is true. ... The mind is an instrument for manufacturing meaning, and it will seize upon the flimsiest fragments to construct a compelling explanatory narrative."

Daniel Kahneman, Thinking, Fast and Slow (Farrar, Straus and Giroux, 2011)
Primary Source

High intelligence confers extraordinary verbal and narrative fluency. When a brilliant analyst encounters an ambiguous situation, their mind effortlessly synthesizes disparate threads into a gripping, highly plausible storyline. Because the explanation feels so elegant and computationally frictionless (cognitive ease), the thinker experiences a profound internal sensation of certainty. They confuse the internal coherence of their mental model with its external correspondence to reality.

Historical Case Study

Ignaz Semmelweis and the Arrogance of Vienna’s Elite

In 1847, Hungarian physician Ignaz Semmelweis discovered that puerperal fever—which was killing up to 30% of women giving birth at the Vienna General Hospital—could be virtually eradicated if doctors washed their hands in chlorinated lime water before examinations. The maternal mortality rate plummeted from 18% to under 2%.

Yet the elite medical professoriate of Vienna—the most educated physicians in the Western world—angrily rejected Semmelweis's findings. Because medical doctrine of the era held that disease was caused by an imbalance of the four humors and atmospheric miasmas, the idea that a doctor’s own hands carried invisible deadly particles was dismissed as preposterous. The professors felt insulted: "Gentlemen do not have dirty hands." They deployed erudite treatises to mock Semmelweis, had him dismissed from the hospital, and continued refusing to wash their hands for decades, causing tens of thousands of avoidable maternal deaths.

Prestige, medical education, and intellectual hierarchy did not aid in discovering truth; they served as an emotional barrier preventing the recognition of an unsettling reality.

5. Foxes vs. Hedgehogs: The Anatomy of Forecasting Accuracy

If high IQ, specialized training, and prestigious credentials do not guarantee sound judgment, what cognitive characteristics actually correlate with accurate real-world prediction?

In a landmark twenty-year study funded by the National Science Foundation, psychologist Philip E. Tetlock tracked 284 elite political and economic experts who collectively made over 28,000 predictions about future geopolitical and financial events.

Tetlock's results shocked the establishment: the average expert was scarcely more accurate than chance. Worse, the most famous experts—those frequently quoted in national newspapers, television news, and corporate keynotes—were systematically less accurate than their less prominent peers.

"Beliefs are hypotheses to be tested, not treasures to be guarded. The best forecasters are not the ones with the highest raw IQ or the most prestigious degrees, but those who possess intellectual humility—the willingness to constantly adjust probabilities in response to disconfirming evidence. They think like foxes, drawing on many diverse perspectives, rather than hedgehogs, who view the entire world through one grand, overarching theory."

Philip E. Tetlock & Dan Gardner, Superforecasting: The Art and Science of Prediction (Crown, 2015)
Primary Source

Tetlock borrowed the distinction from the ancient Greek poet Archilochus and the philosopher Isaiah Berlin:

  • Hedgehogs: Thinkers who view the entire world through the prism of a single big idea (e.g., free markets solve everything; Marxist class struggle determines all; technology inevitably decentralizes power). Hedgehogs are charismatic, highly confident, and write best-selling books. But when reality contradicts their theory, they dismiss the failure as a minor timing error or blame external interference.
  • Foxes: Thinkers who are skeptical of grand unified theories. They gather evidence from multiple disciplines, embrace uncertainty, think in granular probabilities (e.g., distinguishing between a 40% and a 60% likelihood), and instantly update their views when empirical facts shift.

6. The Organizational Pathology: Intellectual Intimidation

When an organization is led by an individual of extraordinary intellect, a distinctive structural fragility takes root.

Because the leader possesses lightning-fast analytical speed and formidable rhetorical command, subordinates quickly learn that disagreeing with them in meetings is a bruising experience. Even when mid-level engineers, analysts, or operations managers detect critical operational anomalies, they self-censor. They assume: "The CEO is a certified genius; she must have already factored this into her strategy."

In her groundbreaking sociological analysis of the Challenger explosion, Columbia University professor Diane Vaughan identified this phenomenon as the normalization of deviance:

"The normalization of deviance occurs when an organization slowly becomes accustomed to a technical anomaly, incrementally redefining risk until what was once considered a dangerous deviation becomes the operational norm. This process is accelerated when dissenting voices are silenced not by physical force, but by bureaucratic protocols that demand an impossible burden of proof before a warning is taken seriously."

Diane Vaughan, The Challenger Launch Decision: Risky Technology, Culture, and Deviance at NASA (University of Chicago Press, 1996)
Primary Source

7. The Reality Check: Where Intelligence Protects vs. Where It Blinds

To preserve intellectual balance, we must avoid the opposite error: the anti-intellectual posture that intelligence is useless. Cognitive ability is an immense, irreplaceable asset under specific environmental conditions:

Domain Characteristic Role of Raw Intelligence Typical Environments
Kind Environments
(Fixed rules, immediate feedback)		 Highly Protective Chess, formal mathematics, computer programming, aircraft piloting, bridge design.
Wicked Environments
(Dynamic rules, delayed/noisy feedback)		 Vulnerable to Overconfidence Geopolitics, macroeconomics, equity investing, startup leadership, public policy, marriage.

In "kind" learning environments (a term coined by psychologist Robin Hogarth), patterns repeat reliably and feedback is immediate and unambiguous. In these domains, high algorithmic intelligence consistently produces superior mastery.

In "wicked" environments, however, feedback is delayed by months or years, human psychology interferes with causal chains, and randomness obscures underlying quality. Here, raw intelligence decoupled from epistemic humility produces catastrophic overconfidence.

8. Practical Application: Epistemic Hygiene Protocols

Because motivated reasoning operates below conscious awareness, resolving to "try harder to be open-minded" is completely ineffective. You cannot out-think your biases through willpower. You must install explicit external protocols:

1. The Gary Klein Pre-Mortem Protocol

Before committing capital, hiring, or launching a major strategic initiative, gather the entire decision-making team in a room. State the following prompt:

"Imagine that we are looking back from twelve months in the future. The project has suffered an absolute, total, humiliating disaster. Take ten minutes in complete silence and write the comprehensive post-mortem history of how and why we failed."

This single psychological shift instantly neutralizes the social pressure of optimism. It gives smart people permission to direct their analytical firepower toward uncovering systemic vulnerabilities rather than defending executive consensus.

2. The Decision Journal: Decoupling Process from Outcome

A foolish decision (e.g., driving home drunk) can yield a positive outcome through pure dumb luck; an impeccably reasoned decision can yield a loss due to an unforeseen macroeconomic shock.

Maintain a dedicated Decision Journal. Before signing a deal, making an investment, or executing a major organizational shift, write down:

  • The exact rationale and primary evidence supporting the thesis.
  • The explicit base rates: how often do ventures of this type succeed in the broader market?
  • The anticipated timeline of verifiable milestones.
  • The Falsification Threshold: What specific, measurable data would prove in six months that this hypothesis was wrong?

Reviewing past entries forces you to confront hindsight bias and prevents your intelligence from rewriting history after the fact.

3. The Institutional Red Team Mandate

In corporate and public policy settings, institutionalize skepticism. Appoint a respected peer or external audit group whose formal operational mandate is to present the most devastating counter-argument against the proposed strategy. Destigmatize dissent by making skepticism a compensated duty rather than an act of insubordination.

A QUESTION TO CARRY

What belief do you currently defend with your intellect that you might readily abandon if your status, peer group, or professional identity did not depend on it?
