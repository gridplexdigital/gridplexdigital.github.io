How Propaganda Simplifies the World

Propaganda is not merely the spread of deliberate falsehoods; it is the radical, violent simplification of reality. By replacing the messy, ambiguous complexity of human life with cartoonish binaries of pure virtue and existential evil, propaganda satisfies our psychological hunger for certainty while paving the road to collective cruelty.

Between April and July of 1994, across the rolling hills of the East African nation of Rwanda, one of the most concentrated, horrifying mass slaughters in modern history occurred. In approximately one hundred days, armed Hutu militias, soldiers, and ordinary citizens armed with machetes, clubs, and rifles murdered an estimated 800,000 ethnic Tutsi civilians and politically moderate Hutus—roughly 75 percent of the Tutsi population of the country. Neighbors butchered neighbors with whom they had shared tea the morning before; doctors murdered their patients in hospital beds; and schoolteachers slaughtered their own students. While the root causes of the conflict involved complex socio-economic tensions, colonial identity cards introduced by Belgian authorities, and political maneuvering, the lethal ignition switch that unleashed the slaughter was a radio transmitter: the Radio Télévision Libre des Mille Collines (RTLM). In a largely agrarian society with high illiteracy, RTLM broadcasted popular music, conversational banter, and upbeat youth culture, interwoven with a relentless, terrifying ideological message. The broadcasters did not present complex economic analyses or historical nuance. Instead, they reduced the entire existence of the nation to a single, primal, black-and-white binary: the Tutsis were not fellow human beings; they were "inyenzi"—cockroaches—an existential, subhuman parasite infiltrating the nation to destroy the Hutu people. Announcers read names, license plates, and home addresses over the airwaves, urging listeners to "go to work and clear the tall trees." By violently stripping away human complexity, moral nuance, and individual identity, propaganda transformed ordinary farmers and shopkeepers into genocidal killers. Propaganda’s ultimate weapon is not the lie; it is the lethal simplification of reality.

1. Jacques Ellul: Propaganda as a Total Sociological Environment

In 1965, French sociologist and philosopher Jacques Ellul published his foundational treatise, Propaganda: The Formation of Men's Attitudes (Vintage Books). Ellul delivered a shocking thesis that demolished the comforting myth of the educated, critical citizen:

"Propaganda satisfies a deep psychological need in modern man... The educated person is actually more vulnerable to propaganda than the illiterate, because they absorb vastly more second-hand information, read complex opinions, and have a desperate need to feel informed and take a stand on global issues about which they have zero direct knowledge."

— Jacques Ellul, Propaganda: The Formation of Men's Attitudes (1965)

Ellul observed that modern industrial society places an unbearable cognitive burden on the individual: we are bombarded by macroeconomic statistics, geopolitical crises, technological disruptions, and moral controversies across continents.

The sheer complexity of modern reality produces intense existential anxiety. Humans hate ambiguity; we crave certainty, clarity, and moral reassurance.

Propaganda is the psychological medicine that relieves this anxiety. It steps forward and offers a clean, totalizing worldview:

  • It answers every complex question: "Why are wages low? Why is housing expensive? Who is responsible for crime?"
  • It provides a single, visible culprit to blame (an ethnic minority, an opposing political party, an external foreign power, or a shadowy cabal).
  • It offers the warm, tribal comfort of belonging: you are part of the pure, righteous "in-group" fighting an existential crusade against a corrupt, sinister "out-group."

The propagandized individual does not feel brainwashed; they feel enlightened, purposeful, and morally validated.

2. Edward Bernays: The Engineering of Consent

While Ellul analyzed political propaganda, the nephew of Sigmund Freud, an American named Edward L. Bernays, pioneered commercial and democratic propaganda in the United States. Bernays literally invented the term "Public Relations" to replace the word "propaganda," which had acquired toxic connotations following World War I.

In his 1928 manifesto, Propaganda, Bernays articulated the philosophy of modern psychological manipulation:

"The conscious and intelligent manipulation of the organized habits and opinions of the masses is an important element in democratic society. Those who manipulate this unseen mechanism of society constitute an invisible government which is the true ruling power of our country."

— Edward L. Bernays, Propaganda (1928)

Bernays realized that people are not swayed by rational, logical arguments; they are governed by unconscious emotional desires, status anxieties, and irrational drives.

In 1929, the American Tobacco Company hired Bernays to solve a business problem: in the 1920s, social taboos strictly forbade women from smoking cigarettes in public. A traditional, linear advertising campaign would have published brochures explaining that cigarettes tasted good or relieved stress.

Bernays executed a masterclass in psychological propaganda:

  1. He consulted with psychoanalyst A. A. Brill, who informed him that to women, cigarettes represented male power, phallic symbols of independence, and liberation from patriarchal control.
  2. During the massive 1929 Easter Sunday Parade in New York City, Bernays hired a group of glamorous debutantes and feminist models to march down Fifth Avenue.
  3. At a pre-arranged signal, the women dramatically pulled Lucky Strike cigarettes from beneath their dresses, lit them in front of press photographers, and announced that they were lighting "Torches of Freedom"!

The spectacle made front-page news worldwide. Bernays had successfully tied a lethal, addictive chemical product directly to the sacred cultural concept of female emancipation and equality. Smoking was no longer a health decision; it had been simplified into an identity statement: If you are a modern, independent woman, you light a Torch of Freedom. Sales of cigarettes to women skyrocketed overnight.

3. George Orwell: The Corruption of Language as Cognitive Warfare

In his 1946 essay "Politics and the English Language" and his dystopian masterpiece 1984, George Orwell diagnosed the ultimate tactical tool of propaganda: The Corruption of Language.

Orwell recognized that human thoughts are constructed using words. If you control, shrink, and sterilize the vocabulary of a population, you physically make dissenting, nuanced thoughts impossible to formulate.

In 1984, the Ministry of Truth systematically enforces Newspeak:

  • The purpose of Newspeak is not to expand vocabulary, but to deliberately destroy words: eliminate shades of grey, eliminate nuance, and eliminate contradictions.
  • The word "free" is retained, but stripped of political meaning: you can say "This dog is free from lice," but you cannot formulate the concept of political freedom, because the intellectual tools have been deleted.
  • Doublethink: The psychological capacity to hold two contradictory beliefs in one’s mind simultaneously, and accept both of them (e.g., "War Is Peace," "Freedom Is Slavery," "Ignorance Is Strength").

In the real political world, Orwell noted that governments use bloodless, sterile euphemisms to mask horrific physical realities:

  • Bombing defenseless civilian villages from the sky and burning peasant huts with napalm is termed "pacification."
  • Stealing property from farmers and expelling millions into frozen wastelands is termed "transfer of population" or "rectification of frontiers."
  • Imprisoning political dissidents without trial for decades is termed "preventative detention."

When language is corrupted, the brain becomes numb to the physical reality of suffering.

4. Empirical and Historical Case Studies

Case A: The "All-or-Nothing" Tribalism of Modern Algorithmic Bubbles

In the twenty-first century, propaganda has evolved from state-run radio broadcasts into personalized, machine-learning algorithmic recommendation engines operating on platforms like TikTok, YouTube, and X (formerly Twitter).

The algorithmic architecture functions as an automated, hyper-efficient propaganda simplification machine:

  • A complex, multi-sided geopolitical or civil issue (e.g., immigration policy, energy transition, or pandemic epidemiology) carries dozens of competing trade-offs, historical context, and economic costs.
  • An algorithmic platform cannot monetize nuance: a five-thousand-word, balanced white paper analyzing trade-offs produces low user engagement.
  • What goes viral is the fifteen-second, hyper-simplified binary: a video clip featuring a smug influencer mocking an opponent, accompanied by dramatic music, framing the debate as pure, saintly heroes vs. malicious, treasonous villains.
  • Users are sorted into algorithmic feedback loops where every dissenting view is caricatured into its most absurd, offensive version.

The consequence is what social psychologists term Affective Polarization: citizens no longer merely disagree on policy; they view their political opponents as existential moral monsters who must be politically obliterated.

Case B: The First World War "Atrocity Propaganda" and the Lusitania

During World War I, the British government established a secret war propaganda bureau at Wellington House in London. To mobilize reluctant American public opinion to enter the bloody European trenches, British propagandists launched a massive campaign of fabricated atrocity stories:

  • They published sensationalized, completely false accounts of German soldiers bayoneting Belgian babies, cutting off the hands of children, and operating a "Corpse Factory" (Kadaververwertungsanstalt) where dead soldiers were boiled down to make industrial lubricating soap and nitroglycerin.
  • The stories were certified as true by prestigious British royal commissions and circulated in major American newspapers.

The campaign succeeded in generating wartime fury. But its long-term, second-order consequence was horrific: during the 1930s, when early, verified reports began emerging from Nazi Germany about Adolf Hitler's concentration camps and the persecution of Jewish citizens, the American and European public largely dismissed the reports as "just another round of British atrocity propaganda." The weaponization of simplification destroyed public trust in genuine moral alarm.

5. Actionable Heuristics for Resisting Propaganda

  1. The "Nuance Litmus Test" (The Simplicity Screen): Whenever you encounter a political speech, manifesto, documentary, or viral video, apply this single cognitive filter: Does this message acknowledge legitimate trade-offs, tragic complexity, and honest counter-arguments? Or does it present the world as a cartoonish struggle between pure, flawless heroes and cartoonish, evil villains? If the message offers simple, black-and-white certainty, it is propaganda.
  2. The Orwellian Euphemism Translator: Whenever a government, corporation, or activist group uses abstract, technical, or sanitary jargon to describe an action, translate the phrase into concrete physical human realities:
    • "Collateral damage" → "Innocent children and families killed by an explosive blast."
    • "Downsizing and organizational restructuring" → "Depriving five hundred families of their income to boost quarterly executive bonuses."
    • "Revenue enhancement" → "Taking more money from your paycheck through taxes."
    Insist on linguistic clarity.
  3. Beware the In-Group Flattery Trap: The most seductive propaganda does not attack the enemy; it flatters you. It tells you that your tribe is morally enlightened, intellectually superior, and uniquely noble, while your opponents are ignorant, uneducated, and malicious. Whenever an ideology strokes your moral vanity, realize you are being manipulated.
  4. Practice Steel-Manning Opposing Views: As philosopher John Stuart Mill famously argued: "He who knows only his own side of the case knows little of that." To inoculate yourself against tribal propaganda, force yourself to write down the strongest, most intelligent, and most compassionate version of your opponent’s argument (the "steel-man"). If you can articulate their position so persuasively that your opponent says "Yes, that is exactly what I believe," you have broken the spell of simplification.

6. Guided Self-Reflection

Audit your mind for the psychological fingerprints of propaganda:

  • What political or social issue do I currently view in pure black-and-white terms, completely unwilling to see legitimate nuance?
  • Where am I using euphemistic corporate or personal language to justify actions that are morally compromised?
  • Do I derive a sense of warm tribal superiority and moral smugness from the political memes and news I consume?
  • What would it take for me to acknowledge that an opponent I despise made a correct, fair, and honorable decision?
