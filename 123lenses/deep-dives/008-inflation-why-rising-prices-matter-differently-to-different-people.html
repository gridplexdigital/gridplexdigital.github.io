Inflation: Why Rising Prices Matter Differently to Different People

Inflation is neither a natural disaster nor an equal-opportunity tax. It is an invisible, radical engine of social and economic redistribution—secretly siphoning purchasing power from cash savers, wage-earners, and retirees, and funneling it to asset owners, primary borrowers, and debtor governments.

In the autumn of 1923, an elderly woman in Berlin, Germany, packed a laundry basket full of paper banknotes and walked to the local bakery to buy a loaf of rye bread. By the time she reached the front of the queue, the price of bread had doubled, and her basket of cash was no longer sufficient. She stepped outside to ask a passerby for directions to the nearest bank. Leaving her heavy laundry basket on the sidewalk for just two minutes, she returned to find a scene that perfectly captured the madness of the Weimar hyperinflation: a thief had dumped the entire mountain of hundreds of billions of marks onto the pavement and stolen the empty wicker basket. The woven wicker had tangible physical utility; the paper currency was literally worthless. During those feverish months, the German Reichsbank operated thirty paper mills and two thousand printing presses night and day, printing quadrillions of marks. Workers were paid twice a day in suitcases; they ran to the markets during their lunch breaks because a cup of coffee that cost 5,000 marks at noon cost 8,000 marks by 1:00 PM. Yet while millions of thrifty middle-class German families, civil servants, and pensioners who had saved diligently in government bonds and bank deposits were plunged into abject destitution, a small circle of leveraged industrial tycoons amassed vast empires. Industrialist Hugo Stinnes borrowed trillions of depreciating paper marks from the central bank to purchase real coal mines, iron foundries, cargo ships, and timber forests across Central Europe. When the loans matured months later, Stinnes repaid his bank debt with a handful of paper marks worth less than the cost of a postage stamp. Stinnes became the wealthiest man in Europe. Inflation was not a neutral rising tide that lifted all prices equally; it was a violent redistributive engine that dismantled the social fabric of an entire civilization.

1. John Maynard Keynes: The Secret Confiscation of Wealth

In 1919, following the conclusion of World War I, British economist John Maynard Keynes published his searing prophetic indictment of the Versailles Treaty, The Economic Consequences of the Peace. In Chapter 6, Keynes analyzed the catastrophic monetary dissolution occurring across Central and Eastern Europe:

"Lenin is said to have declared that the best way to destroy the Capitalist System was to debauch the currency. By a continuing process of inflation, governments can confiscate, secretly and unobserved, an important part of the wealth of their citizens... The process engages all the hidden forces of economic law on the side of destruction, and does it in a manner which not one man in a million is able to diagnose."

— John Maynard Keynes, The Economic Consequences of the Peace (1919)

Keynes recognized that inflation is the ultimate stealth tax. When a government levies an explicit income tax, property tax, or sales tax, it must pass legislation through parliament, sparking intense public debate and voter scrutiny. But when a state expands the money supply to finance its deficits, it reduces the purchasing power of every currency unit already in circulation.

Because the confiscation occurs through the price tag at the grocery store, pharmacy, and gas pump rather than on an official tax bill, citizens naturally direct their anger at local merchants, landlords, and corporations ("corporate greed"). The true architect of the devaluation—the fiscal and monetary apparatus—escapes political accountability.

2. The Cantillon Effect: Why Money Does Not Fall Like Rain

Mainstream monetary models often simplify inflation through the classic equation of exchange ($M imes V = P imes Y$), imagining that if the money supply ($M$) doubles, all prices and all wages throughout the economy double simultaneously and frictionlessly. In this textbook fantasy, nobody is harmed and nobody benefits: if your salary doubles and your living expenses double, your real purchasing power remains unchanged.

In 1755, French-Irish banker and economist Richard Cantillon published his revolutionary treatise, Essai sur la Nature du Commerce en Général (Essay on the Nature of Commerce in General). Cantillon dismantled this naive assumption, introducing what is known today as the Cantillon Effect.

Cantillon observed that new money does not drop evenly from the sky like rain across an entire country. New money is always injected into an economy at a specific physical location, through specific institutional pipelines, into the hands of specific actors:

  • The First Receivers (The Cantillon Beneficiaries): In the eighteenth century, the first receivers were the owners of newly discovered gold and silver mines and the royal court. In the modern financial architecture, the first receivers are central banks, primary-dealer Wall Street commercial banks, private equity giants, and government contractors. These entities receive the freshly created currency first—before it has circulated into the broader economy, and therefore before consumer prices have inflated. They deploy this new purchasing power to buy real estate, commodities, and productive corporate equities at un-inflated prices.
  • The Last Receivers (The Inflation Victims): As the newly created money circulates through the financial system into the real economy, it bids up the prices of food, fuel, housing, healthcare, and education. By the time the money finally trickles down to wage-earners, gig workers, schoolteachers, and fixed-income retirees eighteen to twenty-four months later, consumer prices have already skyrocketed. Their subsequent cost-of-living pay raises, if granted at all, lag far behind inflation.

The Cantillon Effect proves that monetary expansion is fundamentally regressive: it redistributes real wealth upward from the working class and fixed-income savers to asset-holding financial institutions and debt-leveraged corporations.

3. The Great Redistribution: Debtors vs. Creditors

At its mechanical core, inflation acts as a massive, unlegislated wealth transfer from creditors (savers, lenders, bondholders) to debtors (borrowers, governments, real estate speculators).

Consider a debt contract: If you borrow $500,000 on a 30-year fixed-rate mortgage at 3 percent interest, your monthly payment of roughly $2,100 is fixed in nominal dollars for three decades. If unexpected inflation surges at 7 percent annually:

  • The physical house you purchased inflates in nominal market value, rising from $500,000 to $1,000,000 over ten years.
  • Your nominal wages eventually rise to adjust for inflation.
  • Yet your monthly mortgage payment remains permanently locked at $2,100! In real, purchasing-power terms, the burden of your mortgage debt evaporates every single month. You are repaying the bank with dollars that are worth far less than the dollars you borrowed.

The bank, by contrast, is the creditor. The bank receives $2,100 a month in nominal cash, but that cash buys less bread, fuel, and medical care each year. The largest debtor in any modern nation is the national government. Inflation allows indebted governments to execute "financial repression"—quietly melting away the real burden of their multi-trillion-dollar sovereign debts on the backs of bondholders and retirement funds.

4. Empirical and Historical Case Studies

Case A: The Spanish Price Revolution and the Curse of Potosí (16th Century)

In 1545, Spanish explorers in Alto Perú (modern-day Bolivia) discovered Cerro Rico—a literal mountain of silver at Potosí that became the richest silver strike in human history. Over the next two centuries, Spanish galleons transported over 16,000 tons of pure silver across the Atlantic to the royal treasury in Seville.

The Spanish Crown believed this endless influx of silver bullion would make Spain the permanent master of the globe. Instead, it triggered what historians call the Price Revolution.

The flood of new silver quadrupled the European money supply. Because the quantity of goods and services produced in Spain had not increased proportionally, the massive influx of money resulted in a 500 percent inflation across Spain and Europe. Domestic Spanish prices rose far faster than prices in England, France, or Holland.

The result was economic catastrophe for Spain. High domestic prices destroyed Spanish manufacturing: it became cheaper for Spaniards to import textiles, grain, and tools from England and the Low Countries rather than produce them at home. The silver simply flowed straight through Spain into the pockets of foreign manufacturers. Despite importing mountains of treasure, King Philip II declared sovereign bankruptcy four times (in 1557, 1560, 1575, and 1596). When the silver mines of Potosí eventually depleted, Spain was left de-industrialized, bankrupt, and economically hollowed out.

Case B: The 1970s Great Inflation and Paul Volcker’s Shock Therapy

Throughout the late 1960s and 1970s, the United States suffered from the "Great Inflation." Driven by deficit spending on the Vietnam War and Lyndon Johnson’s Great Society programs, compounded by the 1973 and 1979 OPEC oil embargoes, consumer inflation surged from 3 percent in 1967 to an agonizing peak of 14.8 percent in March 1980.

Psychological expectations became unanchored: labor unions demanded annual Cost-of-Living Adjustments (COLAs); businesses raised prices preemptively every quarter; and citizens rushed to spend cash before it decayed. Mainstream Keynesian economists claimed that inflation could not be halted without permanently destroying the economy.

In August 1979, President Jimmy Carter appointed Paul Volcker as Chairman of the Federal Reserve. Volcker recognized that inflation is fundamentally a monetary and psychological cancer that destroys long-term investment planning.

Volcker executed one of the most courageous acts of central banking in history: he restricted money supply growth and allowed the federal funds interest rate to skyrocket to an unprecedented, eye-watering 20 percent in June 1981. The "Volcker Shock" plunged the United States into the deepest economic recession since the Great Depression: unemployment surged past 10 percent, homebuilders mailed two-by-fours to the Fed in protest, and angry farmers blockaded the Federal Reserve building with tractors. But Volcker held his nerve. Within three years, the inflation rate collapsed from 14.8 percent down to under 3 percent, restoring the credibility of the US dollar and laying the stable monetary foundation for the massive economic expansion of the 1980s and 1990s.

5. Actionable Heuristics for Protecting Capital Against Inflation

  1. Never Confuse Nominal Returns with Real Returns: Apply the classical Fisher Equation: $r = i - \pi$ (Real Return = Nominal Interest Rate − Inflation Rate). If your high-yield savings account or government bond pays 4.5 percent interest, but consumer inflation is running at 5.5 percent, your real return is negative 1.0 percent. You are paying a fee for the privilege of storing purchasing power. Always calculate after-tax, after-inflation yields.
  2. Own Assets with Pricing Power (The Warren Buffett Rule): In high-inflation environments, the most resilient assets are businesses with formidable economic moats that can raise consumer prices effortlessly without losing market share (e.g., Apple, regulated utilities, or mission-critical enterprise software). Companies that require massive annual capital expenditures to replace machinery are devastated by inflating replacement costs.
  3. Position Yourself Near the Cantillon Inflow: Recognize where newly created credit enters the economy. Avoid long-term fixed-rate cash contracts; position your enterprise, investments, and career in sectors that capture new liquidity inflows (productive technology, infrastructure, scarce land, and equity ownership).
  4. Leverage Moderate, Fixed-Rate Debt Responsibly: Fixed-rate long-term debt tied to a cash-flow-positive, appreciating physical asset (such as prime residential real estate) is an extraordinary hedge against inflation. Inflation quietly shrinks the principal balance of your debt in real terms, while rental income and asset replacement values rise.

6. Guided Self-Reflection

Audit your financial exposure to the stealth tax of inflation:

  • What percentage of my personal net worth is sitting in nominal cash, certificates of deposit, or fixed-rate bonds that are quietly losing purchasing power?
  • Does my current employer or business model possess true pricing power, or are our profit margins being squeezed between rising costs and customer pushback?
  • Am I psychologically anchored to the nominal dollar amount on my paycheck rather than what that money can actually buy in the grocery store?
  • What tangible, non-reproducible assets do I own that another human being will desperately want twenty years from today?
