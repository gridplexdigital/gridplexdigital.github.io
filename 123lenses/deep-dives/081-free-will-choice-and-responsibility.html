Free Will, Choice and Responsibility

The intuition that we are uncaused first causes—capriciously choosing our thoughts, desires, and actions out of a vacuum—collides directly with modern physics and neuroscience. Navigating the great dilemma between determinism, compatibilism, and moral accountability is the deepest test of ethical maturity.

In 1983, inside a neurophysiology laboratory at the University of California, San Francisco, researcher Benjamin Libet set up an experiment that would ignite a fierce philosophical firestorm that burns to this day. Libet placed electrical electroencephalogram (EEG) electrodes onto the scalps of human participants and attached electromyogram (EMG) sensors to their wrists. In front of each subject was a specialized oscilloscope clock with a bright dot revolving rapidly around its perimeter. Libet gave the participants a simple instruction: sit peacefully, and whenever you experience the spontaneous urge or desire to flex your wrist or flick a finger, do so freely. Crucially, the subject was instructed to note the exact position of the revolving dot on the clock the precise millisecond they first became consciously aware of their intention to act (a moment Libet designated as Time W). For decades, common sense assumed that the chain of human action follows an obvious linear sequence: first, your conscious mind decides to move; second, your brain sends a neural signal down your spinal cord; third, your wrist muscles contract. Libet’s empirical data shattered this intuition: the EEG sensors revealed a surge of unconscious electrical brain activity—known as the Readiness Potential (Bereitschaftspotential)—in the motor cortex that began 350 to 500 milliseconds before the participant experienced the conscious urge to move! The brain had already initiated the physical movement before the conscious "I" was even aware of the choice. Libet’s findings forced humanity to confront an unsettling question that philosophers from Spinoza to Schopenhauer had long posed: Are we truly the conscious authors of our choices, or are our conscious decisions merely post-hoc narrative commentaries on physical neurological events that have already been determined by prior physical causes?

1. The Great Philosophical Trilemma of Free Will

The debate over free will is structured around three mutually competing philosophical positions:

  1. 1. Hard Determinism (Incompatibilism): Physical reality is governed by universal causal laws: every physical event is the inevitable consequence of prior physical states and the invariant laws of physics. Because the human brain is a physical biological organ composed of atoms, every thought, desire, and decision is entirely determined by genetic inheritance, neurochemistry, and past environmental conditioning. As Dutch philosopher Baruch Spinoza famously noted: if a stone flying through the air were suddenly granted consciousness, it would believe it was flying because it willed to fly. Arthur Schopenhauer summarized the paradox in an immortal aphorism: "Man can do what he wills, but he cannot will what he wills." True libertarian free will is a biological illusion.
  2. 2. Libertarian Free Will: The belief that human beings possess genuine, non-determined agency: given the exact same physical universe, brain state, and historical past, an agent could have chosen otherwise. While championed by common human intuition and certain existentialist or quantum-indeterminacy theorists, libertarian free will struggles against physics: replacing strict determinism with subatomic quantum randomness does not grant you moral free will; it merely replaces a clockwork machine with a game of roulette.
  3. 3. Compatibilism (Soft Determinism): Championed by David Hume, John Stuart Mill, and contemporary philosopher Daniel Dennett. Compatibilism asserts that the entire debate rests on a semantic misunderstanding of the word "free." Free will does not require magic—it does not require breaking the physical laws of thermodynamics or escaping universal causality. An action is free if it is performed without external coercion, restraint, or paralysis, flowing from the agent’s own internal desires, character, and rational deliberation.

2. Harry Frankfurt and Higher-Order Desires

In 1971, philosopher Harry Frankfurt published a classic paper in the Journal of Philosophy titled "Freedom of the Will and the Concept of a Person." Frankfurt provided the most elegant, psychologically sophisticated architecture of compatibilist agency ever conceived.

Frankfurt distinguished between two hierarchical levels of human desire:

  • First-Order Desires: Basic impulses and biological urges to perform an action (e.g., the desire to eat a slice of chocolate cake, smoke a cigarette, check your smartphone, or punch an enemy). Animals and infants live entirely at the level of first-order desires.
  • Second-Order Desires (Second-Order Volitions): The distinctively human reflective capacity to evaluate our own desires: "Do I actually want to have this desire?"

Frankfurt illustrated this through the contrast between two drug addicts:

  • The Unwilling Addict: Has a raging first-order desire to consume heroin. But at the second-order level, he hates the addiction, desires to be free, and struggles with all his might against the craving. When he eventually succumbs, his will is unfree: he was overpowered by a physiological compulsion that conflicted with his second-order volition.
  • The Wanton Addict: Has a first-order desire to take heroin, and does not care at all about evaluating it. He simply drifts from impulse to impulse like an animal.
  • The Willing Addict: Has a first-order desire to consume heroin, and upon deep second-order reflection, enthusiastically endorses it: he wants to want heroin.

Frankfurt demonstrated that freedom of the will is the harmony between first-order actions and second-order volitions. When you act in alignment with the kind of person you reflectively desire to be, you possess meaningful moral freedom, regardless of whether your neurons obey the laws of physics.

3. The Moral Dilemma: Desert vs. Retribution

Why does the free will debate matter outside university seminar rooms? Because the concept of free will is the foundation of our entire legal apparatus, criminal justice system, and moral culture of blame and praise.

If human choices are determined by genetic lottery, brain wiring, and childhood trauma, what happens to moral responsibility?

Philosopher Derk Pereboom, in Living Without Free Will (Cambridge University Press, 2001), demonstrated that abandoning hard retributive vengeance does not mean letting violent criminals run wild through the streets. Instead, Pereboom proposed the Quarantine Model of justice:

  • If an innocent human being contracts a lethal, highly contagious viral disease (such as Ebola), society isolates them in a quarantine ward.
  • Why do we quarantine them? Not because we believe they are "evil" or "deserve to suffer"; they contracted the virus through pure bad luck. We quarantine them exclusively to protect innocent citizens from harm.
  • Furthermore, because the quarantine is purely preventative rather than punitive, we have an absolute moral duty to treat the patient with the highest medical compassion, provide comfortable shelter, and release them the moment they are no longer a danger.

Applying determinism to justice dismantles cruel, vindictive retribution: prison systems shift from punishment factories designed to inflict suffering to rehabilitation, mental health treatment, and public safety containment.

4. Empirical and Historical Case Studies

Case A: The Tragic Case of Charles Whitman (1966)

On August 1, 1966, a twenty-five-year-old architectural engineering student and former Marine sharpshooter named Charles Whitman climbed to the observation deck of the Main Building tower at the University of Texas at Austin. Armed with hunting rifles, Whitman opened fire on pedestrians below, killing fourteen people and wounding thirty-one others before being shot dead by police. Earlier that morning, he had murdered his mother and his wife.

Whitman had been an Eagle Scout, possessed an IQ of 138, and had no prior criminal record. In the weeks leading up to the massacre, Whitman felt his mind disintegrating: he suffered overwhelming, uncharacteristic headaches and experienced terrifying, irrational urges to commit violence. In a suicide note typed the night before the massacre, Whitman wrote:

"I do not really understand myself these days. I am supposed to be an average reasonable and intelligent young man. However, lately (I cannot recall when it started) I have been a victim of many unusual and irrational thoughts... After my death I wish that an autopsy would be performed on me to see if there is any mental disorder."

The coroner complied with his request. During the autopsy, pathologists cut into Whitman's brain and discovered a massive, malignant glioblastoma tumor—the size of a pecan—compressing the amygdala, the neurological hub responsible for emotional regulation, fear, and aggressive fight-or-flight impulses.

Whitman's horrific actions were the direct mechanical consequence of biological brain tissue compression. Can anyone rationally claim that Whitman possessed "free will" in any traditional sense? His case forced legal scholars and neuroscientists to recognize that every human brain is constrained by biological architecture.

Case B: Benjamin Libet’s "Free Won't"

While Libet’s 1983 experiment proved that the unconscious brain initiates the readiness potential before conscious awareness, Libet made a second, equally vital empirical discovery that is often ignored by radical determinists:

Libet observed that between the moment conscious awareness of the urge arrives (Time W, roughly 200 milliseconds before muscle movement) and the actual physical contraction of the muscle, there exists a window of roughly 100 to 150 milliseconds.

During that tiny window, the conscious mind has the power to veto or inhibit the motor impulse!

Libet termed this capacity "Free Won't." The unconscious brain may spontaneously generate impulses, desires, and ideas out of the dark void of neurochemistry. But the conscious executive mind possesses the regulatory power of the veto: it can say "No, I will not act on that impulse." Ethical self-control is largely the art of the conscious veto.

5. Actionable Heuristics for Cultivating Ethical Agency

  1. The Frankfurt Higher-Order Audit: Whenever you feel pulled by an intense desire (to buy luxury goods, check social media notifications, or react in rage), pause and conduct a second-order inspection: "Do I truly want to be the kind of human being who is governed by this desire?" True freedom is aligning your daily actions with your endorsed second-order volitions.
  2. Practice the Libet "Free Won't" Veto: Recognize that you cannot control what thoughts, cravings, or emotional reactions spontaneously bubble up into your consciousness from your subconscious brain. (You did not choose to feel a flash of anger; it simply surged). But you possess absolute sovereignty over the veto: create a deliberate three-second pause between stimulus and physical action to veto destructive impulses.
  3. Replace Self-Righteous Blame with Causal Empathy: When dealing with difficult, rude, or toxic individuals, remember that their behavior is the complex, determined product of their genetic wiring, childhood trauma, socioeconomic stress, and neurological state. Stripping away self-righteous indignation does not mean tolerating abuse; it allows you to set firm, calm boundaries without poisoning your own heart with hatred.
  4. Dismantle Meritocratic Hubris: If you are blessed with discipline, high intelligence, resilience, and success, remind yourself that you did not choose your brain, your parents, or the historical era that values your talents. Shift from arrogant entitlement to humble gratitude and a sense of stewardship for those who drew unfortunate biological hands.

6. Guided Self-Reflection

Contemplate your choices and agency through the lens of philosophy:

  • What first-order impulse in my daily life am I currently acting upon that contradicts my deeper, second-order values?
  • Where in my life am I unfairly judging someone for shortcomings that are the clear result of biological or environmental conditioning?
  • How can I expand the space between an unconscious impulse arising in my mind and my physical response (cultivating my 'Free Won't')?
  • If my sense of free will is a construct of my biological brain, how does that deepen my responsibility to design positive daily environments?

Further Reading and Primary Sources

  • Frankfurt, Harry G. "Freedom of the Will and the Concept of a Person." Journal of Philosophy 68, no. 1 (1971): 5–20. The foundational paper establishing first- and second-order desires in compatibilism.
  • Libet, Benjamin. "Do We Have Free Will?" Journal of Consciousness Studies 6, no. 8–9 (1999): 47–57. Libet's own philosophical reflection on readiness potentials and the veto power.
  • Dennett, Daniel C. Elbow Room: The Varieties of Free Will Worth Wanting (MIT Press, 1984). The brilliant, witty defense of evolutionary compatibilism.
  • Pereboom, Derk. Living Without Free Will (Cambridge University Press, 2001). The authoritative philosophical defense of hard incompatibilism and the quarantine model of justice.
  • Harris, Sam. Free Will (Free Press, 2012). A concise, provocative modern critique of libertarian free will from a neuroscientific perspective.
