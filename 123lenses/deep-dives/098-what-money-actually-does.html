What Money Actually Does

Money is neither gold, paper, nor digital bits on a bank server. It is an extraordinary social technology of intersubjective trust—an abstract, collective ledger of moral and economic memory that allows millions of strangers to coordinate complex production across space and time.

In the western Pacific Ocean lies the tiny Micronesian island of Yap. For centuries, the Yapese people conducted trade, settled marriage contracts, and paid restitution for tribal offenses using a currency that seemed completely baffling to European explorers: Rai stones. These were massive, circular discs carved out of aragonite limestone, with a hole carved through the center so they could be slung onto poles and carried by teams of men. Some Rai stones were as small as dinner plates; others reached twelve feet in diameter and weighed over eight thousand pounds. The limestone was not native to Yap; it had to be quarried hundreds of miles away on the island of Palau and transported across open, stormy ocean waters on frail wooden rafts, often at the cost of human lives. Because moving an eight-thousand-pound boulder across the island was physically perilous, the Yapese did not physically exchange the stone when a transaction occurred. The stone simply remained stationary in the previous owner's garden, in front of a village hall, or along a public pathway. The villagers simply conducted a public conversation acknowledging that the title of ownership had transferred from Person A to Person B. In the late nineteenth century, American anthropologist William Henry Furness III documented the most astonishing Rai stone on the island: decades earlier, a family’s ancestors had been transporting a magnificent, high-value stone on a raft when a violent typhoon struck. The raft capsized, and the stone plunged into several hundred feet of ocean water, vanishing beneath the waves. The surviving sailors returned home and described the incident. The islanders unanimously agreed that the accident did not diminish the stone's value: everyone agreed the stone was still physically there, and everyone knew who owned it. For generations, that invisible, submerged stone lying at the bottom of the Pacific Ocean continued to be bought, sold, and traded as one of the most valuable liquid assets on Yap. Furness recognized what modern economists so often forget: money is never a physical commodity; money is an abstract social ledger of mutual trust.

1. The Myth of Barter: David Graeber and the Ledger of Debt

In virtually every introductory economics textbook published over the last two centuries, students are taught an identical origin story: Once upon a time, primitive human beings lived in a world of pure barter. A baker wanted a pair of boots, but the shoemaker only wanted wool. Trapped by this frustrating "double coincidence of wants," society spontaneously invented gold and silver coins as a neutral medium of exchange, which eventually evolved into paper banknotes and digital credit.

In his revolutionary anthropological masterwork, Debt: The First 5,000 Years (Melville House, 2011), anthropologist David Graeber delivered a devastating empirical verdict: the barter myth is complete historical fiction. Anthropologists have studied hundreds of indigenous tribes and ancient communities across every continent; not a single society has ever been discovered that operated on internal barter.

"There is no evidence that it ever happened, and there is enormous evidence suggesting that it did not... In small-scale communities, people did not swap chickens for shoes. If a neighbor needed shoes, the shoemaker gave them shoes, because they were neighbors. The transaction was recorded as a social obligation—a debt."

— David Graeber, Debt: The First 5,000 Years

Graeber demonstrated that money did not begin as physical coins in a marketplace; it began as credit and accounting ledgers in the temple bureaucracies of ancient Sumer and Mesopotamia around 3500 BCE. Long before silver was ever minted into coins, Mesopotamian temple accountants recorded debts, grain rations, and temple tribute on clay cuneiform tablets.

The unit of account was the shekel—which was not a coin, but a standardized weight of barley (equivalent to roughly 180 grains of grain). Silver ingots existed, but they were locked inside temple vaults; ordinary farmers and merchants traded using clay debt tokens and credit agreements settled at harvest time. Money was born not as a material commodity, but as a system of bookkeeping—a formalized institutional ledger recording who owed what to whom.

2. The Classical Triad: Medium, Unit, and Store

While money’s metaphysical essence is social trust, its practical economic functionality is defined by three classical functions first identified by Aristotle in his Politics:

  1. Medium of Exchange: Money eliminates the staggering transaction friction of the double coincidence of wants. In a barter system, if you are a software engineer who wants a dental filling, you must find a dentist who happens to need twenty hours of Python programming that week. Money acts as a universal solvent, allowing you to convert specialized labor into a neutral token that any vendor on Earth will accept.
  2. Unit of Account: Money provides a common denominator for measuring economic value. Without a unit of account, an economy with 1,000 goods requires tracking 499,500 individual exchange ratios (shoes per pig, wine per wheat, wool per olive oil). By pricing everything in a single monetary unit (dollars, euros, yen), those 499,500 prices collapse into exactly 1,000 prices, enabling rational calculation, profit-and-loss accounting, and contractual planning.
  3. Store of Value: Money allows an individual to decouple the generation of value from its consumption across time. A strawberry farmer who produces a massive surplus of fruit in June cannot save strawberries to buy winter coats in December; the fruit will rot within days. Money allows the farmer to convert perishable biological output into an enduring claim on future production.

These three functions are frequently in tension. When an asset is an exceptional store of value (like gold or real estate), people hoard it, which impairs its velocity as a medium of exchange. Conversely, when a government prints currency aggressively to stimulate exchange velocity, it incinerates the currency’s function as a store of value.

3. Georg Simmel: The Philosophy of Money and Alienation

In The Philosophy of Money (1900), German sociologist and philosopher Georg Simmel examined the profound psychological and cultural transformations that occur when a civilization transitions into a monetized economy.

Simmel observed that money is the ultimate agent of human liberation: in a feudal village, economic obligations were deeply personal and oppressive. A serf did not owe forty bushels of grain; he owed fealty, physical presence, and military service to a specific lord. Money depersonalizes exchange: when you buy a loaf of bread at a bakery with a $5 bill, neither you nor the baker cares about the other's religion, political ideology, or family lineage. Money creates a sphere of vast personal autonomy, freeing the individual from tribal and feudal surveillance.

Yet Simmel warned that this liberation carries a tragic psychological cost: the quantification and alienation of life.

"Money is the purest example of the tool... It makes all human interactions fungible. But by reducing the qualitative richness of life to a purely quantitative calculation, money fosters a cynical, blasé attitude in which everything has a price, but nothing has intrinsic value."

— Georg Simmel, The Philosophy of Money

When money becomes the universal metric of human worth, qualitative human virtues—loyalty, artistic integrity, romantic devotion, communal solidarity—are commodified. Relationships that once rested on moral obligation are reduced to market contracts. When an institution attempts to solve interpersonal problems exclusively with financial bonuses or penalties, it often destroys the underlying intrinsic motivation.

4. Empirical and Historical Case Studies

Case A: The Cigarette Economy of Stalag Luft III (1945)

In 1945, British economist R. A. Radford published a classic paper in Economica titled "The Economic Organisation of a P.O.W. Camp." Radford was held as a prisoner of war by the German military in Stalag Luft III during World War II.

Inside the camp, several thousand allied soldiers lived in barbed-wire captivity. Every month, the International Red Cross delivered standardized relief parcels containing tinned meat, butter, jam, biscuits, and cigarettes. The prisoners had no access to official state currencies.

Spontaneously, within weeks of arrival, cigarettes emerged as the universal currency of the camp:

  • Prices were quoted in cigarettes: A tin of Red Cross beef cost twenty cigarettes; a shirt cost eighty cigarettes; washing someone's laundry cost two cigarettes. Non-smoking prisoners accepted cigarettes eagerly because they knew every guard and soldier would accept them in trade.
  • Standardization and Portability: Cigarettes possessed ideal physical monetary attributes: they were lightweight, uniform in size, non-perishable (if kept dry), and divisible into individual units.
  • Gresham's Law Appeared: Low-quality, hand-rolled German civilian cigarettes ("dog-ends") drove high-quality British Players and American Lucky Strikes out of circulation. Prisoners smoked the good cigarettes and spent the ragged ones.
  • Monetary Inflation and Deflation: When Allied bombers struck supply lines and Red Cross parcels failed to arrive, the camp experienced a severe liquidity crunch: the money supply shrank, cigarette prices collapsed (a tin of jam dropped from 15 cigarettes to 4), and economic activity froze into depression. Conversely, when a giant convoy of relief trucks arrived, the money supply quintupled overnight, triggering sudden price inflation across all goods.

The Stalag Luft III experience proved that money is not an artificial decree of nation-states; whenever humans engage in complex, multi-sided exchange, a monetary standard spontaneously crystallizes.

Case B: The Six-Month Irish Bank Strike (1970)

On May 1, 1970, commercial bank employees across the Republic of Ireland went on strike over a wage dispute. All commercial retail banks in Ireland closed their doors and remained completely shut for six months—until November 1970.

Traditional monetary theory predicted an immediate, catastrophic economic collapse: over 80 percent of the nation's money supply and clearing mechanisms were frozen behind locked vault doors. Nobody could access savings accounts, clear corporate checks, or draw cash from branches.

Instead of collapsing into primitive barter, the Irish economy continued to grow smoothly throughout the entire six-month strike.

How did society function without a banking system? The local pub owners became the informal central bankers of Ireland.

In 1970s Ireland, the local pub was the central social hub of every town and village. The pub owner possessed detailed, intimate knowledge of every local resident: they knew who held a steady job, who owned fertile farmland, who drank responsibly, and who was an untrustworthy gambler. Citizens simply began writing personal paper cheques to one another, endorsed on the back. Pub owners cashed these cheques, circulating them as cash, or held them as credit. A single paper cheque for £20 would pass through twenty different hands—paying a butcher, an electrician, a schoolteacher, and a grocer—before finally resting in a pub owner’s safe. Society operated on an informal, decentralized credit ledger grounded in communal trust.

5. Actionable Heuristics for Navigating Monetary Realities

  1. Never Confuse Money with Real Wealth: Money is a claim check on real goods and services; real wealth is the physical, intellectual, and organizational capacity to produce those goods and services. A desert island with ten billion dollars of gold or paper currency is a place of starvation; an island with fertile soil, freshwater springs, clean energy, and cooperative human talent is wealthy. In times of systemic crisis, own the productive asset, not merely the claim check.
  2. The Intersubjective Trust Screen: Whenever you hold a currency, cryptocurrency, or debt instrument, remember that its purchasing power exists exclusively in the collective minds of other humans. If institutional legitimacy, military security, or tax enforcement collapses, the currency's value drops to its intrinsic utility (which for paper, fiat numbers, or tokens is exactly zero).
  3. Beware the Monetization of Social Capital (Simmel's Boundary): Keep market norms strictly separated from social and family norms. If you offer a close friend $50 to help you move a sofa into your apartment, you insult them; if you buy them a craft beer and a warm meal, you strengthen a bond of mutual reciprocity. The moment you introduce cash into relationships governed by love, duty, or friendship, you destroy the social ledger.
  4. Audit the Velocity of Your Working Capital: In business and personal finance, remember that money is a medium of exchange: its productive power is amplified by velocity ($M imes V = P imes Y$). Cash sitting dormant in a non-interest-bearing checking account is frozen potential energy decaying under inflation; money continuously reinvested in productive skills, high-yield tools, and compound assets creates self-reinforcing enterprise.

6. Guided Self-Reflection

Examine your psychological relationship with money through these four diagnostic queries:

  • Do I treat money as a tool for autonomy and freedom, or have I turned the accumulation of numerical tokens into an existential end in itself?
  • Where in my personal relationships or team dynamics have I attempted to use financial incentives where genuine human gratitude and empathy were required?
  • If the institutional monetary ledger were disrupted tomorrow, what tangible skills, physical assets, and social networks do I possess that others would eagerly trade with?
  • Am I holding too much of my net worth in nominal currency claims that are quietly eroding in purchasing power?
