Why Organisations Resist Change

Resistance to transformation is rarely the product of irrational obstinacy or intellectual laziness. It is the logical immune response of a complex living organism protecting established status hierarchies, hard-won tacit mastery, psychological safety, and settled internal peace treaties.

📖 10-Minute Deep Read (1,968 words) ⚡ Short on time? Switch to the 2-minute field note summary →

In the late 1820s, British naval engineers approached the Admiralty in London with working demonstrations of a revolutionary technology: steam-powered iron warships. Steam engines freed vessels from the tyranny of prevailing winds, allowed ships to navigate treacherous river estuaries, and could tow timber ships-of-the-line through dead calms. Yet when the First Lord of the Admiralty, Lord Melville, received the proposals, he issued an official, blistering rejection: 'Their Lordships feel it their bounden duty to discourage to the utmost of their ability the employment of steam vessels, as they consider that the introduction of steam is calculated to strike a fatal blow at the naval supremacy of the Empire.' To modern ears, Melville’s decree sounds like an unbelievable caricature of fossilized stupidity. But look beneath the surface into the institutional sociology: for two hundred years, the Royal Navy’s undisputed global mastery rested upon the sublime, non-transferable craft of canvas and rigging. Thousands of aristocratic British sea captains had spent forty years mastering celestial navigation, sail-trimming, and broadside maneuvers. Introducing steam did not merely introduce a new boiler; it rendered two centuries of British institutional capital overnight obsolete. Worse, it transferred tactical authority from refined aristocratic gentlemen on the quarterdeck down to grease-stained, blue-collar mechanics shoveling coal in the stokehold. The Admiralty was not resisting an engine; it was defending an entire social order.

1. The Myth of the Obstinate Employee: Edgar Schein

Whenever a corporate transformation initiative, agile reorganization, or enterprise software migration stalls, executive suites reflexively blame the frontline: "Employees are lazy, stubborn, afraid of the future, or stuck in their ways." Millions of dollars are thrown at change management consultants to 're-educate' workers.

In his pioneering sociological research at MIT Sloan, Edgar Schein exposed the profound fallacy of this view. Organizations do not resist change because they hate innovation; they resist because culture is a learned defense mechanism against anxiety.

"Organizational culture is the accumulated shared learning of a group as it solves its problems of external adaptation and internal integration. It is a set of basic assumptions that have worked well enough to be considered valid and, therefore, to be taught to new members as the correct way to perceive, think, and feel."

Edgar H. Schein, Organizational Culture and Leadership (1985; 5th ed. 2017)
Organizational Sociology

Schein identified the fundamental psychological dynamic of transformation as the battle between two opposing forms of anxiety:

  • Learning Anxiety: The acute terror of trying something new. It is the fear of looking incompetent, clumsy, or foolish during the transition; the fear of losing status; the fear of having your hard-won tacit mastery rendered worthless; and the fear of social ostracism if you abandon peer group norms.
  • Survival Anxiety: The realization that unless the organization changes, it will face bankruptcy, layoffs, irrelevance, or destruction.

Schein’s master principle of change is simple: Change occurs only when Survival Anxiety exceeds Learning Anxiety.

Most corporate leadership teams attempt to force change by turning up the heat—increasing survival anxiety through threats, panic, and firing warnings. This triggers biological panic and entrenchment. The only humane, sustainable path to change is to reduce learning anxiety: providing psychological safety, dedicated training time, temporary forgiveness for transition errors, and dignified status transitions for legacy practitioners.

2. Active Inertia: Donald Sull and Why Good Companies Go Bad

In an influential Harvard Business Review study, London Business School professor Donald Sull demolished the assumption that failing organizations are paralyzed by passive stagnation. Sull coined the term Active Inertia:

"Inertia does not mean inaction. When market conditions shift dramatically, great companies do not stand still. They respond with frantic, energetic activity. But they respond by digging their existing ruts deeper: doing more of what made them successful in the past."

Donald N. Sull, Why Good Companies Go Bad (1999)
Strategic Management

When an enterprise encounters severe environmental turbulence, its leaders experience cognitive overload. Rather than examining their underlying mental models, they accelerate existing routines:

  • Strategic Frames become Blinders: Mental models that once illuminated market opportunities harden into narrow dogmas that screen out disconfirming signals.
  • Processes become Routines: Flexible, agile operating practices ossify into sacred, bureaucratic rituals that must be followed regardless of effectiveness.
  • Relationships become Shackles: Long-standing partnerships with legacy suppliers, distributors, and unions prevent the enterprise from pivoting to new channels.
  • Values become Dogma: Inspiring founding values harden into rigid orthodoxy, where questioning legacy practices is condemned as heresy.

3. Historical Case Studies: When Systems Defend Their Core

Case Study: Kodak and the Digital Camera Dilemma (1975)

The standard folklore of corporate failure treats Eastman Kodak as an arrogant dinosaur that was blind to the digital revolution. The historical reality is far more tragic: Kodak actually invented the world's first digital camera.

In 1975, a brilliant young engineer at Kodak’s research laboratories in Rochester, New York, named Steven Sasson, built a toaster-sized device using a solid-state charge-coupled device (CCD) that captured black-and-white digital images on a cassette tape.

When Sasson demonstrated the breakthrough to the executive committee, the response was frosty disbelief. An executive famously remarked: "That's cute, Steve—but don't tell anyone about it."

Why did Kodak bury its own child? Because Kodak was not primarily a camera company; it was a high-margin chemical manufacturing monopoly. Kodak earned over 70 percent profit margins on the recurring sale of photographic film, silver-halide developer chemicals, and specialized yellow-box photographic paper. Digital cameras had no film, no paper, and no chemicals. To embrace digital photography meant that Kodak had to willingly cannibalize its core multi-billion-dollar chemical cash cow. The corporate immune system suffocated the digital infant to protect the adult chemical host, until digital technology matured outside Kodak's walls and bankrupt the company in 2012.

Case Study: Semmelweis and the Resistance to Antiseptic Handwashing

In 1847, Hungarian physician Ignaz Semmelweis discovered that maternal mortality rates from deadly puerperal (childbed) fever in the First Obstetrical Clinic of the Vienna General Hospital were three times higher when patients were examined by medical students who had just performed post-mortem autopsies on cadavers, compared to clinics run by mid-wives who performed no dissections.

Semmelweis instituted a strict clinical protocol: doctors and students were required to wash their hands in a chlorinated lime solution before examining laboring mothers. Maternal mortality plummeted from 18 percent down to under 2 percent.

Rather than celebrating his triumph, the Viennese medical establishment reacted with ferocious, personal rage. Senior professors of medicine rejected the findings, refused to wash their hands, and Semmelweis was fired from his hospital post.

Why such violent resistance to an inexpensive, life-saving intervention? Because nineteenth-century physicians were proud aristocratic gentlemen. The idea that a gentleman’s hands could be unclean and that the doctors themselves were the primary agents transmitting lethal disease to their vulnerable patients challenged their foundational moral and class identity. The human mind will tolerate thousands of unnecessary deaths before accepting an idea that shatters its own self-image.

4. The Four Pillars of Legitimate Frontline Skepticism

Leaders often dismiss resistance as pure obstinacy, but experienced practitioners know that frontline skepticism is frequently rooted in rational wisdom:

1. The 'Flavor of the Month' Fatigue

Frontline staff have survived three previous CEOs and seven 'game-changing' reorganizations that burned out teams, generated zero durable value, and were abandoned eighteen months later. Frontline workers drag their feet not out of stupidity, but as a rational preservation strategy to wait out executive fads.

2. The Unaddressed Double Workload

Executives announce a massive digital transformation, but demand that employees maintain 100 percent of their daily legacy output while simultaneously learning the new system. When workers are drowned in impossible hours, they naturally cling to the old, fast routine.

3. Tacit Competence Loss

A seasoned professional who could navigate an old database in three seconds with muscle memory is suddenly reduced to an embarrassed, stumbling novice on a modern cloud UI. Leaders must honor and preserve the professional status of veteran craftsmen during transitions.

4. The Broken Incentive Treaty

Often, the change benefits the executive suite (higher margins, centralized control) while making the frontline worker’s job more precarious, stressful, and surveilled. If a change carries all downside and zero upside for employees, resisting it is perfectly rational.

5. Actionable Heuristics: Leading Adaptive Change

To guide an organization through transformation without triggering catastrophic immune rejection, apply John Kotter and Edgar Schein’s strategic playbooks:

Heuristic 1: Reduce Learning Anxiety First

Never announce a major change without simultaneously announcing the safety net: guaranteed retraining, temporary forgiveness for productivity drops during the learning curve, and dedicated onboarding coaches. When you make it safe to be a novice, learning replaces defensive posturing.

Heuristic 2: Co-Design with the Resisters (The Campfire Rule)

Identify the most respected, vocal skeptics in your organization. Do not isolate or fire them. Bring them into the initial architecture council: "Tell me every reason why this change will fail." Listen with genuine humility, incorporate their operational constraints, and let them become co-architects of the transition.

Heuristic 3: Generate and Celebrate Early Short-Term Wins

In John Kotter’s 8-step framework, transformations fail when leaders promise paradise in five years without delivering proof in sixty days. Carve out a tiny, visible pilot project that demonstrates unambiguous, tangible benefits to frontline workers within two months. Success creates its own social proof.

Heuristic 4: Honor the Past (Never Trash the Legacy)

Never tell your team that what they did for the last ten years was wrong, dumb, or backward. Celebrate the legacy system: "The methods we used got us to where we are today; they were brilliant and served us with distinction. But the external environment has changed, and to honor that past excellence, we must build the next vehicle."

6. Guided Self-Reflection

Audit where you personally resist change in your own life and work:

DIAGNOSTIC INQUIRIES

1. The Learning Anxiety Mirror: What new technical tool, software architecture, or professional skill are you privately avoiding learning, not because you lack time, but because you fear feeling like an incompetent novice again?

2. The Kodak Reflex in Your Career: What legacy skill or credential accounts for your current professional identity, that you are fiercely defending even though market signals indicate it is becoming obsolete?

3. The Empathy Audit for Leaders: When someone on your team pushes back against your new initiative, do you immediately label them as 'resistant to change,' or do you investigate what uncompensated workload or lost autonomy they are defending?

Further Reading and Primary Sources

To master organizational change, cultural sociology, and strategic inertia, read these foundational works:

  • Edgar H. Schein, Organizational Culture and Leadership (Jossey-Bass, 1985; 5th ed. 2017). The definitive sociological treatise on culture as an anxiety defense.
  • John P. Kotter, Leading Change (Harvard Business Review Press, 1996). The classic 8-stage transformation roadmap.
  • Donald N. Sull, "Why Good Companies Go Bad," Harvard Business Review, 77(4) (1999): 42–52. The groundbreaking paper on active inertia.
  • Richard P. Rumelt, Good Strategy/Bad Strategy: The Difference and Why It Matters (Crown Business, 2011). Masterful analysis of internal political treaties and strategic focus.
