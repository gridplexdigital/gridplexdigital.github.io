The Commons of Knowledge: Why Open Knowledge Matters

Unlike physical goods such as timber, land, or oil, knowledge is fundamentally non-rivalrous: one person’s consumption does not deplete it, and sharing it multiplies its collective value. Treating knowledge as an open commons fuels compounding innovation, democratic self-governance, and human flourishing.

On the evening of April 12, 1955, the legendary CBS broadcast journalist Edward R. Murrow looked into the television cameras and interviewed a thirty-nine-year-old physician and virologist named Dr. Jonas Salk. Hours earlier, across the United States, church bells had tolled, sirens had wailed, and crowds had wept in the streets following the historic announcement from the University of Michigan: after a massive clinical trial involving 1.8 million school children, Salk’s injected vaccine had proven 90 percent effective in preventing paralytic poliomyelitis. For decades, polio had been humanity’s most terrifying phantom: every summer, public swimming pools, movie theaters, and playgrounds closed as parents lived in paralyzing terror of their children suddenly waking up paralyzed or suffocating in metal iron lungs. Salk was instantly hailed as a global savior. Toward the end of the broadcast, Murrow leaned forward and asked a simple, inevitable question: "Who owns the patent on this vaccine?" Salk smiled gently and delivered one of the most immortal, profound answers in the history of science: "Well, the people, I would say. There is no patent. Could you patent the sun?" Had Jonas Salk and the National Foundation for Infantile Paralysis aggressively patented the vaccine and licensed it for maximum commercial royalty extraction, Wall Street analysts estimated the patent would have been worth approximately $7 billion in today's currency. Instead, Salk and the foundation left the manufacturing protocols completely in the public domain. Pharmaceutical manufacturers across the globe immediately began producing millions of doses at cost. Within a few short decades, an ancient, crippling scourge that had haunted humanity since the time of the Egyptian pharaohs was virtually eradicated from the face of the earth. Salk understood what modern market fundamentalism perpetually forgets: that knowledge is not a commodity to be hoarded; it is a sacred commons that belongs to all humankind.

1. Thomas Jefferson: The Candle That Lights Another

The fundamental economic and philosophical difference between physical property and human knowledge was articulated with sublime clarity by Thomas Jefferson in an August 1813 letter to inventor Isaac McPherson. Jefferson had served as the first administrator of the United States patent system, and his experience had convinced him that ideas are fundamentally distinct from tangible objects:

"If nature has made any one thing less susceptible than all others of exclusive property, it is the action of the thinking power called an idea, which an individual may exclusively possess as long as he keeps it to himself; but the moment it is divulged, it forces itself into the possession of every one, and the receiver cannot dispossess himself of it. Its peculiar character, too, is that no one possesses the less, because every other possesses the whole of it. He who receives an idea from me, receives instruction himself without lessening mine; as he who lights his taper at mine, receives light without darkening me."
— Thomas Jefferson, Letter to Isaac McPherson (August 13, 1813)

Economists describe this property as Non-Rivalry. If you eat an apple, I cannot eat that apple; if you occupy a plot of farmland or mine a ton of copper, that physical matter is depleted and unavailable to anyone else. Tangible goods are rivalrous and zero-sum.

Knowledge, however, is radically non-rivalrous and positive-sum. If you teach me the Pythagorean theorem, read my computer code, or synthesize an mRNA vaccine formula based on my published genomic sequencing, your knowledge does not diminish mine by a single bit. In fact, the exact opposite occurs: the more minds that possess an idea, the higher the probability that someone will discover a flaw, improve the design, or combine it with another concept to create an entirely new breakthrough. Ideas compound through transmission.

To impose private property monopolies upon non-rival knowledge through patents and copyrights requires the artificial, state-enforced creation of artificial scarcity. While temporary intellectual property rights were originally designed as limited utilitarian incentives to encourage inventors, modern corporate legal systems have inverted this logic, transforming intellectual property into an extractive wall of enclosure that strangles the very innovation it was meant to nurture.

2. Elinor Ostrom and Charlotte Hess: The Knowledge Commons

In 1968, ecologist Garrett Hardin published his famous essay "The Tragedy of the Commons," arguing that any shared pasture, fishery, or forest with open access is doomed to ruin because selfish individuals will inevitably overgraze or overfish until the resource collapses. For decades, economists argued that there were only two solutions to prevent the tragedy: either privatize the resource into corporate hands, or impose top-down coercive state regulation.

In 1990, political scientist Elinor Ostrom shattered this binary in her Nobel Prize-winning work, Governing the Commons. Ostrom proved through empirical field studies that communities around the world had successfully managed Common Pool Resources (CPRs)—such as Swiss alpine pastures, Spanish irrigation canals, and Japanese communal forests—for centuries without privatization or state coercion, by establishing bottom-up social norms, clear boundaries, and collective monitoring.

In 2006, Ostrom partnered with information specialist Charlotte Hess to publish Understanding Knowledge as a Commons, extending her institutional framework to digital and intellectual ecosystems:

Physical Commons vs. Knowledge Commons

  • The Physical Commons (Pastures, Fisheries, Groundwater): These resources are subtractable and rivalrous. Overuse leads to depletion, erosion, and collapse. The primary threat is over-harvesting.
  • The Knowledge Commons (Scientific Literature, Code, Cultural Heritage): Knowledge is non-subtractable and non-rivalrous. You cannot "over-read" a book or "wear out" a mathematical equation. In fact, unused knowledge withers away into irrelevance. The primary threats to the knowledge commons are enclosure, artificial paywalls, privatization, and digital lock-in.

Hess and Ostrom showed that when private corporate interests enclose the knowledge commons—locking scientific papers behind $35 paywalls, patenting biological gene sequences, or enclosing cultural folklore through perpetual copyright extensions—they trigger what legal scholar Michael Heller termed the Tragedy of the Anticommons. In an anticommons, so many fragmented property owners hold rights of exclusion that no one can effectively access or use the resource, paralyzing scientific research and human progress.

3. Yochai Benkler and Commons-Based Peer Production

In 2006, Harvard Law professor Yochai Benkler published The Wealth of Networks: How Social Production Transforms Markets and Freedom, identifying the emergence of an entirely new mode of economic organization: Commons-Based Peer Production.

Throughout the industrial era, large-scale human cooperation required either the price signals of the capitalist market or the managerial hierarchies of the corporate firm (as Ronald Coase articulated in his theory of transaction costs). Factories, printing presses, and broadcast television towers were wildly expensive capital assets that only corporations or states could afford.

The personal computer and the internet decentralized the physical capital of production. The tools required to process, store, and communicate information were suddenly distributed across hundreds of millions of ordinary citizens. Benkler demonstrated that in this digital environment, voluntary networks of individuals collaborating without managerial command or monetary compensation can systematically out-produce and out-innovate the world's largest commercial hierarchies:

"Commons-based peer production is a new socio-economic model of production where the creative energy of large numbers of people is coordinated (usually with the aid of the internet) into large, meaningful projects mostly without traditional hierarchical organization or financial compensation."
— Yochai Benkler, The Wealth of Networks (2006)

The undisputed historical proof of Benkler’s thesis is the battle between Microsoft Encarta and Wikipedia. In the 1990s, Microsoft—the richest, most powerful technology corporation on earth—spent millions hiring distinguished professors, editors, and cartographers to produce the ultimate multimedia encyclopedia on CD-ROM. In 2001, Jimmy Wales and Larry Sanger launched Wikipedia: an open website where anyone could create and edit articles under an open Creative Commons license without being paid a penny.

Mainstream economists and publishing executives predicted that Wikipedia would degenerate into a cesspool of libel, schoolboy vandalism, and chaotic misinformation. Yet by 2009, Microsoft surrendered, shutting down Encarta forever. Today, Wikipedia hosts over 60 million articles in more than 300 languages, edited by hundreds of thousands of passionate volunteer editors operating under transparent Ostrom-like community norms (such as Neutral Point of View, verifiable citations, and consensus moderation). It is the fifth most visited website in human history, remaining entirely nonprofit, non-commercial, and free to every child with an internet connection.

4. The Epic Battle for the Human Genome: Public Commons vs. Celera

The most consequential, high-stakes battle over the knowledge commons in human history occurred between 1998 and 2003 over the fundamental operating code of biological life: the Human Genome Project.

In 1990, the United States National Institutes of Health (NIH), the Department of Energy, and the Wellcome Trust in the United Kingdom launched the publicly funded Human Genome Project, led by Francis Collins, John Sulston, and Eric Lander. The international consortium recognized that the three billion base pairs of human DNA constituted the shared patrimony of our species. In 1996, at a historic meeting in the Caribbean, the consortium adopted the Bermuda Principles: every sequencing laboratory agreed to release all identified DNA sequence data into the public database (GenBank) within twenty-four hours, completely unencumbered by patents, copyrights, or proprietary claims.

Then, in May 1998, brilliant biochemist and entrepreneur J. Craig Venter formed a private commercial enterprise named Celera Genomics. Backed by hundreds of millions of dollars in private venture capital and armed with cutting-edge automated shotgun sequencers, Venter announced that Celera would sequence the human genome faster and cheaper than the bureaucratic public consortium.

However, Celera’s business model was based on enclosing the biological commons: Celera planned to file preliminary patent applications on thousands of newly discovered human genes, lock the complete genomic sequence behind a private, proprietary database, and charge pharmaceutical companies and academic universities millions of dollars in annual subscription fees to view their own genetic code.

What followed was a furious, round-the-clock technological sprint. Public consortium scientists worked eighteen-hour shifts, scaling up their public sequencers to ensure that the open data stayed ahead of Celera’s proprietary claims. The public scientists understood that once a patent was granted on a human gene, any researcher seeking to develop a diagnostic test for breast cancer, Alzheimer’s, or cystic fibrosis would have to pay exorbitant licensing fees to a private corporate tollbooth.

The conflict reached the highest levels of global diplomacy. In March 2000, US President Bill Clinton and British Prime Minister Tony Blair issued a joint declaration urging that human genomic data be made freely accessible to scientists everywhere. The announcement caused biotechnology stocks to plunge overnight, forcing Celera to negotiate. On June 26, 2000, Clinton and Blair stood alongside Francis Collins and Craig Venter in the White House East Room to announce the completion of the first draft of the human genome.

Because the public consortium held the line and insisted on radical openness, the human genome remained a free, unpatentable global commons. Every modern breakthrough in personalized cancer immunotherapy, CRISPR gene editing, and mRNA vaccines rests squarely on top of the unencumbered, public foundation preserved by the defenders of the commons.

5. Aaron Swartz and the Open Access Crusade

While genomics was saved, the vast majority of human academic scientific research remains trapped behind corporate enclosures. In the late 20th century, commercial publishing conglomerates (such as Elsevier, Springer Nature, and Wiley) pulled off one of the greatest economic extractions in history.

The economics of modern academic publishing are astonishing:

  • Taxpayers fund the original scientific research through government grants (NIH, NSF, European Research Council).
  • Scientists write the research papers without receiving a penny of payment from the publishers.
  • Other scientists review the papers (peer review) entirely for free as professional service.
  • Commercial publishers then package these papers into digital PDFs, claim exclusive copyright, and sell access back to the public and university libraries for hundreds of millions of dollars in subscription bundles, yielding operating profit margins exceeding 35 to 40 percent—higher than Apple, Google, or ExxonMobil.

In 2008, a twenty-one-year-old computer prodigy, co-founder of Reddit, and digital activist named Aaron Swartz wrote the Guerilla Open Access Manifesto in Eremo, Italy. Swartz exposed the moral obscenity of locking the world’s scientific heritage behind private tollbooths:

"Information is power. But like all power, there are those who want to keep it for themselves. The world’s entire scientific and cultural heritage, published over centuries in books and journals, is increasingly being digitized and locked up by a handful of private corporations... Providing access to those without it is a moral imperative."
— Aaron Swartz, Guerilla Open Access Manifesto (2008)

In 2011, Swartz was arrested for downloading millions of academic articles from the digital repository JSTOR through an unlocked wiring closet at MIT, intending to release them into the public domain. Targeted by aggressive federal prosecutors threatening decades in prison, Swartz tragically took his own life in January 2013 at age twenty-six. His sacrifice ignited a global Open Access revolution. Today, global funding bodies, initiatives like Plan S, and open repositories like arXiv and bioRxiv are dismantling the paywall regime, ensuring that scientific discovery belongs to the humanity that paid for it.

6. Core Heuristics for Defending and Contributing to the Commons

1. Default to Open: The Public Goods Mindset

Whenever you produce foundational tools, educational materials, software libraries, or research insights, ask: "Is this a proprietary competitive advantage, or is it a non-rival public good that creates 100x more value if open?" By defaulting to open licenses (Creative Commons, MIT, Apache), you transform your work from a perishable private secret into compounding infrastructure that entire ecosystems build upon.

2. Protect Against the Tragedy of the Anticommons

Be fiercely vigilant against patent thickets and defensive intellectual property hoarding. In biotechnology and software, excessive patenting does not encourage innovation; it forces startups to spend more on patent attorneys and litigation defense than on engineering and research. Support open standards, open APIs, and patent pools that prevent tollbooth capture.

3. Institutionalize Ostrom’s Design Principles

Open communities do not survive on good vibes alone; they require robust governance. If you lead an open-source project or community wiki, implement Elinor Ostrom’s core CPR rules: define clear participation boundaries, establish transparent dispute-resolution mechanisms, institute graduated sanctions for malicious actors, and ensure that those affected by rules participate in modifying them.

4. Support Preprints and Open Access Publishing

If you are a researcher, academic, or institutional funder, refuse to sign away exclusive copyrights to predatory paywalled journals. Upload your work to open preprint servers (arXiv, bioRxiv, SSRN, OSF) before submission, and publish in fully open-access journals. Knowledge produced with public funds must remain accessible to doctors in Kenya, students in India, and curious citizens everywhere.

7. Self-Diagnostic Checklist: Auditing Your Relationship to Knowledge

  • When I acquire valuable expertise or solve a difficult technical problem, do I document it publicly so others can learn from it, or do I hoard it to protect my personal indispensability?
  • Does my organization build exclusively on top of open-source software and open standards without contributing financial support, bug fixes, or documentation back to the commons?
  • Am I locked into closed, proprietary platforms that own my personal data and digital creations, or do I maintain my content in portable, open formats (Markdown, open databases)?
  • Do I support public libraries, community archives, and open knowledge initiatives like Wikipedia and the Internet Archive through financial donations or active volunteer participation?
  • When reading or citing scientific research, do I verify whether the underlying data and code are openly available for independent reproduction?
