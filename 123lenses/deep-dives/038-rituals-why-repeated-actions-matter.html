Rituals: Why Repeated Actions Matter

Rituals are far more than archaic superstitions or empty formal gestures. By embedding symbolic meaning in rigid, repetitive, non-utilitarian actions, human rituals coordinate trust, synchronize social bonding, silence catastrophic anxiety, and anchor personal identity in an unpredictable universe.

In the remote, misty highland rainforests of the Bismarck Mountain Range in Papua New Guinea, the Tsembaga Maring people lived for centuries in an environment of perpetual ecological and martial fragility. Small clans cultivated steep sweet potato gardens on jungle slopes, hemmed in by rival tribes with whom they periodically fought vicious wars with bows and stone axes. Without written law, courts, money, or a centralized government, how did the Maring prevent cycles of retaliatory revenge from wiping out their civilization? How did they manage delicate soil fertility, food security, and territorial boundaries? The answer was not a bureaucratic treaty or an economic market; it was an elaborate, decennial ritual cycle centered on pigs: the kaiko. When pig herds multiplied over a decade to the point where they overwhelmed the labor of women and began destroying neighboring gardens, social friction surged toward war. At this boiling point, the tribal elders planted a sacred boundary plant called the rumbim into the soil and declared the commencement of the kaiko. Over the next year, the entire society shifted into ritual overdrive: neighboring clans and former enemies were invited into the village under sacred safe-conduct; dancers painted their bodies in shimmering ochre and bird-of-paradise plumes, singing and stamping in unison for nights on end. Finally, in a climactic festival, hundreds of pigs were ritually slaughtered, their succulent fat and meat roasted in earth ovens and distributed to allies to repay social debts, seal peace pacts, and restore biological vigor. When the last pig was consumed and the rumbim was uprooted, the ecological ledger was completely wiped clean: the forest had time to regenerate, the pig population was reset to zero, and the peace was preserved. What modern rationalists dismiss as "primitive superstition" was, in reality, a masterclass in ecological systems engineering, cybernetic feedback, and conflict resolution executed entirely through ritual.

1. Émile Durkheim: Collective Effervescence and the Sacred

In the early 20th century, the French founding father of sociology, Émile Durkheim, published his masterwork, The Elementary Forms of the Religious Life (1912). Durkheim sought to understand what held human societies together in the absence of absolute monarchs or brutal military force. By analyzing the sacred totemic rituals of Australian Aboriginal clans, Durkheim arrived at an astonishing conclusion: rituals do not exist primarily to satisfy abstract theological curiosities about the heavens; rituals exist to generate the glue that prevents human groups from disintegrating into selfish chaos.

Durkheim divided the human experience into two distinct, fundamentally separate realms:

  • The Profane: The mundane, utilitarian, everyday realm of individual survival—gathering food, sleeping, calculating transactions, and tending to personal bodily needs. In the profane world, we are isolated, selfish, and fragmented.
  • The Sacred: That which is set apart, protected by taboos, and imbued with transcendent significance. The sacred is not necessarily a deity; it can be an altar, a national flag, an ancestor's skull, or a constitutional scroll.

How does a group transform ordinary objects and people into something sacred? Through the collective physical performance of ritual. Durkheim described the psychological explosion that occurs when human bodies gather in close physical proximity, moving in identical rhythms, chanting identical words, and focusing their attention upon a single shared symbol:

"The very fact of the concentration acts as an exceptionally powerful stimulant. When they are once come together, a sort of electricity is formed by their collecting which quickly transports them to an extraordinary degree of exaltation. Every sentiment expressed finds a place without resistance in all the minds, which are very open to outside impressions; each re-echoes the others, and is re-echoed by the others."
— Émile Durkheim, The Elementary Forms of the Religious Life (1912)

Durkheim termed this psychological state Collective Effervescence. In this ecstatic state, the solitary individual forgets their selfish aches, fears, and egoism. They feel carried along by an invisible, colossal power greater than themselves. Durkheim pointed out the ultimate anthropological truth: that "god" worshiped by the tribe in the midst of collective effervescence is nothing other than society itself, experiencing its own collective vitality in symbolic form.

2. Roy Rappaport: Costly Signaling and Ecological Regulation

In his landmark 1968 study Pigs for the Ancestors and his 1999 treatise Ritual and Religion in the Making of Humanity, anthropologist Roy Rappaport brought evolutionary biology and cybernetics into the study of ritual.

Rappaport asked a fundamental game-theoretic question: In human societies, talk is cheap. Anyone can verbally promise to be a loyal friend, a brave warrior, or an honest trading partner, only to defect the moment danger arises. Why do societies spend enormous quantities of physical energy, scarce food, and excruciating pain performing complex rituals that produce no immediate material harvest?

Rappaport demonstrated that rituals function as unfalsifiable, costly signals of social commitment. A ritual cannot be executed half-heartedly. When a young warrior undergoes a grueling scarification rite, dancing for three days without sleep in blistering heat, or when a community slaughters half its livestock in an ancestral banquet, they are paying an enormous physical and biological tax. That very costliness makes the signal reliable. By willingly submitting to the invariant order of the ritual, the individual publicly subordinates their private autonomy to the collective authority of the group.

Furthermore, Rappaport demonstrated that rituals act as thermostats for complex social-ecological systems. In the Tsembaga Maring pig feast, the society possessed no statistical spreadsheets, no census bureau, and no ecological forestry monitors. Yet the ritual of the kaiko was triggered precisely when the ratio of pigs to human labor reached a threshold of psychological distress. The ritual automatically reset the system before ecological collapse or catastrophic starvation occurred:

"Ritual is not merely a way to express feelings or an artistic performance; it is the basic social act upon which all human communication, morality, and systemic equilibrium depend."
— Roy Rappaport, Ritual and Religion in the Making of Humanity (1999)

3. Arnold van Gennep and Victor Turner: The Magic of Liminality

How do human beings transition across life's major thresholds—from childhood to adulthood, from civilian to soldier, from single person to spouse, from life to death? In 1909, French ethnographer Arnold van Gennep identified the universal architecture governing every transformative ritual across world cultures: the Rite of Passage. Van Gennep showed that every transformative ritual follows a precise three-stage sequence:

The Three Stages of Transformative Ritual

  1. Separation (Pre-Liminal): The initiate is violently stripped of their previous social identity, clothing, and physical environment. Military recruits have their heads shaved, uniforms replaced with identical fatigues, and personal belongings confiscated. A bride leaves her parents' home and changes into a ceremonial dress.
  2. Liminality (Liminal / The Threshold): From the Latin limen, meaning doorway or threshold. In this middle stage, the initiate exists in a suspended state: they are no longer who they were, but not yet who they will become. Anthropologist Victor Turner expanded van Gennep’s theory in The Ritual Process (1969), showing that in the liminal state, ordinary social hierarchies, titles, and property rights dissolve. Initiates experience pure, unadulterated human brotherhood, a state Turner called Communitas. They are humbled, stripped naked, and exposed to sacred teachings.
  3. Reincorporation (Post-Liminal): The initiate re-enters ordinary society, but bearing an entirely new status, permanent insignia, adult rights, and solemn community responsibilities. The recruit marches out as a Marine; the boy returns as an initiated man permitted to speak in the council.

Modern secular society has largely abolished formal rites of passage, reducing transitions to bureaucratic paper stamps (a driver’s license, a college diploma mailed in an envelope). Yet human psychology remains hungry for the transformative power of liminality. When institutions fail to provide structured, meaningful rites of passage, youth inevitably invent their own underground, often dangerous substitutes—such as violent street gang initiations, fraternity hazing rituals, or reckless thrill-seeking—to prove their crossing into adulthood.

4. The All Blacks Haka: Neurological Synchrony and Invulnerability

A breathtaking contemporary demonstration of the biological power of collective ritual is the performance of the Haka by the New Zealand All Blacks rugby team before international test matches.

Originally an ancient Maori war dance composed of rhythmic body slapping, tongue protrusion (*whetero*), eye-widening defiance (*pukana*), and thunderous foot stamping, the Haka was composed in its most famous form, Ka Mate, around 1820 by the warrior chief Te Rauparaha to celebrate escaping his pursuers. When performed by fifteen towering international athletes on a grass stadium in front of 80,000 screaming spectators, it is not mere sports theater; it is a profound psychophysiological weapon.

Cognitive scientist Dimitris Xygalatas, director of the Experimental Anthropology Lab at the University of Connecticut, has spent decades using biometric sensors to measure the physiology of participants in extreme, high-arousal rituals around the world—from fire-walking rituals in San Pedro Manrique, Spain, to synchronized religious processions in Mauritius. Xygalatas discovered that during synchronized physical rituals, an astonishing phenomenon occurs: the heart rates and autonomic nervous systems of participants synchronize down to the millisecond.

When humans move in exact physical unison, their brains release massive surges of endorphins and oxytocin, dampening physical pain, shutting down subjective fear, and dissolving individual ego boundaries. For the All Blacks, the Haka functions as a neurochemical catalyst: in ninety seconds, fifteen distinct individuals are fused into a singular, fear-resistant, hyper-bonded organism. For the opposing team standing thirty meters away, the sheer evolutionary power of seeing a synchronized human phalanx issuing a primal challenge triggers immediate, involuntary sympathetic stress responses: elevated cortisol, dilated pupils, and elevated heart rates before the whistle has even blown.

5. The Neuroscience of Personal Ritual: Anxiety, Control, and the Checklist

Rituals operate not only on large groups; they exert profound, measurable effects on solitary human nervous systems. In recent years, behavioral psychologists such as Michael Norton and Francesca Gino at Harvard Business School have investigated why elite athletes, surgeons, and performing artists develop bizarre, rigid private rituals.

Consider tennis legend Rafael Nadal. Nadal’s pre-point ritual is legendary for its compulsive, millimeter precision: he places two energy drink bottles at his chair with the labels turned at a precise angle toward the baseline; he crosses lines only with his right foot first; he wipes his sweat, adjusts his shirt on both shoulders, tucks his hair behind his left ear, touches his nose, tucks his hair behind his right ear, and bounces the ball an exact number of times before every single serve. To an outside observer, this looks like obsessive-compulsive pathology. But to the human nervous system, it is an ingenious cognitive defense mechanism.

In laboratory experiments where participants were subjected to severe social anxiety (such as singing in public or taking high-stakes mathematical exams), researchers instructed half the participants to perform a brief, arbitrary personal ritual before the task—for example, sprinkling salt on a piece of paper, folding it three times, and counting to ten. The results were dramatic:

  • Participants who performed the ritual exhibited significantly lower salivary cortisol levels and lower heart-rate spikes.
  • fMRI neuroimaging reveals that engaging in a rigid, repetitive, predictable sequence of actions temporarily suppresses activity in the Anterior Cingulate Cortex (ACC)—the brain's internal alarm bell that detects errors, monitors threats, and generates catastrophic anxiety.
  • By imposing strict, self-controlled order over their immediate physical movements, individuals restore a visceral sense of control in an otherwise unpredictable, high-stakes environment, preventing the brain from freezing or choking under pressure.

This exact psychological mechanism was harnessed by surgeon Atul Gawande in his creation of the WHO Surgical Safety Checklist. As documented in his 2009 work, The Checklist Manifesto, Gawande introduced a mandatory, 19-item pause in operating rooms across eight global hospitals. Before administering anesthesia, the team must execute a verbal ritual: every nurse, surgeon, and technician must state their name and role out loud; they confirm the patient's identity and surgical site; and they review anticipated emergency risks.

While framed as an engineering tool, the checklist operates culturally as a secular operating room ritual. By forcing the senior, god-like chief surgeon to state his name and invite input from the youngest scrub nurse, the ritual temporarily flattens the toxic medical hierarchy, licensing junior personnel to speak up if they observe a contaminated instrument or an incorrect dosage. In its initial global trial, this simple ritual slashed major surgical complications by 36% and cut patient mortality by an astonishing 47 percent.

6. Core Heuristics for Harnessing the Power of Ritual

1. Distinguish Routines from Habits and Rituals

Do not confuse habits, routines, and rituals:

  • Habit: An automatic, unconscious behavioral loop triggered by a cue to obtain an immediate reward (e.g., mindlessly brushing your teeth or checking notifications).
  • Routine: A deliberate, instrumental sequence of functional actions executed for efficiency (e.g., following a morning commute checklist).
  • Ritual: A deliberate sequence of symbolic, non-utilitarian actions performed with emotional intentionality, rigid adherence, and transcendent meaning (e.g., lighting a candle, pausing for a gratitude reflection, or gathering a family for a ceremonial meal). Routines organize your calendar; rituals nourish your soul.

2. Design Personal Shutdown Rituals to Protect Deep Work

In a hyper-connected, remote-work knowledge economy, the boundary between professional stress and domestic peace has disintegrated. Computer scientist Cal Newport recommends establishing a formal Workday Shutdown Ritual: at the end of each workday, conduct an unvarying sequence—review open task lists, close every browser tab, whisper an invariant concluding phrase (e.g., "Shutdown complete"), and physically shut the laptop lid. This symbolic closure signals to your anterior cingulate cortex that the cognitive loop is sealed, preventing open work anxieties from poisoning your evening rest.

3. Build Shared Team Rites to Foster Psychological Safety

High-performing teams do not bond exclusively through formal quarterly performance reviews; they bond through shared, informal rituals. Whether it is an unvarying Monday morning coffee check-in where work talk is forbidden, a ceremonial "failure funeral" celebrating ambitious experiments that didn't work, or an annual team retreat with structured rites of welcoming, shared rituals create the communitas that makes vulnerability and innovation safe.

4. Protect Sacred Spaces from Profane Intrusion

Follow Durkheim’s golden rule of spatial hygiene: never allow the profane to contaminate the sacred. If your dinner table is the sacred center of family bonding and intimate conversation, ban smartphones, laptops, and spreadsheet review from that physical perimeter without exception. Once a sacred space is polluted by profane digital noise, the ritual spell is broken.

7. Self-Diagnostic Checklist: Auditing Your Ritual Landscape

  • Do I possess meaningful personal rituals that demarcate major psychological transitions in my day (waking, starting deep work, ending work, entering sleep), or do I drift mindlessly between reactive states?
  • When my team or family experiences a profound milestone, victory, or tragic loss, do we mark it with a solemn shared ritual, or do we quickly gloss over it and return to business as usual?
  • Have the traditions and ceremonies in my organization deteriorated into empty, bureaucratic formalities that evoke cynical eye-rolling rather than genuine meaning?
  • What sacred physical spaces or times in my life have been colonized by profane distractions, such as doomscrolling in bed or answering work emails at the dinner table?
  • How does my community welcome newcomers and initiate them into our shared values? Do we provide a structured rite of passage, or do we leave them to sink or swim?
