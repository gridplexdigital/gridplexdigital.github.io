Why Measurement Changes Behaviour

The moment a quantitative measure is elevated into a target for reward or punishment, human intelligence shifts from pursuing the genuine underlying mission to gaming the proxy number. Metrics are valuable sensory inputs, but mistaking the speedometer for the destination corrupts institutional integrity.

In the mid-2000s, British public health authorities instituted an ambitious, high-profile performance metric aimed at eliminating emergency room delays across the National Health Service (NHS): hospital trusts were mandated to ensure that 98 percent of patients arriving at emergency departments were either admitted to an inpatient ward or treated and discharged within exactly four hours of arrival. Hospital chief executives faced immediate public humiliation, loss of autonomy, and termination if their facilities missed the benchmark. On official statistical dashboards, the reform was a triumph: four-hour compliance soared. But when investigative journalists and independent clinical auditors stepped into hospital corridors, they uncovered a dark, surreal theatre of metric gaming. Paramedics were ordered to keep acute patients waiting inside idling ambulances parked outside hospital bays for hours because the official 'arrival clock' was legally paused until the patient physically crossed the threshold into the building. Other hospitals removed wheels from triage stretchers and reclassified them as 'beds' to claim patients had been admitted, or created fake hallway holding areas called 'Clinical Decision Units' where patients languished without care purely to stop the ticking dashboard timer. The metric looked immaculate; patient care had deteriorated.

1. The Formulations: Goodhart, Campbell, and Strathern

In modern organizational management, the ubiquitous slogan popularized by business guru Peter Drucker (and often misattributed to Lord Kelvin) is: "What gets measured gets managed." Executives treat metrics as objective, neutral instruments that shine light on performance, believing that attaching financial bonuses or punishments to numbers is the fastest path to excellence.

Social science, however, has proven that measurement is never neutral. Across economics, education, sociology, and anthropology, three foundational laws explain why quantitative optimization backfires:

1. Goodhart's Law (Charles Goodhart, 1975)

Formulated by British economist Charles Goodhart while advising the Bank of England on monetary policy:

"Any statistical regularity will tend to collapse once pressure is placed upon it for control purposes."

Goodhart observed that when the central bank attempted to control inflation by targeting specific monetary aggregates (such as the M3 money supply), financial institutions immediately invented new shadow-banking credit instruments that evaded the formal statistical definition. The historical correlation between the metric and economic health vanished the moment the metric was used as a lever of power.

2. Campbell's Law (Donald T. Campbell, 1979)

Formulated by social psychologist Donald Campbell when studying educational and social indicators:

"The more any quantitative social indicator is used for social decision-making, the more subject it will be to corruption pressures and the more apt it will be to distort and corrupt the social processes it is intended to monitor."

When standardized test scores become the sole criteria for funding public schools or firing teachers, schools stop educating children. Instead, they teach exclusively to the test, eliminate art and physical education, suspend struggling students during testing week, or engage in outright erasure and alteration of answer sheets.

3. Strathern's Formulation (Marilyn Strathern, 1997)

British anthropologist Marilyn Strathern compressed these insights into the canonical, elegant maxim:

"When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure."

"Metric fixation is the aspiration to replace human judgment with numerical indicators of comparative performance. It produces unintended consequences: goal displacement, gaming, short-termism, and the loss of the professional ethos that once motivated real excellence."

Jerry Z. Muller, The Tyranny of Metrics (2018)
Organizational Sociology

2. The Mechanics of Distortion: How Humans Game Proxies

Why does metric fixation consistently backfire? In his incisive study The Tyranny of Metrics, historian Jerry Z. Muller analyzed the systematic pathologies that arise whenever organizations substitute quantitative proxies for holistic professional judgment:

  • Goal Displacement (Surrogate Gaming): The proxy metric displaces the true mission. A customer support department targets 'average call resolution time under three minutes'; customer service representatives quickly hang up on customers with complex, difficult problems to hit their speed targets, destroying customer satisfaction.
  • Creaming (Cherry-Picking): Decision-makers select only easy, low-risk cases. Surgeons evaluated on raw mortality rates refuse to operate on critically ill, high-risk patients who desperately need surgery, redirecting them to rival hospitals to protect their survival statistics.
  • Lowering the Standard: Police departments evaluated on reducing major felony crime rates systematically reclassify armed burglaries as minor petty larcenies, or intimidate victims to prevent them from filing formal crime reports.
  • Short-Termism: Corporate executives facing quarterly earnings per share (EPS) targets slash R&D budgets, cancel maintenance on factory equipment, and execute aggressive stock buybacks, inflating short-term quarterly bonuses while hollowing out the enterprise's ten-year competitive survival.

3. Historical Case Studies: Metric Catastrophes in Practice

Case Study: The Soviet Gosplan Factory Paradoxes

In the centralized command economy of the Soviet Union, the state planning agency (Gosplan) set quantitative production quotas for every industrial enterprise. Lacking a price mechanism to signal consumer desire, bureaucrats relied on single metrics:

  • When Gosplan evaluated nail manufacturing plants by total weight of output, factories produced a tiny handful of colossal, five-hundred-pound steel spikes that bent train tracks and were useless for carpentry. When Gosplan switched the quota to total quantity of nails, factories manufactured millions of tiny, razor-thin brads that broke at the tap of a hammer.
  • When flat glass factories were evaluated by square meters of glass, factories rolled sheets so paper-thin that the panes shattered during the slightest breeze. When Gosplan switched the metric to weight of glass, factories manufactured sheets so impossibly thick that light could barely penetrate the windows.

Workers and plant managers were not lazy; they were brilliantly rational agents optimizing the exact mathematical function handed down by the state. The error was assuming a single numerical proxy could capture the multi-dimensional quality of human utility.

Case Study: Wells Fargo and the 'Eight Is Great' Cross-Selling Scandal

For over a decade, Wall Street celebrated Wells Fargo as the undisputed gold standard of retail banking. The source of this acclaim was its legendary cross-selling ratio: the average number of financial products (checking accounts, credit cards, mortgages, auto loans) held by each customer household.

Leadership instituted a relentless corporate mantra: "Eight is Great"—mandating that branch employees cross-sell eight distinct accounts to every family. Branch managers and tellers were subjected to extreme daily surveillance: regional managers held hourly conference calls threatening immediate termination and public humiliation for anyone who missed their daily sales quota.

Faced with mathematically impossible targets in saturated markets, thousands of employees engaged in wholesale consumer fraud: secretly forging customer signatures, opening over 3.5 million unauthorized bank accounts and credit cards, moving money between accounts without customer knowledge, and charging customers overdraft fees on cards they never requested. When the scandal erupted in 2016, Wells Fargo was hit with over $3 billion in regulatory fines, its CEO was forced to resign in disgrace, and the Federal Reserve imposed an unprecedented asset cap that paralyzed the bank’s commercial growth for years. A metric intended to measure customer relationship depth had destroyed customer trust.

Case Study: Robert McNamara and the Body-Count War in Vietnam

During the Vietnam War in the 1960s, United States Secretary of Defense Robert McNamara—a former Ford Motor Company president and statistical control pioneer—sought to manage the counterinsurgency war using computerized, quantitative systems analysis. McNamara believed that every human endeavor could be reduced to mathematical optimization.

Because there were no stable territorial frontlines in guerrilla warfare, the Pentagon selected a primary metric to track military progress: enemy casualty body count. Military commands and promotions were tied directly to reported enemy dead, on the statistical theory that once the US military killed enemy combatants faster than the Viet Cong could replenish them (the 'crossover point'), victory was mathematically guaranteed.

The result was a catastrophic moral and strategic distortion. Faced with relentless demands for higher body counts, field commanders routinely inflated numbers, counting innocent civilian villagers, water buffalo, and unconfirmed shadows as enemy combatants.

More critically, the body-count metric completely blinded military leadership to the true political nature of counterinsurgency: winning the allegiance and trust of the rural civilian population. American battalions decimated rural areas to generate high body counts, alienating the very population whose loyalty was the sole prerequisite for strategic victory. McNamara optimized the mathematical scoreboard while losing the war.

4. Actionable Heuristics for Grounded Measurement

Quantitative metrics are not evil; they are indispensable navigational instruments. The grounded leader protects their organization by treating metrics as sensory dials, not automated steering wheels:

Heuristic 1: Pair Countervailing Metrics (The Balancing Dial)

Never measure speed without simultaneously measuring quality; never measure volume without measuring customer retention; never measure cost reduction without measuring error rates. When an engineer or salesperson knows that optimizing Metric A will trigger an alarm on Countervailing Metric B, metric gaming is suppressed.

Heuristic 2: Decouple Measurement from High-Stakes Financial Triggers

Use metrics for epistemic diagnosis and learning, not as automated triggers for compensation bonuses or immediate firing. The moment you tie a specific quarterly bonus to hitting a single number, you guarantee the number will be gamed. Compensate professionals on holistic, qualitative evaluations conducted by peers who know the work.

Heuristic 3: Audit the Gaming Ecosystem (Talk to the Frontline)

Regularly ask frontline employees: "What dumb, absurd things are you currently doing just to make our reporting dashboard look green?" Frontline staff always know where the metrics distort reality. Listen without defensiveness and reform the metric.

Heuristic 4: Value the Qualitative and the Unmeasurable

Recognize that the most precious assets of an enduring institution—trust, integrity, morale, craftsmanship, long-term brand equity, and mentorship—are inherently unmeasurable by quantitative spreadsheets. As sociologist William Bruce Cameron famously observed: "Not everything that counts can be counted, and not everything that can be counted counts."

5. Guided Self-Reflection

Audit how measurement shapes your own life and professional habits:

DIAGNOSTIC INQUIRIES

1. The Personal Fitness/Health Proxy: Are you gaming your personal fitness trackers (e.g., obsessing over 10,000 steps on an Apple Watch by pacing your living room, or fasting unnaturally to hit a temporary scale weight) while neglecting actual physical strength, mobility, and deep sleep?

2. The Vanity Metric in Your Business: What number does your company celebrate on investor decks (page views, registered users, gross merchandise volume) that masks underlying financial unprofitability or poor customer retention?

3. The Subconscious Metric Trap: If your social media follower count or bank account balance was permanently hidden from view, how would your daily work and creative output change?

Further Reading and Primary Sources

To master the dynamics of institutional metrics, gaming, and organizational sociology, read these essential works:

  • Jerry Z. Muller, The Tyranny of Metrics (Princeton University Press, 2018). The definitive sociological critique of metric fixation.
  • Charles A. E. Goodhart, "Problems of Monetary Management: The U.K. Experience," in Papers in Monetary Economics (Reserve Bank of Australia, 1975). The original articulation of Goodhart's Law.
  • Donald T. Campbell, "Assessing the Impact of Planned Social Change," Evaluation and Program Planning, 2(1) (1979): 67–90. The formulation of Campbell's Law.
  • Cathy O'Neil, Weapons of Math Destruction: How Big Data Increases Inequality and Threatens Democracy (Crown, 2016). Modern algorithmic extensions of metric gaming and proxy distortion.
