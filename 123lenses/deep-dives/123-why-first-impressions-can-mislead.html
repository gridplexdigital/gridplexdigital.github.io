Why first impressions can mislead

Early information serves as an unyielding psychological anchor. The brain treats initial impressions not as tentative hypotheses to be tested, but as foundational verdicts around which all subsequent evidence is reshaped.

📖 10-Minute Deep Read (2,042 words) ⚡ Short on time? Switch to the 2-minute field note summary →

In the early morning hours of July 3, 1988, the guided-missile cruiser USS Vincennes was engaged in surface combat with Iranian gunboats in the Persian Gulf. In the ship’s dark, high-tech Combat Information Center, computer consoles suddenly illuminated an unidentified aerial track climbing out of Bandar Abbas international airport. The aircraft was Iran Air Flight 655, a commercial Airbus A300 carrying 290 civilian passengers and crew on a scheduled flight to Dubai.

The ship’s tactical commanders made an instantaneous initial assessment: the aircraft was an Iranian F-14 Tomcat fighter diving into an attack profile. Over the next four minutes, as the tension inside the operations room mounted toward panic, the cruiser's sophisticated sensors generated a continuous stream of contradictory data: the aircraft’s transponder was broadcasting a civilian squawk code; its altitude was climbing, not descending; and its airspeed was well below that of a supersonic military attack jet.

Yet the crew did not see the data. Trapped by their initial hypothesis, radar operators shouted that the plane was dropping in altitude and accelerating toward the ship. Every sensor readout was psychologically reinterpreted to confirm the terrifying opening narrative. Captain Will Rogers III authorized the firing of two surface-to-air missiles, blowing the civilian airliner out of the sky and killing all 290 people on board.

The formal Navy investigation, led by Admiral William Fogarty, uncovered a chilling cognitive phenomenon that military psychologists labeled scenario fulfillment: when operators form a high-stakes first impression, the brain unconsciously filters out all contradictory telemetry, actively hallucinating confirmation from disconfirming facts.

The catastrophe of Flight 655 is an extreme manifestation of a universal psychological law: First impressions are not tentative drafts; they are cognitive anchors that seize control of all subsequent perception.

1. Solomon Asch: The Architecture of the Primacy Effect

In 1946, Gestalt psychologist Solomon Asch published a groundbreaking paper in the Journal of Abnormal and Social Psychology titled "Forming Impressions of Personality." Asch was determined to understand whether the human brain evaluates people by summing up individual traits like numbers on an accounting ledger, or whether perception operates through an organic, holistic Gestalt.

"When a task of impression-formation is given, the individual does not simply register discrete traits one by one. The very first characteristics perceived establish a central direction; they determine the emotional and conceptual context within which all subsequent traits must fit. A trait perceived early in the encounter acts as a dynamic force that alters the meaning of every trait that follows."

Solomon E. Asch, "Forming Impressions of Personality" (Journal of Abnormal and Social Psychology, 1946)
Primary Source

Asch designed a classic experiment, presenting subjects with two lists of descriptive adjectives describing a hypothetical person:

  • Series A: Intelligent — Industrious — Impulsive — Critical — Stubborn — Envious
  • Series B: Envious — Stubborn — Critical — Impulsive — Industrious — Intelligent

When subjects evaluated Person A, they described a courageous, ambitious leader whose "impulsiveness" was an asset indicating passion, and whose "stubbornness" represented integrity and firmness of conviction.

When subjects evaluated Person B, they described a petty, dangerous, frustrated sociopath. His "intelligence" was interpreted not as a virtue, but as a malicious weapon: his brilliance made his envy and stubbornness vastly more dangerous.

The descriptive words were mathematically identical. But the primacy effect was absolute: the opening traits established the interpretive frame, compelling the brain to twist the remaining words until they conformed to the opening verdict.

2. Alexander Todorov: The 100-Millisecond Face Machine

How fast does this anchoring process occur? In modern psychology, researchers assumed for decades that forming a first impression required several minutes of observed behavior, conversation, and posture.

In his landmark research at Princeton University, neuroscientist Alexander Todorov demonstrated that the human face-processing system makes sweeping judgments about competence, trustworthiness, aggressiveness, and warmth in less than 100 milliseconds—a literal fraction of a heartbeat.

"We form immediate, unshakeable impressions of a person’s character within a tenth of a second of seeing their face. These rapid facial inferences are completely automatic; they operate below conscious awareness. ... Astonishingly, ratings of competence based on a one-second glance at candidates’ facial photographs predict the outcomes of real gubernatorial and congressional elections with nearly 70% accuracy. Yet empirical testing demonstrates that these facial cues have zero correlation with actual governance performance or moral character."

Alexander Todorov, Face-Value: The Irresistible Influence of First Impressions (Princeton University Press, 2017)
Primary Source

Todorov's findings reveal a terrifying evolutionary mismatch. In the ancestral Pleistocene savannah, rapid facial assessment evolved to answer two existential questions: "Does this stranger intend to hurt me?" and "Do they have the physical capacity to carry out that intent?"

Today, however, we use this primitive, hyper-fast machinery to make complex social judgments: hiring chief executives, selecting judges, electing presidents, and awarding research grants. We mistake wide cheekbones, a strong jawline, and symmetrical bone structure for strategic vision, fiscal prudence, and moral courage.

Historical Case Study

The Warren Harding Error: The Price of Presidential Looks

In 1899, a prominent Ohio political strategist named Harry Daugherty saw Warren Gamaliel Harding in a hotel lobby. Harding was tall, broad-shouldered, possessed silver-gray hair, a deep baritone voice, and an unshakeable aura of patriarchal dignity. Daugherty had an instantaneous epiphany: "That man looks like a president."

Daugherty engineered Harding’s political rise, culminating in his landslide election to the presidency in 1920. Yet as historians now document, Harding was an intellectual lightweight with no interest in governance, who confessed to friends: "I am not fit for this office and should never have been here."

His administration degenerated into the infamous Teapot Dome bribery scandal, chronic cronyism, and catastrophic corruption, routinely ranked by presidential historians as one of the three worst presidencies in United States history. In Blink, Malcolm Gladwell dubbed this tragic vulnerability the Warren Harding Error: our fatal tendency to allow dignified physical presence to silence all critical scrutiny.

3. Malcolm Gladwell: The Limits of Thin-Slicing

In Blink: The Power of Thinking Without Thinking, Malcolm Gladwell popularized the concept of thin-slicing: the ability of our unconscious mind to find patterns in situations based on narrow windows of experience.

Gladwell showed that thin-slicing can produce miracles of accuracy when exercised by seasoned experts operating within well-calibrated domains—such as art historians who immediately spot a forged Greek kouros statue because an intuitive cold dread hits them before they can articulate why.

However, Gladwell issued a profound warning that popular readers frequently overlook:

"Snap judgments are an enormous part of our human cognitive repertoire, but they are also profoundly vulnerable to corruption. When our rapid unconscious reactions are contaminated by prejudices, social stereotypes, or sensory irrelevancies, thin-slicing fails catastrophically. The tragic shooting of Amadou Diallo by four police officers in the Bronx occurred because an initial, distorted thin-slice misread a harmless immigrant reaching for his wallet as a dangerous armed criminal. If we rely on first impressions without understanding their structural failure modes, we become prisoners of our own biases."

Malcolm Gladwell, Blink: The Power of Thinking Without Thinking (Little, Brown, 2005)
Primary Source

4. Lee Ross and the Fundamental Attribution Error

Why do first impressions hold such unyielding persistence even when contradicted by subsequent behavior? The answer lies in the Fundamental Attribution Error, formalized by Stanford psychologist Lee Ross in 1977.

"When we observe another person's behavior, our immediate, automatic impulse is to attribute that behavior to their enduring disposition, character, or intellect, while systematically underestimating the powerful situational constraints acting upon them. If someone arrives late to a job interview, we immediately conclude: 'They are unorganized and disrespectful.' When we ourselves arrive late, we explain: 'The highway was blocked by an unavoidable tanker fire.'"

Lee Ross & Richard E. Nisbett, The Person and the Situation: Perspectives of Social Psychology (McGraw-Hill, 1991)
Primary Source

Because the initial encounter occurs in a specific, artificial micro-context (e.g., an awkward job interview or a formal networking reception), the candidate’s nervousness, silence, or over-exuberance is entirely a function of the situation. Yet the observer’s brain immediately converts that situational artifact into a permanent character diagnosis: "She is arrogant," or "He is timid."

Once this character label is assigned, confirmation bias takes over: any future confident remark from the person is categorized as "arrogance," and any quiet remark is categorized as "aloofness." The first impression becomes a self-reinforcing perceptual prison.

5. The Reality Check: Unstructured Interviews Are Pure Theater

In modern corporate life, the single most common business decision—hiring—is still dominated by the traditional 45-minute unstructured interview. Research by industrial psychologist Jason Dana at Yale University demonstrates that unstructured interviews have virtually zero predictive validity for future job performance:

Selection Methodology Predictive Validity ($r$) Primary Vulnerability
Work-Sample Tests High ($r \approx 0.54$) Costly to design and evaluate; requires objective grading.
Structured Behavioral Rubrics Moderate-High ($r \approx 0.51$) Requires strict standardization and interviewer training.
Unstructured Casual Interviews Near Zero ($r \approx 0.14$) Completely hijacked by the first 90 seconds of small talk and physical charm.

Interviewers make up their minds within the first two minutes based on handshake firmness, eye contact, and conversational warmth. They spend the remaining 43 minutes looking for evidence to justify the verdict their gut already delivered.

6. Practical Application: Epistemic De-Anchoring

1. Treat First Impressions as Hypotheses, Not Verdicts

Whenever you form an immediate positive or negative impression of someone, mentally classify it as a weak working hypothesis. Say to yourself: "My brain has just generated a preliminary story about this person based on 60 seconds of superficial data. Now, what evidence would prove this story false?"

2. Standardize Evaluation Before Meeting

In hiring or partner selection, write down your evaluation criteria, specific situational questions, and scoring rubric before you ever lay eyes on the candidate. Score each answer immediately against an objective anchor before moving to the next question. This prevents an early favorable impression from pulling up scores on unrelated questions.

3. The 48-Hour De-Anchoring Rule

Never make a binding contractual or hiring commitment immediately following a charismatic in-person pitch or emotional presentation. Wait 48 hours for the visceral neurochemical rush of mirror-neuron rapport to subside. Review the candidate or vendor purely on the cold, dispassionate facts of their track record and work product.

A QUESTION TO CARRY

Who in your life did you initially misjudge—either by over-trusting someone whose charm disguised incompetence, or by undervaluing someone whose awkwardness hid extraordinary capability—and what was the specific perceptual trap that fooled you?
