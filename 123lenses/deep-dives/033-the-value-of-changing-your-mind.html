The value of changing your mind

Revising a belief in response to disconfirming evidence is the definitive hallmark of intelligence, not a sign of moral weakness. Consistency is a virtue only when the underlying reasons remain sound.

📖 11-Minute Deep Read (2,118 words) ⚡ Short on time? Switch to the 2-minute field note summary →

In January 1895, a young Jewish artillery officer in the French Army named Alfred Dreyfus was marched onto the parade grounds of the École Militaire in Paris before a crowd of thousands chanting anti-Semitic slurs. In a ritual of public degradation, an executioner ripped the golden braids from Dreyfus's uniform, snapped his ceremonial sword across his knee, and shouted that he was a traitor to the French Republic. Dreyfus was sentenced to solitary life imprisonment on Devil's Island, a barren, disease-ridden penal rock off the coast of French Guiana.

The conviction rested on a torn memorandum recovered from the wastebasket of the German military attaché in Paris, containing French artillery secrets. French military intelligence handwriting experts claimed the script matched Dreyfus’s hand. The case was declared closed; the honour of the French High Command was preserved.

Six months later, the French General Staff appointed a brilliant 41-year-old lieutenant colonel named Georges Picquart as the new head of the military counter-espionage bureau. Picquart was a devout French patriot, an ambitious career officer, and like most members of his aristocratic military caste, held unexamined, conventional anti-Semitic prejudices. He held no personal sympathy for Dreyfus.

While investigating continued leaks to Germany in 1896, Picquart intercepted a special-delivery letter from another French officer, Major Ferdinand Walsin Esterhazy. When Picquart compared Esterhazy’s handwriting to the infamous memo that had sent Dreyfus to Devil's Island, his breath caught: the handwriting was identical in every loop, slant, and flourish. Dreyfus was completely innocent.

Picquart carried the evidence to his superiors in the High Command, expecting them to correct the terrible miscarriage of justice. Instead, his commanding generals were appalled. They ordered Picquart to bury the evidence and stay silent: to admit that the army had framed and convicted an innocent Jewish officer would destroy the public prestige of the military establishment. When Picquart refused, his superiors transferred him to dangerous front-line combat zones in Tunisia, forged documents to frame him, and eventually had him arrested and imprisoned for a year.

Yet Picquart stood unyielding. He had smuggle the evidence to Émile Zola and Georges Clemenceau, sparking the publication of Zola's historic manifesto "J'Accuse...!". Dreyfus was eventually exonerated, and Picquart was restored to the army with honors, later serving as Minister of War.

In her foundational 2021 work The Scout Mindset, cognitive theorist Julia Galef identified Picquart as the quintessential embodiment of a rare, transformative epistemic virtue: The willingness to change your mind when reality contradicts your deepest preconceptions.

1. Julia Galef: The Soldier vs. The Scout

Galef developed a powerful metaphor that divides human thinking into two opposing psychological stances:

"Soldier mindset is defensive. When you're in soldier mindset, reasoning is like defensive combat: ideas are either allies to be defended or enemies to be demolished. When you encounter contrary evidence, your immediate impulse is: 'Must I believe this?' You search for any loophole to reject it. When you encounter supportive evidence, your question is: 'Can I believe this?' ... Scout mindset, by contrast, is rooted in curiosity. A scout's job is not to defend territory; a scout’s job is to go out, survey the terrain, and come back with an accurate map of reality. A scout wants to know what's really there, even if it's unpleasant or inconvenient."

Julia Galef, The Scout Mindset: Why Some People See Things Clearly and Others Don't (Portfolio / Penguin, 2021)
Primary Source

The French General Staff operated entirely in Soldier Mindset. They viewed evidence through the lens of tribal warfare: preserving the army's reputation was their sacred fortress; Dreyfus’s innocence was an enemy grenade to be deflected.

Picquart operated in Scout Mindset. Despite his personal prejudices and career vulnerability, he cared more about whether his map of reality was accurate than whether his tribe was comfortable. He understood that a map that refuses to show an iceberg does not protect the ship from sinking.

2. Adam Grant: Preachers, Prosecutors, Politicians, and Scientists

In Think Again: The Power of Knowing What You Don't Know, Wharton organizational psychologist Adam Grant demonstrated that when people argue and deliberate, they constantly slip into three counter-productive professional identities:

  • The Preacher: When our sacred beliefs are in jeopardy, we deliver sermons to protect and promote our ideals. We seek converts, not truth.
  • The Prosecutor: When we spot flaws in other people's reasoning, we marshall evidence to prove them wrong and win the debate. We seek a guilty verdict.
  • The Politician: When we want to win approval from our peers or bosses, we campaign and lobby for consensus, adjusting our views to whatever is popular.

Grant argues that true wisdom requires adopting a fourth identity: The Scientist.

"Thinking like a scientist doesn't mean you need to work in a laboratory or run clinical trials. It means favoring humility over pride, doubt over certainty, and curiosity over closure. When you think like a scientist, you treat your beliefs as hypotheses to be tested with data, rather than identities to be defended with rhetoric. You look for reasons why you might be wrong, not just reasons why you must be right. The mark of lifelong intelligence is not how much you know; it is how quickly you can unlearn and rethink."

Adam Grant, Think Again: The Power of Knowing What You Don't Know (Viking, 2021)
Primary Source
Scientific History Case Study

Joseph Lister and the Antisepsis Revolution

In the 1860s, Scottish surgeon Joseph Lister read Louis Pasteur’s papers on microscopic germ fermentation. At the time, hospital gangrene was killing nearly half of all surgical amputees in British hospitals; surgical doctrine insisted that wound rot was caused by "bad air" (miasma) and chemical oxidation.

Unlike the Viennese professors who had mocked Semmelweis, Lister operated as a pure scientist. He hypothesized that microscopic airborne microbes were infecting open flesh. He began soaking surgical dressings in carbolic acid, spraying the operating theater, and sterilizing instruments. The postoperative mortality rate in his Glasgow ward plummeted from 45% to under 12%.

When skeptical colleagues in London challenged his technique, Lister did not become dogmatic or defensive. He published meticulous, transparent case series, continually modified his chemical concentrations when carbolic acid irritated tissue, and publicly invited his harshest critics to inspect his wards. Because Lister viewed his own procedures as testable hypotheses rather than sacred dogma, antiseptic surgery transformed modern medicine, saving millions of human lives.

3. Philip Tetlock: Belief Update Velocity in Superforecasters

In modern society, changing one's mind is often branded by politicians and media commentators as "flip-flopping"—a sign of spineless weakness or moral inconsistency. In cognitive science, the exact opposite is true.

In his landmark twenty-year Good Judgment Project, psychologist Philip E. Tetlock analyzed what distinguished "Superforecasters"—amateurs who consistently beat CIA intelligence analysts and Wall Street macroeconomic strategists:

"The strongest predictor of forecasting accuracy is not raw IQ, mathematical sophistication, or specialized domain expertise. It is belief update velocity: how frequently and incrementally a forecaster updates their probabilities in response to new evidence. ... While stubborn dogmatists hold their ground until an undeniable crisis forces an abrupt, traumatic reversal, superforecasters practice Bayesian updating: they nudge their confidence from 60% to 65%, or down to 52%, constantly fine-tuning their maps as the facts evolve."

Philip E. Tetlock & Dan Gardner, Superforecasting: The Art and Science of Prediction (Crown, 2015)
Primary Source

Tetlock invokes the famous quip famously attributed to British economist John Maynard Keynes: "When the facts change, I change my mind. What do you do, sir?"

Stubborn consistency is not a virtue when the world is dynamic and complex. To hold the exact same geopolitical, financial, or organizational beliefs in 2026 that you held in 2016 is not proof of timeless wisdom; it is proof of intellectual stagnation.

4. Karl Popper: Falsification as the Demarcation of Truth

In the philosophy of science, Sir Karl Popper established the fundamental boundary separating genuine knowledge from pseudo-scientific dogma in his 1963 classic Conjectures and Refutations:

"The criterion of the scientific status of a theory is its falsifiability, or refutability, or testability. ... A theory which is not refutable by any conceivable event is non-scientific. Irrefutability is not a virtue of a theory (as people often think) but a vice. Every genuine test of a theory is an attempt to falsify it, to refute it."

Karl Popper, Conjectures and Refutations: The Growth of Scientific Knowledge (Routledge, 1963)
Primary Source

Popper showed that any belief system that can explain everything—such as astrology, rigid Freudian psychoanalysis, or dogmatic conspiracy theories—actually explains nothing. If every piece of news can be twisted into proof of your theory, your theory is an unfalsifiable religious faith.

To hold an intellectually honest belief, you must always be capable of answering a single devastating question: "What specific, observable empirical finding would convince you that your belief is wrong?" If your answer is "nothing could ever convince me," you are no longer reasoning; you are practicing theology.

5. The Reality Check: When Is Consistency Essential?

To avoid intellectual chaos, we must distinguish between two realms:

Domain Role of Consistency Appropriate Stance
Ethical Values & Promises High Consistency Vital Integrity, promise-keeping, basic human rights, honesty.
Empirical Hypotheses & Strategies High Flexibility Vital Business models, investment theses, technical architectures, medical treatments.

Changing your ethical values to match whatever is convenient or profitable is moral opportunism. Changing your strategic hypotheses when the data proves they are failing is the essence of wisdom. Confusing moral integrity with strategic stubbornness is how great empires and Fortune 500 corporations collapse.

6. Practical Application: Epistemic Unlearning Protocols

1. Decouple Your Beliefs from Your Identity

Paul Graham, co-founder of Y Combinator, articulated the master rule: "Keep your identity small." The moment you label yourself an "Austrian economist," an "Agile purist," or a "Bitcoin maximalist," every attack on that philosophy becomes an existential attack on your self-worth. Define yourself by your values (integrity, curiosity, rigor), not by your temporary technical hypotheses.

2. The "What Would Change My Mind?" Drill

Whenever you find yourself locked in a fierce political, corporate, or personal argument, pause and ask the other person with genuine gentleness: "What piece of evidence would cause you to change your mind on this?" Then turn the mirror on yourself: "What evidence would cause me to change mine?" If neither person can identify a falsification condition, stop arguing; you are not having a discussion, you are performing a ritual.

3. Celebrate the "Aha!" Discovery of Error

Reframe the emotional experience of being proven wrong. When someone demonstrates that your financial model was flawed or your timeline was unrealistic, do not experience shame. Train your brain to feel relief: "Thank goodness I discovered that mistake today when it only cost me ten minutes of pride, rather than discovering it next year when it would have cost me $2 million and my reputation."

A QUESTION TO CARRY

What is the most significant belief you have changed your mind about in the last five years—and what was the specific piece of disconfirming evidence that finally forced you to update your map?
