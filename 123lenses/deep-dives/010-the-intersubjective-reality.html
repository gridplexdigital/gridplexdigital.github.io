The Intersubjective Reality

Reality is not divided simply into the objective (trees, rocks, gravity) and the subjective (personal pain, private dreams). The defining superpower of humanity is the intersubjective realm: shared webs of belief—money, corporations, nations, human rights—that exist exclusively within the mutual imagination of millions of human minds.

At 7:32 PM on December 25, 1991, in the grand Kremlin palace in Moscow, Soviet President Mikhail Gorbachev sat before television cameras and delivered a quiet, ten-minute speech announcing his resignation as head of the Union of Soviet Socialist Republics. Minutes later, atop the Kremlin dome, the red hammer-and-sickle Soviet banner was lowered into the snowy winter night, and the tricolor flag of the Russian Federation was raised in its place. At that exact moment, the Soviet Union—the colossal Eurasian superpower that commanded 30,000 thermonuclear warheads, four million active soldiers, a globe-spanning KGB intelligence network, and one-sixth of the dry land surface of the earth—simply ceased to exist. Yet not a single nuclear warhead had detonated; no foreign army had breached its borders; its factories were still standing; its soil was intact; and its 290 million citizens were still breathing the same air. If a visiting alien biologist had landed in Siberia that evening, the physical biosphere would have appeared completely unchanged. What, then, had died? What had vanished into thin air? The answer is that the Soviet Union was never an objective physical object made of atoms, like a mountain or a river; nor was it a private subjective dream inside Mikhail Gorbachev’s solitary skull. The Soviet Union was an Intersubjective Reality—a colossal, shared web of collective belief that existed exclusively within the mutual imagination of hundreds of millions of human minds. When the shared belief dissolved, the superpower evaporated overnight. Human civilization dominates planet Earth not because we are stronger than lions or smarter than computers, but because we are the only species on earth capable of organizing millions of strangers around shared, intersubjective fictions.

1. Yuval Noah Harari: The Three Layers of Reality

In his epochal 2014 masterpiece, Sapiens: A Brief History of Humankind, historian Yuval Noah Harari formulated the foundational ontological breakthrough that unlocks human history: the division of human experience into three distinct realms:

  • The Objective Realm: Physical phenomena that exist independently of human consciousness, beliefs, or desires. Radioactivity, mountains, DNA, viruses, and the law of universal gravitation are objective. If every human being on earth died tomorrow, Mount Everest would still rise 8,848 meters into the sky, and gravity would still pull rocks to the ground. Objective reality does not care what you believe.
  • The Subjective Realm: Private, internal phenomenological experiences that exist exclusively within the consciousness of an individual subject. My toothache, your feeling of jealousy, the joy of tasting coffee, or a private nightmare are subjective. If the individual dies or recovers, the subjective reality vanishes completely.
  • The Intersubjective Realm: Phenomena that exist within the shared communication network connecting the subjective consciousness of many individuals. If a single person stops believing in an intersubjective entity, nothing changes; but if millions of people simultaneously stop believing in it, the entity collapses into non-existence.
"The intersubjective is something that exists within the communication network linking the subjective consciousness of many individuals. If a single individual changes his beliefs, or even dies, it is of little importance. However, if most individuals in the network die or change their beliefs, the intersubjective phenomenon will change or disappear... Most of the history of the world revolves around this intersubjective web: money, nations, corporations, human rights, and gods."
— Yuval Noah Harari, Sapiens: A Brief History of Humankind (2014)

2. The Legend of Peugeot S.A.: The Legal Fiction

To illustrate the magical power of the intersubjective reality, Harari offered the unforgettable modern case study of the French automobile manufacturer Peugeot.

In the 1890s, Armand Peugeot inherited a modest family manufacturing shop in eastern France that made hand tools and bicycles. In 1896, Armand decided to build automobiles and established a brand-new entity: Société Anonyme des Automobiles Peugeot. Today, Peugeot is one of the largest automakers in the world, employing over 200,000 workers and generating tens of billions of euros in annual revenue.

Harari asks a simple, radical question: In what sense does Peugeot exist?

The Ontological Dissection of Peugeot

  • Is Peugeot its physical vehicles? No. Even if every single Peugeot car on the roads today were crushed in a giant compactor, Peugeot S.A. would not cease to exist. It would borrow money, manufacture new cars, and continue trading.
  • Is Peugeot its factories, machines, and showrooms? No. If a catastrophic hurricane leveled every assembly plant and office building owned by the company, Peugeot could file an insurance claim, contract new suppliers, and rebuild its operations.
  • Is Peugeot its employees, managers, or shareholders? No. If all 200,000 workers, along with the CEO and the board of directors, were killed in a tragic simultaneous accident, the company's legal executor would hire new staff, appoint a new board, and Peugeot would march on.

Peugeot is an incorporeal ghost—a legal fiction. It was brought into existence by a French notary signing an official parchment according to the legal statutes governing limited liability corporations passed by the French parliament. It belongs to a species of collective fictions that lawyers call fictio juris.

Yet this imaginary ghost holds bank accounts, owns real estate, hires attorneys, pays taxes, and sues other corporations in court! Why? Because the French legal system, the banking system, French police officers, and international consumers all participate in the exact same intersubjective agreement. If the French republic repealed its corporate code tomorrow, Peugeot would vanish as completely as the morning mist.

3. The Yap Island Rai Stones: The Ocean-Floor Ledger

The most pure, visceral historical demonstration of intersubjective reality in action occurred in the remote western Pacific archipelago of Yap in Micronesia.

For centuries, the Yapese people did not use gold, silver, or paper bank notes as currency. They used Rai stones: massive, circular limestone discs quarried on the distant island of Palau and transported across hundreds of miles of open ocean on fragile wooden outrigger canoes. The largest Rai stones measured twelve feet in diameter and weighed over four metric tons.

Because moving a four-ton stone across the island to pay for a pig or a marriage dowry was physically impossible, the stones were placed along roadsides or outside village meeting houses and left permanently motionless.

In 1903, American anthropologist William Henry Furness III traveled to Yap and documented an extraordinary phenomenon. The islanders possessed a wealthy family whose fortune was recognized across the archipelago. Yet Furness observed that the family had no physical Rai stones outside their home.

When Furness inquired, the island elders explained that two generations earlier, the family’s ancestors had quarried an exceptionally large, magnificent Rai stone in Palau. While sailing home in an outrigger canoe, a furious typhoon struck. To prevent the canoe from capsizing, the sailors were forced to cut the ropes, and the colossal stone plunged into the pitch-black depths of the Pacific Ocean.

When the crew returned home, they testified to the chief and the village elders that the stone had been quarried, that it was of superb quality, and that its sinking was an act of nature. The village elders held a council and made a formal declaration: the accident did not impair the stone's value. The stone remained legitimate money; it simply happened to rest on the ocean floor.

For the subsequent eighty years, that submerged stone was traded across Yap. When the family purchased land or settled a political dispute, the title to a stone lying five hundred feet beneath the waves was transferred from one party to another. Nobody had seen the stone for eighty years, yet everyone accepted its value!

The Yapese were not primitive fools; they were sophisticated financial innovators who understood what modern economists call a distributed ledger. Money is not gold; money is not paper; money is not a stone. Money is an intersubjective system of mutual trust recorded in human memory.

4. John Searle: Institutional Facts vs. Brute Facts

In his foundational 1995 philosophical treatise, The Construction of Social Reality, UC Berkeley philosopher John Searle provided the formal logical mechanics that govern the intersubjective world.

Searle divided all facts in the universe into two categories:

  • Brute Facts: Facts that require no human institution or belief to be true. (e.g., "Hydrogen has one proton," "This rock weighs twelve kilograms," "Water freezes at zero degrees Celsius.") Brute facts exist in the objective realm.
  • Institutional Facts: Facts that exist only because of human agreement, convention, and collective intentionality. (e.g., "This green piece of paper is a twenty-dollar bill," "Barack Obama was President of the United States," "These two people are legally married," "This boundary line is the border of Canada.")

Searle codified the universal mathematical formula that transforms brute physical objects into institutional realities:

X counts as Y in Context C

Consider a physical dollar bill:

  • $X$ is the brute physical object: a rectangular piece of cotton-linen paper measuring 6.14 by 2.61 inches, coated in green ink.
  • $Y$ is the institutional status: legal tender for all debts, public and private.
  • $C$ is the social context: the United States financial and legal system established by the Federal Reserve Act.

The moment the context $C$ collapses—as occurred in Weimar Germany in 1923, Zimbabwe in 2008, or the Confederacy in 1865—the status $Y$ evaporates, and the object instantly reverts back to its brute physical reality $X$: useless, dirty waste paper that people use to light fires.

5. The Fragility of the Imagined Order: Why Force Is Never Enough

Because intersubjective realities exist exclusively within human minds, they are perpetually haunted by a fatal vulnerability: if people stop believing in them, they dissolve.

Dictators and authoritarian regimes mistakenly believe that an intersubjective order can be sustained indefinitely through raw military force, bayonets, and secret police. But as French statesman Talleyrand famously remarked to Napoleon: "You can do anything with bayonets, Sire, except sit on them."

Why? Because who commands the soldiers who hold the bayonets? If a general orders his soldiers to fire on a crowd of protesting citizens, why do the soldiers obey? The soldiers do not obey because the general is physically capable of fighting ten thousand soldiers in hand-to-hand combat; the soldiers obey because they believe in the intersubjective myth of the military chain of command, the sacred oath to the constitution, or the divine authority of the Emperor.

If the soldiers simultaneously stop believing in that myth—as the Russian Imperial Army did in February 1917, or as the East German border guards did on the night of November 9, 1989—the general’s commands become empty vocal vibrations in the cold air. An army cannot operate without shared myths. Therefore, every ruling order spends vast sums of money not on weapons, but on indoctrination, national anthems, public school curricula, flags, and cultural rituals to continuously reinforce the intersubjective faith.

6. Core Heuristics for Navigating the Intersubjective Landscape

1. The "Alien Biologist" Test (Strip the Intersubjective Fiction)

Whenever you find yourself stressed, intimidated, or paralyzed by a social institution, title, or bureaucratic barrier, perform the Alien Biologist Thought Experiment: look at the situation strictly through the eyes of an extraterrestrial zoologist observing physical primates. Strip away the institutional labels ($Y$): instead of seeing a "Supreme Court Justice wearing sacred black robes dispensing justice," see an aging primate wearing dyed fabric sitting on elevated wood, emitting sound waves that other primates obey because of shared conditioning. This mental filter strips false reverence and allows you to analyze institutional mechanics with clinical objectivity.

2. Never Mistake the Metaphor for the Molecule

Distinguish rigorously between brute physical constraints and intersubjective rules. Climate change, antibiotic resistance, and thermodynamic energy limits are brute physical realities: they will crush human civilization regardless of what we believe. Fiscal debt ceilings, intellectual property patents, and sovereign national borders are intersubjective rules: humans created them, and humans can rewrite them overnight. Never allow an imaginary institutional rule to prevent you from solving a physical existential crisis.

3. Build Intersubjective Coalitions to Scale Change

If you want to launch a startup, pass a law, or transform a company culture, recognize that you cannot succeed purely through individual technical brilliance. You must become a master storyteller: you must weave an intersubjective narrative (a mission, a brand, a manifesto) that multiple independent minds can inhabit simultaneously. People will not march into danger for a spreadsheet; they will march into danger for a shared story.

4. Audit Your Personal Intersubjective Indoctrination

Identify the shared fictions that govern your own life: money, professional prestige, corporate job titles, national identity. Recognize that while these fictions are necessary tools for social coordination, they are tools, not gods. Never sacrifice your physical health, your family intimacy, or your ethical integrity on the altar of an intersubjective phantom.

7. Self-Diagnostic Checklist: Auditing Your Reality Layers

  • Can I clearly distinguish which aspects of my daily worries are rooted in brute physical reality (illness, hunger, weather) versus intersubjective fictions (credit scores, job titles, social status)?
  • If the corporation or institution I work for dissolved overnight, how much of my personal identity and self-worth would survive the collapse?
  • Am I treating human-created economic or legal rules as if they were unalterable laws of physics?
  • What shared stories, values, and rituals hold my immediate team or family together? Are those stories healthy and life-giving, or toxic and exhausting?
  • When I attempt to persuade someone, do I rely exclusively on cold data, or do I invite them into a compelling shared narrative?
