Existentialism and Responsibility

There is no pre-written cosmic script, no innate human nature to blame, and no authority that can make our choices for us. We are radically, dizzyingly free—and with that absolute freedom comes the total, inescapable weight of personal responsibility.

📖 11-Minute Deep Read (2,151 words) ⚡ Short on time? Switch to the 2-minute field note summary →

In the autumn of 1945, in the freshly liberated city of Paris, French philosopher Jean-Paul Sartre stepped onto the podium at the Club Maintenant to deliver a public lecture that would define an intellectual generation. World War II had just concluded: the continent of Europe was in ruins, concentration camps had exposed the horrific capacity of human beings to murder millions in industrialized slaughter, and traditional religious and bourgeois institutions had lost all moral credibility. Millions of young people were paralyzed by existential disorientation: if God does not exist, and civilization’s comforting grand narratives of inevitable progress are illusions, what is the basis for human action? Sartre’s lecture—published as Existentialism Is a Humanism—ignited a cultural wildfire. The crowd was so vast that people were crushed against doors, furniture was broken, and women fainted in the aisles. Sartre did not offer his audience comforting spiritual anesthesia or easy moral rules. Instead, he presented a philosophy of terrifying, uncompromising moral gravity. He declared that human beings are "condemned to be free." We did not create ourselves, yet once thrust into the world, we are entirely responsible for every choice we make, every value we endorse, and the kind of person we become. Existentialism was not an excuse for despair; it was a thunderous call to adult moral agency.

1. Existence Precedes Essence: The Core Formula

In traditional Western philosophy and theology, from Plato and Aristotle through medieval Christianity, it was taken as self-evident that essence precedes existence.

Sartre illustrated this through the metaphor of a paper knife (a letter opener). Before a physical paper knife is manufactured in a factory, its "essence"—its concept, purpose, material blueprint, and function—already exists in the mind of the craftsman. The artisan knows that the tool must be sharp enough to slice paper and comfortable to hold. The knife’s essence dictates its existence.

For centuries, religious doctrines applied this same manufacturing model to human beings: God was the divine artisan who created human beings according to a predetermined divine essence and moral purpose.

Sartre turned this entire metaphysical hierarchy upside down. In an atheistic or secular universe:

Existence Precedes Essence

"Man first of all exists, encounters himself, surges up in the world—and defines himself afterwards... There is no human nature, because there is no God to have a conception of it. Man simply is. Not that he is simply what he conceives himself to be, but he is what he wills himself to be."

— Jean-Paul Sartre, Existentialism Is a Humanism (1946)

You are not born a coward, a hero, a genius, or a criminal; you possess no immutable, pre-determined "core" that dictates your destiny. You first appear in the world as an unwritten blank slate—pure possibility. It is exclusively through your physical actions, your commitments, and your daily choices that you carve out your essence. You are nothing other than the sum of your deeds.

2. Bad Faith (Mauvaise Foi): The Lie of Having No Choice

If human beings are entirely free to define themselves, why do so many people spend their entire lives feeling trapped, powerless, and miserable?

Sartre’s profound psychological diagnosis was Bad Faith (mauvaise foi). Bad faith is the seductive, self-deceiving lie we tell ourselves to escape the dizzying anxiety of radical freedom. When the burden of responsibility feels too heavy, we pretend to be inanimate objects—things without agency—hiding behind social scripts, corporate roles, and biological determinism.

In Being and Nothingness (1943), Sartre offered his celebrated, iconic vignette: The Parisian Café Waiter:

  • Observe the waiter in the café: his movements are a little too precise, his walk a little too mechanical, his balance of the tray a little too theatrical, his voice a little too eager to please.
  • He is playing a role. He is imitating what society expects a "waiter" to be. He treats his social occupation as an immutable physical property—like ink being black or water being liquid.
  • Why does he perform this pantomime? Because if he acknowledges that he is radically free, he must confront the terrifying realization that at any moment, he could walk out of the café, board a ship to Argentina, or become a revolutionary. Hiding behind the uniform of "the waiter" gives him psychological safety.

Bad faith manifests every day in modern excuses:

  • "I had no choice; my boss made me do it." (You chose to obey your boss because you valued your salary more than your integrity).
  • "That’s just the way I am; I was born with a fiery temper." (You chose to scream because managing your temper required difficult emotional exertion).
  • "I am trapped in this marriage/career by duty." (You are choosing to stay every single morning because the disruption of leaving feels more costly).

To the existentialist, claiming "I had no choice" is the supreme moral cowardice. You always have a choice—even if the only choice left is how you face your execution.

3. Sartre’s Student: The Agony of Unanchored Choice

During the dark days of the Nazi occupation of France, a young university student came to Sartre’s apartment seeking desperate moral counsel.

The student faced a harrowing existential dilemma:

  • His older brother had been killed in the German offensive of 1940, leaving his elderly mother devastated. The student was her only remaining companion; she lived exclusively through him. If he abandoned her, her heart would break and she might die of grief.
  • Simultaneously, the student burned with patriotic and moral outrage to avenge his brother and liberate his country. He wanted to cross the Pyrenees mountains on foot, escape through Spain, travel to England, and join General Charles de Gaulle’s Free French forces to fight fascism.

The student asked Sartre: "Which path is the morally right one? Should I stay with my mother or go to England to fight?"

Sartre pointed out that no established ethical framework could resolve the dilemma:

  • Christian doctrine says: "Love your neighbor and honor your mother." But who is the neighbor? The mother who needs immediate care, or the millions of French citizens suffering under Nazi occupation?
  • Kantian deontology says: "Never treat human beings as mere means." If he joins the army, he treats his mother as a means; if he stays with his mother, he treats the soldiers dying on the front line as a means.

Sartre told the student: "You are free, therefore choose—that is to say, invent."

There are no cosmic guideposts painted on the road of life. No priest, no philosopher, and no moral formula can make the choice for you. In deciding which path to take, the student was not uncovering a pre-existing moral fact; he was actively inventing his own values, and he would have to carry the total, un-transferable responsibility of that choice for the rest of his life.

4. Empirical and Historical Case Studies in Responsibility

Case A: Adolf Eichmann and the Ultimate Bureaucratic Bad Faith

In 1961, inside a bulletproof glass booth in a courtroom in Jerusalem, former Nazi SS-Obersturmbannführer Adolf Eichmann stood trial for his central role in organizing the logistics of the Holocaust—coordinating the deportation of millions of European Jews to the extermination camps of Auschwitz, Treblinka, and Belzec.

Philosopher Hannah Arendt covered the trial for The New Yorker, documenting her observations in Eichmann in Jerusalem: A Report on the Banality of Evil. The world expected a monstrous, bloodthirsty demon. Instead, Arendt observed a terrifyingly ordinary, mediocre, bureaucrat who spoke entirely in administrative clichés.

Eichmann’s universal defense was the ultimate manifestation of lethal Bad Faith: "I was merely a transportation administrator following orders." He argued that he held no personal hatred for Jewish people; he had simply fulfilled the bureaucratic duties of his desk, obeying the legal commands of his sovereign superiors (the Führerprinzip).

The judges in Jerusalem and the moral verdict of history rejected Eichmann’s bad faith: human beings can never surrender their moral agency to an institutional title or a chain of command. In following orders to transport innocent people to death camps, Eichmann was actively choosing to cooperate with industrialized murder every single day.

Case B: The Corporate "Wells Fargo" Cross-Selling Scandal (2016)

Between 2011 and 2016, thousands of retail branch employees at Wells Fargo bank secretly created over 3.5 million unauthorized bank accounts, debit cards, and credit lines in customers' names without their knowledge or consent, forging signatures and racking up fraudulent fees.

When the scandal broke, the bank’s executive leadership and branch managers engaged in classic institutional bad faith. Senior executives claimed they were unaware of the fraud, blaming "rogue low-level employees." Branch workers, in turn, pleaded: "We had no choice! Management imposed impossible sales quotas of eight products per customer ('Eight Is Great'); if we missed the quota, we were publicly humiliated and fired!"

Existentialism exposes the fraud of this defense. While the corporate pressure was undeniably brutal and corrupt, every individual employee who forged a customer’s signature faced a choice: forfeit their integrity to keep their bonus, or blow the whistle and risk their job. Tens of thousands of workers chose fraud; a principled minority resigned and preserved their character. Blaming the "system" is an attempt to flee from personal moral responsibility.

5. Actionable Heuristics for Practicing Existential Responsibility

  1. The Language Audit: Eradicate "I Have No Choice": Pay rigorous attention to your vocabulary. Strike the phrases "I have to," "I am forced to," and "I have no choice" from your speech. Replace them with radical existential honesty: "I am choosing to do X because I am unwilling to accept the consequences of doing Y." This simple linguistic shift re-anchors your moral agency.
  2. The "Universal Legislator" Test: Whenever you make a difficult choice—cutting an ethical corner, speaking truth to power, or breaking a commitment—remember Sartre's principle of universal responsibility: in choosing for yourself, you are fashioning an image of what a human being ought to be. Ask yourself: "If every person on Earth acted exactly as I am acting right now, what kind of world would we create?"
  3. Reject the Myth of the "Unexpressed Genius": Never comfort yourself with the thought of what you "could have done" if circumstances had been different. You are not your unexpressed intentions, your secret daydreams, or your potential. You are strictly your actual, physical outputs. If you want to be a writer, write; if you want to be an entrepreneur, launch; if you want to be loving, love. Deeds alone define essence.
  4. Confront Radical Anguish with Lucid Action: When faced with major life decisions, do not search desperately for signs, omens, or third-party validation to make the choice for you. Recognize that anxiety (anguish) is the natural dizziness of freedom. Make your choice courageously, commit fully to the consequences, and carry the responsibility with dignity.

6. Guided Self-Reflection

Examine your life through the lens of existential agency:

  • Where in my career or personal life am I currently acting in "bad faith"—pretending that I am trapped in a situation where I actually hold freedom?
  • If I were stripped of my corporate title, social roles, and external expectations, what authentic values would guide my actions?
  • Am I blaming my parents, my childhood, or my genetic disposition for shortcomings that are actually the result of my daily choices?
  • What is a courageous choice I have been avoiding out of fear of the responsibility that freedom entails?

Further Reading and Primary Sources

  • Sartre, Jean-Paul. Existentialism Is a Humanism (trans. Carol Macomber, Yale University Press, 2007; orig. 1946). The essential, accessible manifesto on freedom and moral choice.
  • Sartre, Jean-Paul. Being and Nothingness: An Essay on Phenomenological Ontology (trans. Sarah Richmond, Routledge, 2018; orig. 1943). The foundational philosophical treatise on bad faith and human consciousness.
  • Camus, Albert. The Myth of Sisyphus and Other Essays (trans. Justin O'Brien, Vintage Books, 1991; orig. 1942). The classic essay on the Absurd, revolt, and living passionately without appeal.
  • Beauvoir, Simone de. The Ethics of Ambiguity (trans. Bernard Frechtman, Citadel Press, 1948). A profound feminist and existentialist exploration of action and political responsibility.
  • Arendt, Hannah. Eichmann in Jerusalem: A Report on the Banality of Evil (Viking Press, 1963). The indispensable historical analysis of administrative thoughtlessness and moral abdication.
