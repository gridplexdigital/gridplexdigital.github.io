The Danger of Human Irrelevance in the Automated Age

The twentieth century was defined by ideological struggles against economic exploitation. In the twenty-first century, the far more terrifying existential threat is not exploitation, but obsolescence: the creation of a massive, economically useless class of humans who are no longer needed for labor, war, or consumption.

In 1900, on the bustling cobblestone streets and fertile agricultural fields of the United States, there was an indispensable, tireless biological workforce that numbered more than twenty-one million working individuals: the horse. For centuries, horses had been the essential muscular backbone of industrial and agrarian civilization. They hauled freight from docks to warehouses, pulled omnibus streetcars carrying commuters across Manhattan and Chicago, plowed millions of acres of Midwest wheat, and carried cavalry charges into battle. Whenever 19th-century economists and social commentators expressed anxiety about new steam engines or mechanical threshers, agricultural leaders comforted them with an unshakeable economic law: technology creates more jobs than it destroys. When steam locomotives replaced stagecoaches, horses were simply redirected to haul short-distance local delivery drays; the total population of working horses in America reached an all-time peak in 1915. Then arrived the internal combustion engine, the Ford Model T, and the mechanical tractor. In 1983, Nobel laureate economist Wassily Leontief published an unforgettable, chilling analogy that shattered classical economic complacency. Leontief pointed out that when tractors and trucks replaced equine muscle, the horses did not retrain. They did not become auto mechanics; they did not become highway asphalt contractors; and they did not open buggy-whip design studios. The market simply had no further economic use for them. Between 1915 and 1960, the working horse population in the United States plummeted by nearly 90 percent. The horses were sold to slaughterhouses, boiled down for gelatin and glue, or simply not bred. In 21 Lessons for the 21st Century, Yuval Noah Harari delivers the defining political and economic warning of our era: for two hundred years, human workers comforted themselves that unlike horses, human beings possess unique cognitive flexibility, emotional intelligence, and creativity. But as artificial intelligence and autonomous robotics match and exceed human capability across both cognitive and physical domains, humanity faces a prospect far more terrifying than economic exploitation: the creation of a massive, permanent class of economically useless humans.

1. Yuval Noah Harari: Exploitation vs. Irrelevance

Throughout the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, the great political, social, and ideological battles of modern history—Marxism, Socialism, Trade Unionism, and Democratic Capitalism—were fought over a single fundamental struggle: Economic Exploitation.

Industrial capitalists owned the factories, the steam engines, and the coal mines; the working-class proletariat owned only their physical labor. Capitalists ruthlessly exploited workers, demanding fourteen-hour workdays in dangerous conditions for starvation wages.

Yet Harari points out a crucial historical reality: even under the most brutal exploitation, the working class possessed immense, structural political leverage:

  • The factory owner needed human hands to operate the loom; if the workers walked out on strike, production stopped, profits vanished, and the owner went bankrupt.
  • The imperial state needed millions of healthy, literate young men to serve as cannon fodder in mass conscript armies (World War I, World War II) and millions of factory workers to manufacture ammunition.

Because the political and economic elites desperately needed the masses for labor and war, the elites were forced to invest in universal public education, build public hospitals, establish sanitation systems, grant voting rights, and construct the modern welfare state. Mass public health was not pure charity; it was the biological maintenance of the state's most critical asset: human muscle and blood.

In the twenty-first century, this foundation is crumbling:

"In the twentieth century, the working class fought against exploitation, and sought to translate their economic power into political influence. In the twenty-first century, the masses are losing their economic value, and with it, their political bargaining power. It is much worse to be irrelevant than to be exploited."
— Yuval Noah Harari, 21 Lessons for the 21st Century (2018)

2. The Demise of the Mass Conscript Army

To witness the evaporation of human strategic relevance, one need only look at the history of modern warfare.

In 1916, at the Battle of the Somme, the British Army suffered 57,000 casualties in a single morning. Winning a 20th-century war was a contest of raw demographic mass: whichever nation could mobilize, train, feed, and march five million young men into artillery barrages won the conflict. The soldier was an indispensable industrial unit.

Today, modern twenty-first-century warfare has completely decoupled from mass infantry:

The Shift to Autonomous War

  • Autonomous Drone Swarms and Precision Munitions: A single pilot sitting in an air-conditioned trailer in Nevada can operate autonomous Predator drones over the Middle East. Micro-drone swarms coordinated by artificial intelligence can eliminate targets in dense urban areas with surgical precision, requiring zero infantry sweeps.
  • Cyber Warfare and Satellite Interception: A nation's electrical power grid, banking system, and air-traffic control network can be crippled in thirty seconds by a state-sponsored malware script written by ten elite computer scientists in a bunker, accomplishing what previously required an armada of 1,000 B-17 bombers.
  • Special Operations and AI Logistics: Armies are shrinking rapidly in personnel while multiplying exponentially in algorithmic lethality.

When a government no longer requires millions of eighteen-year-old citizens to defend its territory, the state loses its primary geopolitical incentive to invest in the health, education, and social safety net of the lower classes. An elite class that commands autonomous drones and algorithmic cyber-defenses has no existential survival need for the masses.

3. Daron Acemoglu: The Race Between Displacement and Reinstatement

Mainstream orthodox economists routinely dismiss warnings of technological unemployment by citing historical precedent: "The Luddites smashed mechanical weaving looms in 1811 because they thought machines would cause permanent poverty; instead, the Industrial Revolution created millions of new, better-paying jobs in railways, offices, and retail!"

In their landmark 2020 study in the Journal of Political Economy, MIT economists Daron Acemoglu and Pascual Restrepo demonstrated why the Luddite analogy is dangerously flawed in the era of artificial intelligence.

Acemoglu and Restrepo showed that technological change exerts two competing forces on human labor:

  1. The Displacement Effect: Capital and automated machinery directly substitute for workers in existing tasks, reducing labor’s share of national income and eliminating jobs.
  2. The Reinstatement Effect: Technological progress creates brand-new, complex tasks and industries in which human beings possess a comparative advantage, restoring employment and raising wages.

During the 19th and 20th centuries, the displacement effect and the reinstatement effect were roughly balanced: as machines replaced farm hands, new industries (automobile manufacturing, aviation, telecommunications, corporate accounting) created millions of new human tasks.

Today, however, Acemoglu and Restrepo prove that the displacement effect is massively outstripping the reinstatement effect. Artificial intelligence is not merely automating physical muscle; it is automating general cognitive processing, pattern recognition, and language—the very faculties that previously allowed displaced workers to retrain into new knowledge industries!

4. The Radiologist vs. The Plumber: The White-Collar Automation Paradox

For decades, career counselors advised young people: "Get a high-skill college degree, become a doctor, lawyer, or programmer, and you will be completely safe from automation. Only low-skill manual factory workers get replaced by robots."

The reality of contemporary deep learning has inverted this hierarchy completely. Consider the contrast between a Board-Certified Medical Radiologist and a Residential Plumber:

The Radiologist vs. The Plumber

  • The Radiologist: An elite medical specialist who spent four years at university, four years in medical school, and five years in clinical residency. They earn $450,000 a year sitting in a dark room staring at two-dimensional digital pixels on a screen, diagnosing lung cancer, bone fractures, and brain tumors. But that task is fundamentally a digital pattern-recognition problem. Deep convolutional neural networks trained on tens of millions of CT and MRI scans can analyze an image in three milliseconds, never experience sleep deprivation, and consistently match or exceed human radiologist accuracy in detecting early malignant micro-nodules. Automating radiology requires zero physical robots; it requires only a few lines of code downloaded over the internet.
  • The Plumber: An individual who works with a wrench, kneeling under a cramped kitchen sink, maneuvering through spiderwebs and copper pipes in an unpredictable physical environment, diagnosing a leaking valve based on tactile touch, sound, and spatial intuition. As Hans Moravec demonstrated (Moravec’s Paradox), building an autonomous robot capable of crawling into an unknown basement, diagnosing an obstructed pipe, and twisting a rusted wrench without breaking the wall requires billions of dollars of robotics research that remains decades away!

In the twenty-first century, white-collar knowledge workers who manipulate digital symbols on a screen are far more vulnerable to immediate algorithmic replacement than blue-collar trade workers whose jobs require physical dexterity, spatial flexibility, and embodied common sense.

5. The Political Abyss: Managing the Useless Class

If millions of human beings become economically irrelevant, what happens to democratic politics, social cohesion, and human dignity?

Silicon Valley billionaires and technocrats propose a single, universal palliative: Universal Basic Income (UBI). Let autonomous algorithms, automated logistics, and technology monopolies generate trillions in wealth; tax a sliver of that profit, and distribute a monthly government stipend of $1,500 to every citizen so they can purchase food, shelter, and consumer goods.

Harari exposes the fatal spiritual and political flaw of this solution: basic income keeps human bodies biologically alive, but it does not provide human lives with meaning, dignity, or power.

A population living on government stipends, spending twelve hours a day in immersive virtual reality video games and consuming algorithmic entertainment, is not a citizenry; it is a domestic animal herd. They have zero political leverage. If an authoritarian regime or a billionaire technocracy decides to cut their benefits, privatize resources, or ignore their welfare, the irrelevant class cannot strike, cannot halt production, and cannot rebel. They have become purely parasitic consumers in an automated economy that no longer needs them.

6. Core Heuristics for Navigating the Era of Automation

1. Pivot from "Specialized Routine" to "Embodied Generalism"

Never build your career entirely upon narrow, codified, algorithmic knowledge (e.g., standard tax accounting, basic contract drafting, routine radiology, or junior coding). If your daily work can be defined by a clear checklist or a mathematical formula, a machine will do it faster and cheaper. Pivot toward multidisciplinary synthesis, high-stakes human negotiation, physical craftsmanship, and deep interpersonal counseling—domains where human presence, emotional resonance, and physical embodiment remain irreplaceable.

2. Cultivate "Mental Elasticity" Over Static Degrees

In the twentieth century, life was divided into two rigid phases: a period of learning (until age twenty-five), followed by a period of working in that same specialized profession until retirement. In the twenty-first century, the pace of algorithmic disruption means that your profession will likely be rendered obsolete every ten years. The most vital survival skill is not mastering a single skill, but the psychological capacity to unlearn, reinvent yourself, and endure the emotional pain of being a total beginner multiple times across your lifespan.

3. Fight for "Human-Centric Labor Reinstatement"

In corporate and public governance, reject the fatalistic myth that automation must always destroy human jobs. Follow Daron Acemoglu’s guidance: actively design and deploy technology that empowers, augments, and reinstates human workers rather than merely displacing them. Use AI as a bicycle for the human mind, enabling nurses to deliver physician-level triage, or teachers to provide hyper-personalized mentorship, rather than replacing humans with cold software kiosks.

4. Separate Human Self-Worth from Economic Productivity

Begin dismantling the toxic capitalist conditioning that equates a human being's moral value with their economic productivity or market salary. A human life is sacred because it possesses conscious sentience, the capacity to love, experience beauty, and practice kindness—not because it generates GDP. Cultivate community institutions, artistic practices, civic volunteerism, and spiritual traditions that honor human dignity independent of market utility.

7. Self-Diagnostic Checklist: Auditing Your Economic Fragility

  • How much of my daily professional work consists of routine cognitive pattern recognition that could theoretically be performed by a fine-tuned large language model?
  • Does my value lie in following established operating procedures, or in navigating chaotic, unscripted human emotions and complex ethical trade-offs?
  • How emotionally prepared am I to completely retrain and learn an entirely new craft or industry if my current profession is automated within the next decade?
  • Do I derive my personal sense of dignity and identity exclusively from my corporate job title, or am I grounded in rich community and human relationships?
  • Am I advocating for ethical policies, democratic oversight, and educational reforms that protect human workers from being cast aside as economic waste?

  • Leontief, Wassily. "National Perspective: The Choice of Technology." The New York Times (June 30, 1983). The classic, chilling article establishing the horse analogy for human technological unemployment.

  • Susskind, Daniel. A World Without Work: Technology, Automation, and How We Should Respond (Metropolitan Books, 2020). An Oxford economist's brilliant, accessible diagnosis of the future of labor.
  • Keynes, John Maynard. "Economic Possibilities for our Grandchildren." In Essays in Persuasion (Macmillan, 1931; orig. 1930). The visionary essay that first coined the term "technological unemployment."
