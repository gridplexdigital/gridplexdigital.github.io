The Importance of Maintenance

Modern culture worships the flashy drama of creation, launch, and disruption, while treating routine upkeep with bored contempt. Yet all complex systems—from bridges and power grids to software codebases and human relationships—decay under the relentless law of entropy. Civilization survives not through innovation alone, but through the quiet, heroic labor of maintenance.

📖 13-Minute Deep Read (2,675 words) ⚡ Short on time? Switch to the 2-minute field note summary →

At 6:05 PM on August 1, 2007, during the peak of evening rush hour in Minneapolis, Minnesota, the eight-lane steel truss arch I-35W Mississippi River Bridge suddenly groaned, snapped, and plunged 115 feet into the murky waters below. In a matter of seconds, 1,000 feet of highway collapsed into tangled rebar and crushed concrete, carrying 111 vehicles and a yellow school bus filled with sixty children into the abyss. Thirteen people were killed, and 145 were severely injured. The disaster horrified the nation: how could a critical artery carrying 140,000 vehicles a day through a major American metropolis simply vanish into a river on a clear, sunny summer evening? The subsequent investigation by the National Transportation Safety Board (NTSB) identified a fatal combination of an original 1960s design flaw in undersized gusset plates, compounded by a catastrophic cultural and institutional disease: decades of deferred maintenance. For more than fifteen years leading up to the collapse, state inspection reports had consistently rated the bridge as "structurally deficient," documenting widespread corrosion, fatigue cracking, and severe rust on critical structural steel. Yet year after year, state and municipal officials repeatedly delayed comprehensive repairs, opting instead for superficial patch jobs and cosmetic repaving while pouring hundreds of millions of public dollars into ribbon-cutting ceremonies for shiny new highway extensions. Ribbon-cutting ceremonies win elections; fixing rusty bolts beneath an underpass does not. Modern civilization suffers from a collective, narcissistic pathology: we glorify the "innovator," the "founder," and the "disruptor," while treating the maintainer with invisible, underpaid contempt. Yet under the unyielding law of the Second Law of Thermodynamics, all physical and social architecture rots without care. Civilization does not endure because we invent new things; civilization endures because an unsung army of maintainers wake up every morning to hold back the tide of entropy.

1. Andrew Russell and Lee Vinsel: The Innovation Delusion

In 2016, technology historians Andrew Russell and Lee Vinsel published an explosive essay in Aeon titled "Hail the Maintainers," which they later expanded into their monumental 2020 book, The Innovation Delusion: How Our Obsession with the New Has Disrupted the Work That Matters Most.

Russell and Vinsel launched a devastating critique against what they termed "Innovation-Speak"—the glossy, Silicon Valley-dominated corporate theology that treats disruptive novelty as the highest human virtue. Innovation-speak promises that every societal problem, from crumbling schools to climate change, can be solved by launching an app, pitching venture capitalists, and disrupting existing institutions.

Russell and Vinsel revealed the profound, dangerous distortion this ideology creates:

"Entire societies have been hypnotized by the cult of innovation. We celebrate the start-up founder, the venture capitalist, and the disruptive visionary, while ignoring the people who actually keep our world running: the mechanics, the sewage technicians, the road menders, the nurses, the line cooks, and the code refactorers. The true foundation of human flourishing is not innovation, but maintenance."
— Andrew Russell & Lee Vinsel, The Innovation Delusion (2020)

The authors demonstrated that across the entire life cycle of any physical or digital technology—whether an automobile, a power grid, a hospital, or an operating system—less than 20 percent of total lifetime costs and labor are spent on initial design and construction. Over 80 percent of a technology’s existence is pure maintenance. By starving maintenance of funding, prestige, and cultural respect, modern societies build glittering, brittle empires that collapse at the first shock.

2. Stewart Brand and the Shearing Layers of Architecture

In his 1994 masterpiece, How Buildings Learn: What Happens After They're Built, polymath and founder of the Whole Earth Catalog Stewart Brand formulated one of the most profound models in systems engineering: The Shearing Layers of Architecture (building on the work of British architect Frank Duffy).

Brand observed that a building is not a static sculpture; a building is an ongoing, dynamic dialogue among components that age and change at radically different temporal speeds:

Stewart Brand’s Six Shearing Layers

  1. Site (Eternal / Geographical): The physical parcel of land, street boundaries, and geological bedrock. Measured in centuries or millennia.
  2. Structure (30 to 300 years): The load-bearing foundation, concrete pillars, and steel framework. Inflexible and dangerous to change.
  3. Skin (20 to 30 years): The exterior facade, cladding, masonry, and roof surfaces exposed to weather.
  4. Services (7 to 15 years): The working vascular organs of the building: plumbing pipes, electrical wiring, HVAC air ducts, and elevators. They wear out and require periodic replacement.
  5. Space Plan (3 to 30 years): The interior layout: partition walls, doors, ceilings, and hallways. Redesigned whenever commercial tenants shift.
  6. Stuff (Daily to Monthly): The transient furniture, lamps, desks, laptops, and personal belongings moved around daily.

Brand revealed the supreme law of architectural maintenance: A successful building must allow its fast-changing layers (Stuff, Space Plan, Services) to be repaired, replaced, and maintained without tearing apart or destabilizing the slow-moving foundations (Structure, Site).

When architects lock high-maintenance pipes inside poured structural concrete, or when software engineers tightly couple front-end UI buttons directly to deep database schema logic, they commit a architectural crime. True systems wisdom designs for repairability, creating modular boundaries that allow maintainers to swap out decaying services without bringing down the foundation.

3. Technical Debt in Software: The Southwest Airlines Christmas Meltdown

In the digital economy, maintenance neglect takes on an invisible, insidious name: Technical Debt. Just like financial debt, taking shortcuts in software engineering—copy-pasting quick patches, skipping automated testing, and delaying refactoring—accumulates compound interest. If that debt is not paid down through continuous maintenance, the system eventually goes bankrupt.

The most catastrophic modern demonstration occurred during Christmas week in December 2022 at Southwest Airlines.

When Winter Storm Elliott swept across North America with freezing temperatures and snow, all major airlines experienced delays. American, Delta, and United canceled hundreds of flights, stabilized their schedules, and resumed normal operations within forty-eight hours.

Southwest Airlines, however, suffered an unprecedented, apocalyptic operational collapse. The airline canceled over 16,700 flights in ten days, stranding more than two million passengers, losing millions of pieces of luggage, and leaving flight attendants and pilots stranded in hotel lobbies across the nation without assignments.

What caused the meltdown? It was not the winter storm. It was the total collapse of Southwest’s internal crew-scheduling software, known as SkySolver.

For decades, Southwest’s leadership had pursued aggressive commercial expansion, buying new Boeing 737 aircraft, paying generous shareholder dividends, and launching new routes. Meanwhile, the airline’s internal IT scheduling infrastructure remained a neglected, brittle relic dating back to the 1990s. The software was so fragile that when weather disrupted flight schedules, the algorithm crashed, requiring human schedulers to manually pick up telephones and match tens of thousands of pilots and flight attendants to airplanes on whiteboards and Excel spreadsheets.

For years, unionized pilots and software engineers had repeatedly warned Southwest’s board of directors that the system was held together with "duct tape and baling wire." Leadership dismissed the warnings, choosing short-term quarterly profits over boring software maintenance. The price of their neglect was absolute humiliation, federal investigations, and an estimated $1.1 billion in financial losses in a single week.

4. The Millennial Maintenance of the Ise Grand Shrine

To understand how human civilization can sustain physical and cultural continuity across thousands of years, one must travel to the sacred cypress forests of Mie Prefecture, Japan: the Ise Grand Shrine (Ise Jingu).

Dedicated to the sun goddess Amaterasu, Ise is the spiritual beating heart of Shinto tradition. Yet if you visit Ise, you will not find ancient, moss-covered, crumbling stone pillars like the Parthenon in Athens or the Colosseum in Rome. The wooden pavilions at Ise look as pristine, golden, and fresh as if they were built yesterday.

Because they were. For more than 1,300 years, beginning in the reign of Empress Jito in 690 CE, the Japanese have practiced a sacred ritual of radical maintenance known as Shikinen Sengu (式年遷宮):

Shikinen Sengu: Rebuilding the Eternal

  • Every twenty years, without fail, the entire shrine complex is completely dismantled and rebuilt identically from scratch on an adjacent, identical plot of land.
  • The construction requires sixteen years of preparation: harvesting 10,000 Japanese cypress (*hinoki*) logs from sustainably maintained sacred forests, hand-hewing timbers using traditional iron planes, weaving reeds for thatched roofs, and forging 714 distinct sacred treasures and ceremonial swords.
  • When the new shrine is consecrated, the divine spirit is transferred in a midnight procession, and the twenty-year-old adjacent shrine is carefully disassembled, its timbers distributed to rebuild community shrines across Japan. In 2013, Ise completed its 62nd consecutive rebuilding.

Why do the Japanese rebuild the shrine every twenty years? Because they recognized a profound truth about human memory and physical architecture: wood rots, and human skill dies. If you wait one hundred years to rebuild a shrine, the master carpenters who built the original are long dead, their tacit knowledge, tool-making skills, and joining techniques lost forever.

By rebuilding every twenty years, the generation of forty-year-old master carpenters trains the twenty-year-old apprentices, while the sixty-year-old elders advise. The shrine remains permanently immortal not by resisting decay, but by institutionalizing maintenance as a continuous ritual of renewal.

5. Robert Pirsig and the Spiritual Dignity of Care

In his philosophical classic Zen and the Art of Motorcycle Maintenance (1974), Robert M. Pirsig elevated maintenance from an unpleasant chore to a profound spiritual and existential practice.

Pirsig contrasted two friends on a cross-country motorcycle trip: John Sutherland, who represents the modern romantic consumer, hates knowing how his motorcycle works. When his engine sputters, he feels helpless, alienated, and infuriated by the cold, ugly "technology" that ruined his day. Pirsig, by contrast, approaches his machine with the quiet, reverent focus of a Zen master. He listens to the micro-vibrations of the engine, measures the clearance of the spark plug with a feeler gauge, and cleans the carburetor.

Pirsig realized that maintenance is where human beings encounter Quality (Arete):

"The test of the machine is the satisfaction it gives you. There isn't any other test. If the machine produces tranquility it's right. If it disturbs you it's wrong until either the machine or your mind is changed... The Buddha, the Godhead, resides quite as comfortably in the circuits of a digital computer or the gears of a cycle transmission as he does at the top of a mountain or in the petals of a flower."
— Robert M. Pirsig, Zen and the Art of Motorcycle Maintenance (1974)

To maintain something—a motorcycle, a garden, a database, or a marriage—is to enter into an intimate, caring relationship with reality. It is the recognition that nothing in this universe stays healthy without our loving, patient attention.

6. The Threshold Bridge: The Maintenance of Intersubjective Realities

As we conclude the foundational first 101 lenses of human systems, we arrive at the profound realization that maintenance is the invisible bridge linking the physical world to human culture.

Physical infrastructure—bridges, power grids, and servers—must be maintained against the physical entropy of rust, wear, and heat. But what about the invisible infrastructure of human civilization? What keeps a currency valuable? What keeps a legal constitution binding? What keeps a corporation like Google or Apple functioning across a hundred thousand employees who have never met one another?

These institutions are not physical objects made of atoms; they are Intersubjective Realities—shared myths, collective stories, and social fictions that exist exclusively within the shared imagination of millions of human minds. And just like the Ise Grand Shrine or the I-35W bridge, these intersubjective orders decay, rot, and collapse into violence if they are not maintained through continuous cultural renewal, shared rituals, ethical norms, and active civic trust.

It is here, at the frontier of human social architecture, that our next chapter begins: stepping into the anthropological vision of Yuval Noah Harari, the philosophical psychology of the Bhagavad Gita, and the ancient strategic wisdom of China.

7. Core Heuristics for the Maintainer's Mindset

1. The "Twenty Percent Maintenance Tax" Rule

In every engineering sprint, corporate budget, or personal calendar, mandatorily allocate at least 20 percent of your resources exclusively to paying down technical, structural, and relational debt. Refactor messy code, repair broken tools, clear backlogged administrative debt, and clean your workspace. If you spend 100% of your energy sprinting toward new features, you are borrowing against your future solvency at usurious interest rates.

2. Celebrate the Invisible Heroism of Maintenance

In organizational life, deliberately shift incentives and public praise away from the "firefighters" and toward the "fire preventers." The engineer who stays up all night on a frantic heroic crusade to save a crashed server gets applauded; the quiet engineer who maintained the database so well that it never crashed in five years is ignored. Change your corporate culture: reward and promote the maintainers whose quiet diligence ensures that crises never happen.

3. Design for Disassembly and Repair (The Brand Rule)

Whenever you build an organizational workflow, a technical architecture, or a physical facility, follow Stewart Brand’s shearing layers principle: make every component that wears out fast easily accessible and cheap to replace without touching the core foundation. Never lock volatile services into permanent concrete.

4. Treat Maintenance as a Daily Liturgy, Not an Emergency Response

Do not wait for a bridge to collapse, an engine to seize, or a marriage to break before paying attention. Build small, unvarying daily and weekly maintenance rituals: checking tire pressure, auditing software logs, conducting regular relationship check-ins, and clearing desk surfaces. Maintenance practiced continuously is effortless; maintenance delayed is catastrophic.

8. Self-Diagnostic Checklist: Auditing Your Maintenance Debt

  • What is the critical piece of physical, digital, or operational infrastructure in my life that I have been taking for granted while ignoring its warning signs of decay?
  • In my professional team, do we celebrate the quiet maintainers who prevent disasters with the same enthusiasm that we celebrate the flashy innovators who launch new products?
  • How much accumulated 'technical debt' (unresolved bugs, disorganized files, deferred repairs, unaddressed relational friction) am I currently carrying?
  • When designing new projects, do I budget for the multi-year costs of maintenance and upkeep, or do I focus exclusively on the thrill of the launch date?
  • Am I maintaining the invisible social and civic commons—investing in community trust, local institutions, and deep friendships—that sustains my personal freedom?
