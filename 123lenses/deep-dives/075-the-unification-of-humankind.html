The Three Universal Unifiers of Humankind

For millennia, human tribes lived divided by geography, language, and ancestral hatred. Global integration was not achieved through philosophical treaties or moral sermons; it was forged through three ruthless, practical institutions: the monetary order, the imperial order, and universal religions.

In the sweltering summer of 1192, outside the fortified coastal walls of the Crusader port of Jaffa in the Holy Land, two of the greatest military commanders in world history stood locked in mortal ideological combat. On one side was King Richard the Lionheart of England, leading an army of European Catholic Crusaders; on the other was Sultan Saladin, the revered leader of the Islamic Ayyubid Empire. For decades, Europe and the Middle East had poured blood and treasure into the sands of the Levant, whipped into religious fury by papal bulls and jihadi declarations. To the Christian crusader, the Muslim was an infidel doomed to eternal hellfire; to the Muslim warrior, the crusader was a barbaric idolater defiling holy shrines. When they met on the battlefield, neither side gave quarter; men hacked off limbs and impaled prisoners in the name of God. Yet when the fighting paused and merchants set up tents in the neutral border markets between Damascus, Acre, and Jaffa, an astonishing economic phenomenon took place. Christian crusader merchants eagerly accepted the Islamic gold Dinar—a coin stamped in flowing Arabic script with the sacred Islamic declaration of faith: "There is no God but Allah, and Muhammad is His Messenger." Simultaneously, Muslim merchants in Cairo and Aleppo gladly accepted the silver Venetian Groat and the gold Ducat, stamped with the image of Jesus Christ standing in divine majesty and holding the Gospels! Men who would slit each other’s throats over the theological status of Jesus Christ had zero qualms about accepting each other's money. Why? Because while Christians and Muslims could agree on almost nothing in the heavens, they agreed with 100 percent unanimity on the value of a gold coin. In Part Three of Sapiens, Yuval Noah Harari reveals the grand, sweeping arc of human history: the arrow of history moves relentlessly from fragmentation toward global unity. And that colossal unification was not forged by pacifist philosophers or United Nations treaties; it was forged by three ruthless, practical universal orders: Money, Empires, and Universal Religions.

1. The Arrow of History: From Worlds to a World

If you stood on the surface of planet Earth in 10,000 BCE, human civilization was not a single global community; it was an archipelago of thousands of completely isolated, disconnected human worlds. A hunter-gatherer living in the Amazon rainforest had zero awareness that farmers were cultivating rice along the Yangtze River; an Aboriginal Australian had no idea that mammoth hunters lived in the Siberian tundra. Each tribe possessed its own language, its own ancestral spirits, its own taboos, and its own territory. Strangers from an adjacent valley were viewed as non-human beasts to be murdered on sight.

Yet over the subsequent ten millennia, Harari demonstrates that human history has moved in a single, irreversible, non-random direction: toward global unification. Today, eight billion human beings are bound into a single, interconnected planetary civilization:

  • A single international geopolitical system (the United Nations).
  • A single global economic and financial market.
  • A single universal body of science, medicine, and legal standards.

What were the practical historical engines that dissolved the thousands of tribal boundaries and welded humanity into a single global order? Harari identifies the three great unifiers:

The Three Universal Orders of Humankind

  1. The Economic Unifier (The Monetary Order): Money is the ultimate, apolitical system of mutual trust—the only intersubjective reality that everyone on earth believes in, regardless of culture, race, or religion.
  2. The Political Unifier (The Imperial Order): Empires conquered disparate, warring tribes, melted away their local parochial boundaries, and incorporated them into vast zones of common law, standard taxation, universal alphabets, and shared citizenship.
  3. The Ideological Unifier (Universal Religions): Missionary religions (Buddhism, Christianity, Islam) declared for the first time that all human beings, regardless of ancestry, were equal subjects under a single, universal cosmic law.

2. Order #1: The Monetary Order (The Universal Trust Machine)

In modern intellectual circles, money is routinely denounced as the root of all evil, alienation, and corruption. But from an anthropological standpoint, Harari points out that money is the apogee of human tolerance and cooperation:

"Money is the most universal and most efficient system of mutual trust ever devised... Money is the only trust system created by humans that can bridge almost any cultural divide, and that does not discriminate on the basis of religion, gender, race, age, or sexual orientation. Thanks to money, even people who do not know each other and do not trust each other can nevertheless cooperate effectively."
— Yuval Noah Harari, Sapiens (2014)

Consider how difficult human cooperation was before money. In a pure barter economy, trade requires a "Double Coincidence of Wants": if an apple orchard farmer wants a pair of shoes, he must find a cobbler who not only has shoes, but who happens to desperately crave ten bushels of perishable apples at that exact second. Barter caps trade at the local village perimeter.

Money solved this bottleneck through a pure intersubjective consensus: money is anything that people agree to use to systematically represent the value of other things. When an Iraqi merchant trades oil for Japanese cars using American digital dollars stored on a Swiss server, no one needs to meet, share a religion, or speak a common tongue. Money converts personal distrust into universal mathematical cooperation.

3. Order #2: The Imperial Order (The Melting Pot of History)

In contemporary political discourse, "empire" is a dirty word, associated exclusively with violent conquest, pillage, and racial exploitation. Harari acknowledges that empires were almost always founded through unspeakable military brutality, slaughter, and imperial subjugation.

Yet Harari forces us to confront a profound, uncomfortable historical reality: the imperial order was the primary historical mechanism that broke down tribal chauvinism and created universal human culture.

Before the rise of empires, the world was fractured into thousands of tiny, squabbling city-states and ethnic clans that viewed each other with xenophobic hatred. Empires had two defining characteristics:

  • They ruled over a vast diversity of distinct ethnic, linguistic, and cultural groups.
  • They possessed flexible, porous borders and an insatiable appetite to absorb new territories and peoples into a common administrative architecture.

The Roman Empire and the Invention of Common Citizenship

In 212 CE, the Roman Emperor Caracalla issued the Constitutio Antoniniana (the Edict of Caracalla). With a stroke of an imperial stylus, Caracalla declared that every free man living within the borders of the Roman Empire was granted full Roman citizenship!

Think about what that meant: a Celtic Briton living near Hadrian’s Wall, a Greek merchant in Athens, a Jewish scholar in Alexandria, a Berber farmer in North Africa, and a Syrian soldier on the Euphrates were suddenly equal citizens under Roman civil law. They spoke a common lingua franca (Latin or Greek), traded with the same Roman coins, used the same Roman baths and roads, and were judged by the same legal code.

Over centuries, the distinction between the "conquering Romans" and the "conquered barbarians" melted away completely. Multiple Roman Emperors—including Trajan and Hadrian (born in Spain), Septimius Severus (born in Libya), and Philip the Arab (born in Syria)—hailed from the conquered provinces. The empire had violently conquered them, but then forged them into a unified Mediterranean civilization.

Almost all modern nations—including China, India, the United States, France, and Mexico—are the direct cultural descendants of historical empires that crushed thousands of regional dialects and tribal identities into a single shared national consciousness.

4. Order #3: Universal Religions (The Cosmic Equality of Souls)

The third great unifier was the birth of Universal, Missionary Religions.

For most of human history, religions were local, animist, or polytheist. An ancient Sumerian worshipped the local god of Ur; an ancient Athenian sacrificed to Athena; an ancient Roman prayed to Mars. These local gods did not care about people outside their city gates. If a Roman merchant traveled to Egypt, he did not demand that the Egyptians stop worshipping Isis; he simply tipped his hat to Isis and sacrificed to her while in Alexandria! Polytheism was inherently pluralistic, local, and tolerant.

Then, in the first millennium BCE, a revolutionary spiritual phenomenon emerged: Universal Religions:

  • Universal Scope: They claimed to possess a single, eternal cosmic truth that applied to every single human being on earth, regardless of their tribe, nation, language, or caste.
  • Missionary Imperative: They believed it was their sacred, urgent duty to actively spread this truth to the ends of the earth, converting all of humanity to the true faith.

Emperor Ashoka and the Buddhist Moral Unification

In the 3rd century BCE, following the horrific, bloody conquest of the province of Kalinga in eastern India—where 100,000 soldiers were slaughtered and 150,000 driven into exile—the third Mauryan Emperor, Ashoka the Great, experienced a profound spiritual awakening. Horrified by the carnage he had unleashed, Ashoka renounced imperial conquest and embraced the teachings of Siddhartha Gautama (the Buddha).

Ashoka did not build victory arches to glorify his military conquest. Instead, he carved his famous Major Rock Edicts into towering stone pillars across modern India, Pakistan, Nepal, and Afghanistan.

Ashoka declared a revolutionary universal moral doctrine: Dhamma. He proclaimed that all men were his children, banned animal sacrifices, opened free hospitals for both humans and animals, ordered the planting of shade trees and digging of wells along public roads for travelers, and dispatched Buddhist monks on peaceful diplomatic missions to Sri Lanka, Burma, and the Hellenistic kingdoms of Greece and Egypt. Ashoka proved that universal religion could unite an empire not through the sword, but through universal compassion and ethical duty.

5. The Irony of Global Unification: Conflict on a Planetary Scale

Harari notes a profound, tragic historical irony: the very institutions that unified humanity also introduced unprecedented, apocalyptic forms of conflict.

Ancient animists fought vicious border skirmishes over hunting grounds, but they never fought global ideological holy wars. A Cherokee warrior never sailed across the Atlantic to wage a bloody crusade to force the King of France to worship the Great Spirit.

It was only when universal orders took root—when empires claimed universal dominion, when monotheistic religions claimed exclusive access to eternal salvation, and when modern ideologies (liberalism, communism, fascism) claimed to represent the universal destiny of humanity—that humans began slaughtering each other by the millions over abstract global doctrines. When an order claims to be universal, anyone who refuses to submit to it is viewed not merely as a neighbor with different habits, but as an existential infection that must be cleansed from the planet.

6. Core Heuristics for Navigating the Universal Orders

1. The "Monetary Bridge" in Polarized Negotiations

When negotiating across fierce ideological, political, or cultural chasms, never begin by trying to reconcile abstract religious, philosophical, or historical grievances. Follow the lessons of Richard the Lionheart and Saladin: begin on the neutral terrain of practical, mutual economic benefit. Find the shared commercial contract, the joint supply-chain cost savings, or the shared physical infrastructure where money speaks a universal language. Once commercial trust is established, ideological temperature drops.

2. Recognize the "Imperial Inheritance" of Your Identity

Examine your native language, your legal system, your architectural styles, and your culinary habits. Recognize that you are the product of historical imperial melting pots. The English language is a mongrel fusion of Anglo-Saxon, Norman French, Latin, and Scandinavian dialects forged through Roman and Norman invasions; Indian democracy is conducted in English over British-built railways using British parliamentary procedure. Shed pure ethnic chauvinism; humanity is an ongoing hybrid creation.

3. Beware the Fanaticism of the "Universal Truth"

Whenever an ideology, a technology platform, or a political movement claims that it possesses the single, universal formula for human progress (e.g., "Everyone on earth must adopt our economic model, our governance structure, or our moral values"), proceed with extreme caution. Universal missionary certainty is the psychological precursor to imperial hubris and cultural devastation. Cultivate the pluralistic humility to allow diverse human cultures to solve the human dilemma in diverse ways.

4. Defend the Global Commons from Neo-Tribal Fracturing

The challenges of the twenty-first century—climate catastrophe, nuclear proliferation, autonomous AI weapons, and global pandemics—are radically planetary. They cannot be solved by any single sovereign nation-state or tribal clan. While respecting local cultural roots, fiercely defend the global, universal institutions (international law, open scientific coordination, global agreements) that hold humanity together. Fracturing back into armed tribal camps is a suicide pact in a thermonuclear, automated age.

7. Self-Diagnostic Checklist: Auditing Your Global Citizenship

  • Do I view people of different nationalities, races, or religions as fundamentally alien creatures, or do I recognize that we operate on the exact same underlying biological and intersubjective software?
  • Am I holding onto a myth of 'pure' ancestral culture, while ignoring how my own language, food, and laws were forged through centuries of imperial and global cross-pollination?
  • When facing difficult professional or political disagreements, do I default to moral self-righteousness, or do I search for common, practical ground?
  • Do I celebrate the global trade and technological networks that sustain my daily life, while actively supporting the ethical and ecological reforms required to make them fair?
  • How do I contribute to building trust across cultural boundaries in my immediate community or company?

Further Reading and Primary Sources

  • Harari, Yuval Noah. Sapiens: A Brief History of Humankind (Harper, 2014). Part Three: "The Unification of Humankind" (Chapters 9–12). The definitive macro-historical synthesis of money, empires, and universal religions.
  • Simmel, Georg. The Philosophy of Money (trans. Tom Bottomore and David Frisby, Routledge, 1978; orig. 1900). The classical sociological masterpiece on the objectifying and leveling power of money.
  • Gibbon, Edward. The History of the Decline and Fall of the Roman Empire (Strahan & Cadell, 1776–1789). The monumental classic history of the Roman imperial order and Mediterranean integration.
  • Thapar, Romila. Asoka and the Decline of the Mauryas (Oxford University Press, 1961). The authoritative historical and epigraphical study of Emperor Ashoka's Edicts and universal Dhamma statecraft.
  • Greif, Avner. Institutions and the Path to the Modern Economy: Lessons from Medieval Trade (Cambridge University Press, 2006). A rigorous economic analysis of medieval long-distance trade and cross-cultural institutions.
