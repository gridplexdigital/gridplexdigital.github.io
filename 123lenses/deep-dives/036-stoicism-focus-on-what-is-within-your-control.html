Stoicism: Focus on What Is Within Your Control

Suffering arises not from external events, but from the flawed judgments we form about them. By dividing the universe strictly into what is within our sovereignty and what is not, Stoicism provides an unbreakable psychological citadel of emotional calm, moral agency, and radical resilience.

On September 9, 1965, Commander James Bond Stockdale was flying an A-4E Skyhawk attack jet over North Vietnam when a barrage of anti-aircraft artillery shattered his aircraft. Ejecting from his burning jet, Stockdale drifted down beneath his parachute into a small Vietnamese village, where an enraged mob immediately surrounded him, beat him savagely, and fractured his leg. As he was falling through the sky toward seven and a half years of unimaginable captivity in the infamous "Hanoi Hilton" prison camp—over four years of which would be spent in complete solitary confinement, locked in heavy iron leg irons, enduring fifteen rounds of agonizing physical torture—Stockdale made a conscious, extraordinary mental pivot. He had spent years studying classical Western philosophy at Stanford University, memorizing the teachings of a first-century Greek slave named Epictetus. As his parachute descended, Stockdale whispered to himself: "I'm leaving the world of technology and entering the world of Epictetus." Stockdale knew that as a senior prisoner of war, his captors held total physical control over his body: they could starve him, isolate him, break his bones, and deny him medical care. But Stockdale resolved that they could never touch his mind, his moral integrity, or his agency. He organized an elaborate secret tapping code through prison walls, organized prisoner resistance, and when told he would be paraded before foreign cameras for propaganda, he slashed his own face with a razor and beat himself with a wooden stool until he was unrecognizable, choosing physical agony to preserve his honor. Stockdale survived seven and a half years of terror and received the Medal of Honor, attributing his survival entirely to a single philosophical principle: the absolute distinction between what is within your control and what is not.

1. Epictetus and the Dichotomy of Control

Stoicism was founded in Athens around 300 BCE by Zeno of Citium, who lectured his students from a painted colonnade (the Stoa Poikile). While early Hellenistic Stoicism encompassed physics and formal logic, Roman Stoicism distilled the philosophy into an urgent, practical art of living.

The towering architect of Stoic psychology was Epictetus (c. 50–135 CE). Born into slavery in Hierapolis (modern-day Turkey), Epictetus was owned by a brutal master in Rome who deliberately broke his leg, leaving him permanently disabled with a severe limp. After gaining his freedom following Emperor Nero’s death, Epictetus established a famous philosophical school in Greece.

Epictetus opened his foundational handbook, the Enchiridion, with the immortal axiom that constitutes the bedrock of Stoic thought—the Dichotomy of Control:

"Some things are in our control and others not. Things in our control are opinion, pursuit, desire, aversion, and, in a word, whatever are our own actions. Things not in our control are body, property, reputation, command, and, in one word, whatever are not our own actions."

— Epictetus, Enchiridion (Chapter 1)

Epictetus divided the entire cosmos into two mutually exclusive categories:

  • Ta Eph' Hemin (Things Up to Us): Your judgments, intentions, values, moral choices, where you direct your attention, and how you react to external occurrences. This is the sole domain of your sovereignty. Inside this citadel, you possess absolute, unassailable freedom.
  • Ta Ouk Eph' Hemin (Things Not Up to Us): The physical body (illness, aging, genetics), external property, your social reputation, whether other people like or praise you, macroeconomic conditions, the weather, and death itself. These things are ultimately governed by external nature, luck, and other people.

The central diagnosis of Stoicism is that 100 percent of human psychological misery stems from confusing these two categories. Whenever you tether your happiness, self-worth, or peace of mind to an external outcome you cannot control (such as being promoted, receiving applause, or avoiding physical sickness), you have made yourself a slave to external circumstance. When the external world fails to deliver what you desire, you experience rage, anxiety, envy, and despair. Peace of mind (ataraxia) is attained the instant you retreat to the only territory you actually own: your own character and moral choices.

2. Marcus Aurelius: The Emperor’s Citadel

While Epictetus began life as an impoverished, disabled slave at the bottom of the Roman world, Marcus Aurelius (121–180 CE) ruled that exact same world as Emperor of Rome—the most powerful human being on Earth.

Yet Marcus Aurelius faced relentless, unimaginable trials: between 165 and 180 CE, the Antonine Plague (likely smallpox) swept across the empire, decimating the Roman military and killing up to five million citizens; Germanic tribes shattered the northern frontier, forcing Marcus to spend the final fourteen years of his life living in freezing, muddy military tents along the Danube River; and eight of his thirteen children died in childhood.

Marcus wrote his private journal—never intended for publication, titled simply Ta Eis Heauton (To Himself, known today as Meditations)—as a daily personal discipline to remind himself of his Stoic duties:

"You have power over your mind—not outside events. Realize this, and you will find strength... The happiness of your life depends upon the quality of your thoughts: therefore, guard accordingly, and take care that you entertain no notions unsuitable to virtue and reasonable nature."

— Marcus Aurelius, Meditations

Marcus recognized that events in themselves are completely neutral: an event is merely a physical occurrence—a rainstorm, a betrayal by a friend, an economic downturn, a tumor. The event carries no moral value. It is the human mind that instantly attaches an interpretive judgment: "This is terrible; my life is ruined!"

As Epictetus famously put it: "Men are disturbed not by things, but by the view which they take of them." If you eliminate the judgment, the suffering vanishes. You cannot control that the rain fell; you can completely control whether you curse the sky or put on a raincoat.

3. Antipater of Tarsus and the Archer’s Paradox

To explain how a Stoic can passionately pursue goals in the external world without becoming emotionally destroyed by failure, the Stoic philosopher Antipater of Tarsus introduced the classical Metaphor of the Archer (later analyzed by Cicero in De Finibus).

Consider an archer preparing to shoot an arrow at a target:

  • What is within the archer’s control? Selecting a straight arrow, stringing the bow with care, drawing the bowstring with disciplined strength, calculating the distance, aiming with razor-sharp concentration, and releasing the arrow cleanly. The archer can execute every single one of these steps with flawless, absolute perfection.
  • What is outside the archer’s control? The instant the arrow leaves the bowstring, the archer's control drops to exactly zero. A sudden gust of wind can blow across the field; a stray bird can fly into the arrow's path; or the target (a deer) can take a step to the left.

A non-Stoic makes hitting the target their sole metric of success: if the wind blows the arrow off course, they scream, curse their luck, and feel humiliated.

A Stoic makes the flawless release of the arrow their sole metric of success. If the arrow flies true and a sudden gale blows it aside, the Stoic remains completely calm: they did everything humanly possible within their sovereignty. In life, business, and leadership, your duty is to prepare rigorously, act with moral excellence, and shoot the arrow with mastery. Whether the external world lets the arrow hit the bullseye is an "indifferent" external variable (adiaphoron).

4. Empirical and Historical Case Studies

Case A: The Stockdale Paradox and the Hanoi Hilton

In his business classic Good to Great, researcher Jim Collins interviewed Admiral James Stockdale about his survival in the North Vietnamese prison camp. Collins asked Stockdale a direct question: "Who didn't make it out?"

Stockdale replied without hesitation: "Oh, that's easy. The optimists."

Collins was stunned: wasn't optimism the supreme virtue of survival? Stockdale explained the devastating cognitive trap of naive optimism:

"The optimists were the ones who said, 'We're going to be out by Christmas.' And Christmas would come, and Christmas would go. Then they'd say, 'We're going to be out by Easter.' And Easter would come, and Easter would go. And then Thanksgiving, and then it would be Christmas again. And they died of a broken heart."

Stockdale articulated what Collins immortalized as the Stockdale Paradox:

"You must never confuse faith that you will prevail in the end—which you can never afford to lose—with the discipline to confront the most brutal facts of your current reality, whatever they might be."

— Admiral James Stockdale, quoted in Jim Collins' Good to Great

The naive optimist tethers their emotional stability to an external timeline they cannot control (being released by Christmas). When reality contradicts their timeline, their spirit shatters. The Stoic, by contrast, embraces the brutal reality of today, focuses exclusively on surviving the next hour with honor, and maintains an unshakeable long-term commitment to prevail.

Case B: Viktor Frankl and the Ultimate Human Freedom in Auschwitz

In 1942, Austrian neurologist and psychiatrist Viktor Frankl was arrested by the Nazi regime and transported to the concentration camps of Theresienstadt, Auschwitz, and Dachau. His parents, brother, and pregnant wife were murdered in the gas chambers.

Stripped of his clothes, his shoes, his medical manuscripts, and every physical possession, shaved of all hair, living in freezing barracks surrounded by electric barbed wire and crematorium smoke, Frankl discovered the modern empirical proof of Epictetus’s Dichotomy of Control.

In his transcendent 1946 masterpiece, Man's Search for Meaning, Frankl wrote:

"Everything can be taken from a man but one thing: the last of the human freedoms—to choose one's attitude in any given set of circumstances, to choose one's own way... Even though conditions such as lack of sleep, insufficient food and various mental stresses may suggest that the inmates were bound to react in certain ways, in the final analysis it becomes clear that the sort of person the prisoner became was the result of an inner decision, and not the result of camp influences alone."

— Viktor E. Frankl, Man's Search for Meaning

Frankl observed that even in the ultimate hell of Auschwitz, some prisoners walked through the huts comforting others, giving away their last piece of bread. They proved that no external tyrant, no barbed-wire enclosure, and no corporate boss can ever force a human being to surrender their internal freedom of response. Between stimulus and response, there is a space; in that space lies our power to choose our response; and in that response lies our growth and our freedom.

5. Actionable Heuristics for Practicing Everyday Stoicism

  1. The "Two-Column" Cognitive Triage: Whenever you feel surging anxiety, anger, or overwhelm, open a notebook and draw a vertical line down the middle of the page:
    • Left Column (Outside My Control): The client’s decision, the macroeconomic interest rate, the airline flight delay, what a colleague whispered in a meeting, the medical test result. Look at this column and say: "This is none of my business; it is not up to me."
    • Right Column (Within My Sovereignty): My preparation, my physical posture, my honesty, my tone of voice, my work ethic today, my kindness to others. Look at this column and say: "This is 100 percent my responsibility." Spend all your cognitive energy here.
  2. Practice Morning *Premeditatio Malorum* (The Premeditation of Evils): Every morning before opening your email or leaving your house, spend three minutes mentally rehearsing the frictions of the day. As Marcus Aurelius instructed himself: "When you wake up in the morning, tell yourself: The people I deal with today will be meddling, ungrateful, arrogant, dishonest, jealous, and surly. They are like this because they cannot distinguish good from evil. But I have seen the beauty of good, and I cannot be harmed by any of them." By expecting friction, you neutralize the shock when it occurs.
  3. The Archer’s Shift: Focus on Input, Detach from Outcome: When launching a startup, applying for a dream job, or pitching an investor, shift your internal reward mechanism. Evaluate yourself exclusively on whether you delivered a world-class pitch, prepared with rigor, and behaved with absolute integrity (the release of the arrow). If they say no, accept it with complete equanimity: the wind blew the arrow; your virtue remains intact.
  4. Embrace *Amor Fati* (Love of Fate): Do not merely tolerate adversity; lean into it as the essential raw material for character. Friedrich Nietzsche and the Roman Stoics championed *Amor Fati*: whatever happens—a canceled contract, an injury, a sudden betrayal—do not whine. Say: "This is exactly what I needed to test my patience, my creativity, and my resilience." The impediment to action advances action. What stands in the way becomes the way.

6. Guided Self-Reflection

Examine your mental resilience through the lens of Stoic philosophy:

  • What external outcome am I currently obsessing over that is completely outside my direct sovereign control?
  • How much emotional energy am I squandering each day trying to manage or alter the opinions of people who do not matter to me?
  • When an unexpected setback occurs, do I waste hours complaining about the injustice of the world, or do I immediately ask: 'What does virtue require of me right now?'
  • If I were stripped of my job, my wealth, and my status tomorrow, what remains of my character that no external catastrophe can touch?
