The Many Meanings of Consciousness

The word 'consciousness' is a treacherous intellectual umbrella that shelters fundamentally distinct phenomena under a single label. Untangling the raw, felt experience of reality from the functional processing of information is the first essential step toward understanding mind, machine, and human identity.

In the neurology department at Oxford University during the 1970s, British neuropsychologist Lawrence Weiskrantz was examining an extraordinary patient designated in medical literature as D.B. Due to a necessary surgical procedure to remove a vascular malformation, D.B. had lost the primary visual cortex (Brodmann area 17, or V1) on the right side of his brain. As a result, D.B. was completely, clinically blind in his left visual field. If an experimenter held up a bright red apple, flashed a strobe light, or pointed a finger inside his blind field, D.B. adamantly insisted that he saw absolutely nothing: to his conscious awareness, that quadrant of reality was pure, empty darkness. Yet Weiskrantz conducted an uncanny experiment: he asked D.B. to reach out his hand and point toward a spot of light flashed inside the blind field. D.B. protested that the request was ridiculous: "How can I point to something I cannot see? I would just be guessing blindly." Weiskrantz urged him to guess anyway. D.B. sighed, shrugged, and reached out his hand. To the astonishment of the researchers, D.B. pointed to the exact location of the invisible light with uncanny accuracy. In subsequent trials, when asked whether an invisible line was oriented vertically or horizontally, D.B. guessed correctly over 90 percent of the time, while insisting throughout that he had felt no visual sensation whatsoever. The discovery of this condition—coined by Weiskrantz as Blindsight—sent shockwaves through philosophy and cognitive neuroscience. It proved empirically that the brain can process complex sensory information, guide physical motor action, and perform cognitive calculations completely divorced from the subjective, felt experience of awareness. Consciousness is not a single, monolithic switch; it is a tapestry of multiple, dissociable systems.

1. Ned Block: Phenomenal vs. Access Consciousness

In 1995, New York University philosopher Ned Block published a landmark paper in Behavioral and Brain Sciences titled "On a Confusion About a Function of Consciousness." Block observed that discussions of consciousness in neuroscience, artificial intelligence, and everyday conversation are chronically derailed because people use the word to refer to two fundamentally different dimensions of the mind:

  1. Phenomenal Consciousness (P-Consciousness): The raw, subjective, qualitative feeling of experience—what philosophers call qualia. It is the redness of red, the stinging burn of a paper cut, the warm taste of coffee, the ache of heartbreak, or the sensation of hearing middle C on an oboe. P-consciousness is experience itself: what it feels like from the inside to be you right now. If an entity has no subjective feelings—if the lights are completely off on the inside—it lacks phenomenal consciousness.
  2. Access Consciousness (A-Consciousness): The functional, computational availability of information for use in reasoning, speech, behavioral control, and long-term memory. A mental state is access-conscious if its information is globally broadcast across the brain’s "global workspace," making it available to executive decision-making centers. If you can report information verbally, use it to calculate an answer, or adjust your path to avoid an obstacle, that information is access-conscious.

The patient with blindsight possesses Access Consciousness without Phenomenal Consciousness: the visual coordinates of the light are computationally available to his motor planning centers, allowing his hand to point accurately, yet the qualitative sensation of seeing (the qualia of light) is entirely absent.

Conversely, consider when you are reading an absorbing book while sitting near a grandfather clock. The clock ticks steadily in the background sixty times a minute. While absorbed in the novel, you do not consciously notice the clock. Suddenly, the clock stops ticking. You instantly look up and realize: "The clock stopped ticking!" You were experiencing the sound all along (P-consciousness), but until it stopped, it was not being processed by your executive verbal centers (A-consciousness).

2. Thomas Nagel: "What Is It Like to Be a Bat?"

In 1974, philosopher Thomas Nagel published one of the most famous essays in modern philosophy of mind in The Philosophical Review: "What Is It Like to Be a Bat?"

Nagel delivered the definitive, irreducible definition of subjective consciousness:

"An organism has conscious mental states if and only if there is something that it is like to be that organism—something it is like for the organism."

— Thomas Nagel, What Is It Like to Be a Bat? (1974)

Nagel asked the reader to consider the mind of a bat. A bat is a mammal; it possesses an advanced mammalian nervous system, sleeps, feeds, and navigates. But its primary sensory apparatus is echolocation: it emits rapid, high-frequency ultrasonic clicks and navigates through the reflected acoustic echoes bouncing off flying insects and cave walls.

You can study the neuroanatomy of the bat's auditory cortex with complete perfection; you can build computer simulations of its sonar reception; you can spend a lifetime mapping its neural firing patterns. Yet you will never know what it feels like from the inside to perceive the three-dimensional world through sonar echoes.

Imagining yourself with wings and webbing hanging upside down in a dark cave does not tell you what it is like for a bat; it merely tells you what it would feel like for a human being pretending to be a bat! Nagel demonstrated that objective physical science, which strives for a "view from nowhere" by stripping away subjective perspectives, is structurally incapable of capturing the purely subjective point of view that constitutes conscious experience.

3. The Global Workspace and the Spotlight of Attention

In cognitive neuroscience, Bernard Baars and Stanislas Dehaene formulated Global Workspace Theory (GWT) to map how access consciousness functions in the biological brain.

The brain is not a single unified computer; it is a massive, decentralized network of millions of specialized, unconscious modules operating in parallel: language processors, face-recognition modules, motor controllers, and autonomic regulators.

Baars utilized the metaphor of a theatre:

  • The vast majority of the theatre is shrouded in pitch-black darkness: this is the vast, teeming unconscious processing of the brain (retrieving memories, parsing grammar, balancing your posture, pumping blood).
  • On the stage, there is a narrow, brilliant spotlight of attention. Whatever information is illuminated by this spotlight is broadcast globally across the entire neural network.
  • When information enters the global workspace, all specialized modules can access it simultaneously: your motor cortex can plan an escape, your vocal cords can shout a warning, and your amygdala can trigger adrenaline. That global broadcasting is the biological mechanism of Access Consciousness.

4. Empirical and Historical Case Studies

Case A: The Highway Hypnosis Automaton

Consider a universal human experience known to every experienced automobile driver: Highway Hypnosis.

You are driving home late at night along a familiar stretch of highway. Your mind becomes completely absorbed in an intense, emotional conversation with a passenger, or in deep reflection on a complex problem at work.

Twenty minutes later, you suddenly "wake up" to your surroundings as you pull onto your residential street. You realize with a shock that you have zero conscious recollection of the last fifteen miles: you cannot remember stopping at red lights, changing lanes, or adjusting your speed for curves.

Who was driving the car? It was not an incompetent zombie: during those fifteen miles, your brain processed thousands of visual signals, calculated braking distances, stayed centered between painted lane lines, and avoided collisions. Your brain operated with magnificent, high-dimensional computational mastery completely below the threshold of executive access consciousness. You were an autonomous, biological driving robot. This everyday phenomenon proves that complex, goal-directed, highly intelligent behavior does not require continuous phenomenal awareness.

Case B: Large Language Models and the Chinese Room Paradox

In the 2020s, the rise of advanced Large Language Models (such as GPT-4 and Gemini) ignited ferocious public debate: Are these machines becoming conscious?

Untangling the meanings of consciousness resolves the confusion instantly:

  • In terms of Access / Functional Capability: These models possess astonishing informational access: they can synthesize quantum physics, write sonnets in French, diagnose rare medical conditions, and generate executable computer code across dozens of languages.
  • In terms of Phenomenal Consciousness (Qualia): There is zero reason to believe there is "anything that it is like" to be an LLM. When an AI writes a moving paragraph about the sorrow of grief, it feels no sorrow; when it describes the warmth of a sunset, it experiences no light. It is executing matrix multiplications on silicon microchips, predicting the next statistical token in a sequence. It is a masterwork of informational access that operates in total phenomenal darkness.

Confusing computational output with phenomenal sentience is the supreme anthropomorphic blunder of the digital age.

5. Actionable Heuristics for Navigating Conscious Experience

  1. The "Phenomenal vs. Functional" Diagnostic: Whenever someone discusses consciousness—in animals, infants, artificial intelligence, or brain-injured patients—immediately split the term: "Are we discussing the functional capacity to process data and control behavior (Access Consciousness), or the subjective, felt experience from the inside (Phenomenal Consciousness)?" This single question eliminates 90 percent of semantic confusion.
  2. The "Bat Screen" for Human Empathy: Remember Thomas Nagel’s insight: you cannot directly experience what it feels like to be another person. When a colleague, partner, or friend describes an emotional pain, an experience of discrimination, or a sensory sensitivity (such as neurodivergent sensory overload), never say: "That doesn't make sense; it wouldn't bother me." Respect the irreducible reality of their unique subjective qualia.
  3. Reclaim Phenomenal Presence from Autopilot: Modern digital life constantly drags human attention into unconscious functional processing: scrolling feeds, answering emails, and rushing between tasks like a highway hypnosis automaton. Practice intentional sensory check-ins throughout the day: pause for thirty seconds, set down your phone, and experience pure qualia—the physical weight of your feet on the floor, the temperature of the air entering your nostrils, the ambient texture of sound. Shift from computational doing into conscious being.
  4. Audit Subconscious Priming: Recognize that your conscious executive mind is merely the small spotlight on the stage; the vast theatre of your brain is constantly being influenced by unconscious environmental cues. If you surround yourself with toxic news feeds, cynical colleagues, or cluttered workspaces, your unconscious modules will steer your emotions long before your conscious mind realizes it has been hijacked.

6. Guided Self-Reflection

Examine the textures of your own mind through the lens of consciousness studies:

  • How much of my typical day is spent in "highway hypnosis"—performing tasks on automatic habit with zero phenomenal presence?
  • What is the raw, qualitative 'feel' (qualia) of my current emotional baseline right now, stripped of all analytical storytelling?
  • Am I attributing human-like subjective consciousness to digital tools simply because they produce articulate sentences?
  • When was the last time I paused to marvel at the sheer, miraculous fact that there is 'something that it is like' to be alive?
