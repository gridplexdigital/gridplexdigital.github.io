Dataism and the Shift from Human Feelings to Algorithms

For centuries, humanism crowned the human heart as the supreme authority: feelings, voter choices, and consumer preferences were sacred. The emerging religion of Dataism views human beings merely as biochemical data-processing algorithms, paving the way for authority to migrate from inner intuition to external digital networks.

On May 14, 2013, the acclaimed Academy Award-winning actress Angelina Jolie published an op-ed in The New York Times titled "My Medical Choice" that sent shockwaves across global culture. Jolie announced that she had just completed a preventive double mastectomy, surgically removing both of her healthy breasts, and was preparing to have her ovaries and fallopian tubes removed as well. What stunned the world was not merely the gravity of her physical surgery, but the reason behind it: Jolie was not sick. She had no lumps, no physical pain, no malignant tumors, and no clinical symptoms whatsoever. When she looked into the mirror and listened to her own body, her feelings told her that she was in the prime of life, vibrant, radiant, and perfectly healthy. Yet she chose to undergo radical, irreversible anatomical surgery. Why? Because she had taken a genetic test that sequenced her DNA, and an automated predictive algorithm had identified a dangerous, rare mutation in her BRCA1 gene. The computer model calculated that she faced an estimated 87 percent statistical probability of developing breast cancer and a 50 percent risk of ovarian cancer. In that moment of decision, Angelina Jolie did not "listen to her heart"; she did not consult her subjective inner feelings; she did not pray to a deity. She deferred absolute, life-altering authority to a computational algorithm. In Homo Deus: A Brief History of Tomorrow, Yuval Noah Harari identifies this moment as the turning of an epoch: the dawn of Dataism. For three centuries, the reigning religion of Western civilization was Humanism, which taught that human feelings, intuition, and free will were the ultimate source of moral authority. In the twenty-first century, authority is silently, irreversibly migrating from the human heart to external digital algorithms.

1. The Humanist Creed vs. The Dataist Creed

To understand the magnitude of the Dataist revolution, one must examine the intellectual religion it is quietly replacing: Liberal Humanism.

Throughout the pre-modern era, authority resided in God, scriptures, and cosmic laws: if you wanted to know what was true, moral, or beautiful, you consulted the priest, read the Bible, or obeyed the king.

The Humanist revolution of the eighteenth century overturned this hierarchy, placing the sovereign human individual at the center of the universe. Humanism declared that human subjective experience was the supreme authority:

  • In Politics: The voter knows best. Democracy is not based on who has the highest IQ, but on the subjective feelings of individual citizens pulling levers in private voting booths.
  • In Economics: The customer is always right. Free markets are governed by individual consumer preferences and desire.
  • In Ethics: "If it feels good, do it; if it harms no one, it is permissible." Moral truth is found by listening to your inner conscience.
  • In Art: Beauty is in the eye of the beholder. There are no absolute aesthetic rules; what moves human feelings is art.

For centuries, humanism reigned supreme because human feelings were the best data-processing algorithm on earth for making life decisions. But contemporary life sciences and computer science have combined to forge a brand-new, radical philosophy: Dataism.

"Dataism declares that the universe consists of data flows, and the value of any phenomenon or entity is determined by its contribution to data-processing... Dataism collapses the barrier between animals and machines, and expects electronic algorithms to eventually decipher and outperform biochemical algorithms."
— Yuval Noah Harari, Homo Deus: A Brief History of Tomorrow (2016)

2. Humans as Biochemical Algorithms

The intellectual bridge connecting computer science to evolutionary biology is a single revolutionary premise: organisms are algorithms.

An algorithm is simply a methodical set of steps used to perform calculations, resolve problems, and make decisions (e.g., a recipe for baking a cake, or a sorting script in Python).

Modern evolutionary biology demonstrates that when a baboon spots a lion in the grass and decides whether to sprint for a tree or freeze, the baboon is not executing mystical, magical free will. The baboon’s brain is executing a complex biochemical algorithm shaped by millions of years of natural selection:

The Biochemical Decision Algorithm

  • Sensory retinas register the visual photons of moving fur and transmit signals to the visual cortex.
  • The amygdala calculates threat levels, releasing surges of adrenaline and cortisol into the bloodstream.
  • Neural networks calculate the distance to the nearest tree, the velocity of the predator, and the metabolic energy reserves of the muscles in fractions of a second.
  • The subjective sensation that the baboon experiences is what we call "fear." Fear is not an incorporeal spiritual substance; fear is the biological sensation of millions of neurons calculating survival probabilities.

Here is the decisive Dataist syllogism: If human beings are biochemical algorithms, and if algorithms are simply information-processing systems, then there is no fundamental ontological difference between a carbon-based neural network and a silicon-based electronic neural network.

Furthermore, a silicon algorithm operating on a cloud supercomputer has two colossal advantages over human biology:

  1. It has access to billions of times more data (reading all medical journals, tracking every credit card swipe, analyzing millions of genomes).
  2. It is free from biological cognitive biases, fatigue, emotional hysteria, and hormonal spikes.

Therefore, an external algorithm can know you better than you know yourself.

3. The Decoupling of Intelligence from Consciousness

For millions of years of mammalian evolution, high intelligence always went hand-in-hand with subjective consciousness. A creature that possessed high intelligence—capable of stalking prey, recognizing allies, or solving puzzles—invariably possessed subjective feelings: fear, anger, joy, and pain.

Humanism assumed that because computers are cold, unfeeling, non-conscious machines, they could never match or exceed human performance in tasks that require empathy, intuition, and holistic understanding.

Harari reveals the profound, existential blind spot of modern humanism: intelligence is decoupling from consciousness.

"We are facing the prospect of non-conscious algorithms outperforming conscious human beings across an ever-widening array of high-stakes tasks: diagnosing diseases, driving vehicles, trading securities, detecting fraud, and predicting military conflicts. To accomplish these tasks, a machine does not need to feel joy or pain; it only needs to calculate probabilities with superhuman speed."
— Yuval Noah Harari, Homo Deus (2016)

When an AI system plays chess or Go (such as DeepMind's AlphaGo), it does not experience the thrill of victory or the agony of defeat. It does not feel nervous before a move. Yet it defeated the greatest human masters on earth. To fly an autonomous passenger jet, negotiate a high-frequency stock trade, or identify an early tumor in a mammogram, consciousness is completely unnecessary; intelligence is all that matters. As society demands greater efficiency, non-conscious algorithms will systematically displace conscious humans from economic power.

4. Everyday Dataism: How Waze Conquered Human Intuition

You do not need to look to futuristic genetic engineering to witness Dataism in action; you need only observe how human beings navigate a city today using Waze or Google Maps.

A generation ago, driving from one side of a sprawling metropolis like London, New York, or Mumbai required subjective human judgment: a driver looked at paper street maps, recalled past experiences, checked the weather, sniffed the air, and used their personal "gut intuition" to choose a route.

Today, millions of drivers turn on their smartphones, type in an address, and listen to the mechanical voice of the navigation app with religious devotion.

The Waze Epistemological Shift

Suppose you are driving down a familiar, wide four-lane boulevard. The sun is shining, the road ahead looks completely clear and open to your visual eyes. Suddenly, Waze instructs you: "In 200 feet, turn right into a narrow, obscure, cobblestone alleyway."

Your personal subjective consciousness screams: "That's absurd! Why would I turn off a wide, empty avenue into a cramped residential alley?"

Yet what do 99 percent of drivers do? They obey the voice and turn right.

Why do you obey? Because you recognize that your individual conscious eyes see only the 200 meters of road immediately in front of your windshield. The Waze algorithm, by contrast, is monitoring real-time GPS telemetry from 50,000 other vehicles across the entire city: it knows that two miles ahead, a garbage truck has blocked the avenue; it knows that five minutes ago, an accident occurred at the highway intersection.

You have surrendered your cognitive sovereignty to the external network. You no longer trust your own eyes; you trust the algorithm. Multiply this dynamic across healthcare, dating, investing, education, and career choices, and you have the complete migration from Humanism to Dataism.

5. The Ultimate Dataist Frontier: Algorithmic Romance and Career

The humanist novel and cinema spent two centuries celebrating the romantic ideal of following your heart: the young woman choosing between two suitors (Pride and Prejudice), struggling to decipher her deepest, authentic love.

In a Dataist world, relying on your fluttering heart to choose a lifelong spouse is viewed as dangerous, obsolete madness. Why? Because the human heart is notoriously neurotic, easily fooled by perfume, temporary biological lust, evolutionary baggage, and childhood trauma.

Consider what an advanced AI companion (such as a future evolution of ChatGPT or Apple Health) could do:

  • It has monitored your biometric data for twenty years: your heart-rate variability, cortisol levels, galvanic skin response, sleep patterns, and pupil dilation during thousands of conversations.
  • It has read every email you have ever written, monitored every movie you cried at, and tracked every purchase you have made.
  • It has analyzed identical multi-decade behavioral databases of millions of other individuals.

When you ask the algorithm: "Should I marry Alex or Jordan?" the algorithm will reply: "Well, based on your physiological stress markers, when you are with Alex, your blood pressure rises 14%, and your dopamine spikes initially but crashes within two hours; whereas with Jordan, your oxytocin levels remain elevated and your emotional stability is 32% higher. Furthermore, across two million couples with your exact psychological profiles, marriages with people like Jordan have an 84% thirty-year survival rate, compared to 21% for Alex. My recommendation: choose Jordan."

At that moment, the central premise of humanism—that each human being is a unique, mysterious soul who alone knows what they truly feel—evaporates. The algorithm knows who you are far better than your confused, mortal consciousness ever could.

6. Core Heuristics for Preserving the Human Sanctuary in a Dataist Age

1. The "Algorithmic Advisory, Human Sovereignty" Rule

Use algorithms as brilliant analytical advisers, but never surrender final moral sovereignty to them. An algorithm can calculate statistical probabilities, predict financial risks, and flag medical anomalies with superhuman precision. But an algorithm cannot feel grief, cannot experience love, and cannot bear moral responsibility for a tragedy. Retain human agency as the final, inviolable checkpoint.

2. Protect "Unmeasured" Spaces of Human Life

Resist the obsessive Dataist urge to quantify every breath, step, heartbeat, and thought (the Quantified Self trap). Keep portions of your life completely unmeasured and offline: go for walks without an Apple Watch counting your calories; write in physical paper journals that cannot be ingested by large language models; have long, meandering conversations with friends without tracking productivity outcomes. Value exists in things that cannot be reduced to data points.

3. Audit the "Implicit Utility Function" of the Machine

Whenever an algorithm gives you a recommendation—whether YouTube suggesting the next video, Spotify suggesting music, or LinkedIn suggesting a career move—immediately ask: "What is the hidden utility function this algorithm is optimizing for?" Spotify does not optimize for your spiritual enlightenment; it optimizes for user retention and streaming licensing costs. Google does not optimize for your mental peace; it optimizes for advertising conversions. Never confuse the platform’s business model with your soul’s flourishing.

4. Reclaim Epistemic Navigation (Turn Off the Screen)

Periodically practice navigating physical space, intellectual problems, and relational dilemmas without digital assistance. Turn off GPS and navigate a new neighborhood using visual landmarks and asking local citizens for directions; solve a complex organizational problem using a whiteboard and conversation before running an automated software tool. Preserving your cognitive sensorimotor independence keeps your biological neural networks resilient.

7. Self-Diagnostic Checklist: Auditing Your Algorithmic Dependence

  • Do I trust my own physical bodily signals (hunger, fatigue, stress), or do I look at my smartwatch to tell me how I slept and whether I am exhausted?
  • When deciding what to read, watch, or listen to, do I actively seek out challenging works, or do I passively consume whatever an algorithmic recommendation feed serves me?
  • Can I make an important personal or professional decision without frantically polling online forums, review aggregators, and automated tools?
  • Do I treat human emotions and moral intuitions as inconvenient biological glitches to be optimized away, or as the sacred core of what makes life worth living?
  • Am I living as a sovereign, self-determining human soul, or as an organic sensor node feeding data into a planetary corporate cloud?
