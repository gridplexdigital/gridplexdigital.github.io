Why Some Countries Become Wealthy

The staggering divergence in global wealth is not the consequence of racial destiny, cultural superiority, or pure geographic fate. Long-run prosperity is the compounding product of inclusive institutions, state fiscal capacity, technology diffusion, human capital, and the freedom of creative destruction.

Walk along International Street in the city of Nogales. If you stand on the north side of the border fence in Nogales, Arizona, the average household income exceeds $30,000 per year; the vast majority of teenagers graduate from high school; paved roads are maintained by the municipal government; public tap water is clean and potable; emergency medical services arrive within minutes; property titles are recorded in secure county databases; and citizens participate in free elections without fear of cartel extortion. Now take fifty steps south and cross the steel border fence into Nogales, Sonora, in Mexico. The geographic terrain is identical; the rainfall is identical; the native desert vegetation is identical; the baseline disease environment is identical; and the majority of residents on both sides share identical ethnic ancestry, Spanish surnames, culinary traditions, and family ties. Yet on the southern side of the fence, household income is less than one-third of that to the north; public roads are unpaved; running water is contaminated; drug cartels intimidate local businesses; property titles are precarious and prone to bureaucratic expropriation; and infant mortality is significantly higher. How can fifty feet of desert soil create an economic chasm of several hundred percent between identical human beings? The answer exposes the core engine of global wealth: it is not geography, culture, or DNA that dictates prosperity, but the institutional architecture governing human effort.

1. The Malthusian Trap and the Great Escape: Angus Deaton

For virtually all of recorded human history—from the dawn of agriculture in the Fertile Crescent ten thousand years ago until the late eighteenth century—humanity lived trapped in what economists call the Malthusian Trap.

Whenever a society invented a better plow, discovered a new crop, or cleared fertile land, the resulting caloric surplus led to temporary population growth. But because technology improved at a glacial pace, population growth quickly outstripped the food supply. Per capita income crashed back to subsistence levels, punctuated by famine, epidemic plagues, and brutal warfare.

An ordinary peasant living in the Roman Empire under Marcus Aurelius in 170 CE enjoyed roughly the exact same standard of living, caloric intake, and life expectancy (roughly thirty to thirty-five years) as a peasant living in Tudor England in 1500 or Qing China in 1750.

"For thousands of years, those who were lucky enough to escape death in childhood grew up to face lives that were short and hard. The Great Escape is the story of how mankind broke free from that trap, leading to dramatic declines in poverty, unprecedented gains in longevity, and the explosion of human flourishing."

Angus Deaton, The Great Escape: Health, Wealth, and the Origins of Inequality (2013)
Nobel Laureate in Economics

Then, around 1780 in northwestern Europe, something unprecedented happened: economic growth decoupled from population growth. Output per person began to compound year after year.

Over the next two centuries, the fraction of the global population living in extreme subsistence poverty collapsed from roughly 90 percent down to under 10 percent today, while global life expectancy doubled. Economists call this explosive departure The Great Escape. But why did it happen in certain societies while leaving others behind, generating the immense gap between rich and poor nations?

2. Rival Theories of Prosperity: Geography vs. Culture vs. Institutions

Over the past century, three primary schools of thought have competed to explain why wealth clustered in specific corners of the globe:

1. The Geographic Hypothesis (Jared Diamond, Jeffrey Sachs)

Proponents argue that nature dealt an unequal hand. In Guns, Germs, and Steel, Jared Diamond argued that the East-West axis of Eurasia allowed crops, livestock, and technologies to spread rapidly across similar latitudes, giving Eurasians a massive head start over the North-South axes of the Americas and Africa.

Economist Jeffrey Sachs pointed to the burden of tropical diseases (malaria, yellow fever, schistosomiasis) and poor tropical soils, arguing that landlocked countries with tropical ecologies face intrinsic geographic penalties.

The Limitation: Geography cannot explain why identical geographic terrains diverge radically: Nogales North vs. Nogales South, East Germany vs. West Germany, or North Korea vs. South Korea. Geography is real, but institutions are decisive.

2. The Cultural Hypothesis (Max Weber, David Landes)

Max Weber famously argued in 1905 that the 'Protestant Ethic'—emphasizing hard work, thrift, literacy, and worldly vocation—fueled the rise of northern European capitalism. Cultural historians like David Landes suggested that cultural attitudes toward science, curiosity, and individual achievement explain economic dynamism.

The Limitation: Culture changes with breathtaking speed when incentives change. In the 1950s, Western economists routinely argued that 'Confucian culture' with its filial piety and reverence for tradition was fundamentally incompatible with modern capitalism. Within three decades, Confucian Taiwan, South Korea, Hong Kong, and Singapore engineered the fastest industrial growth in human history. Culture adapts to institutional opportunity.

3. The Institutional Hypothesis (Acemoglu, Robinson, North)

The dominant consensus in modern development economics: economic prosperity depends on inclusive political and economic institutions that enforce secure property rights for the broad population, maintain an impartial legal system, provide public goods (education, transport, sanitation), and allow the creative destruction of new technologies without elite vetoes.

3. The Mechanics of the Industrial Breakthrough: Robert Allen

Why did the Industrial Revolution occur in eighteenth-century Great Britain rather than in wealthy Qing China, advanced France, or commercial Holland? Economic historian Robert Allen solved this puzzle in The British Industrial Revolution in Global Perspective through the economics of relative factor prices:

The High-Wage, Cheap-Energy Engine

In the eighteenth century, Great Britain was a unique economic anomaly:

  • Exceptionally High Wages: British workers enjoyed the highest real wages in the world, driven by London's booming global mercantile trade.
  • Exceptionally Cheap Energy: Britain sat atop vast, shallow, accessible coal deposits right next to navigable rivers and canals.

In Qing China or France, labor was dirt cheap and coal was expensive. If an inventor built an early steam engine or a mechanical spinning jenny—which was clumsy, broke down frequently, and required huge amounts of fuel—a Chinese or French factory owner had zero incentive to buy it. It was far cheaper to simply hire fifty peasant laborers to spin by hand.

In Britain, however, because labor was expensive and coal was virtually free, investing immense capital to mechanize paid for itself. The British invented and deployed labor-saving, fuel-hungry machinery because it was economically profitable to do so in Britain, and nowhere else. Once the technology was deployed, learning-by-doing improved its efficiency until steam engines became so productive that they conquered the globe.

4. Natural Experiments in Economic History

Case Study: North Korea vs. South Korea (The 38th Parallel)

In 1945, at the conclusion of World War II, the Korean peninsula was divided along the 38th parallel. Prior to the partition, the northern half of Korea was actually the more industrialized, resource-rich region, possessing major hydroelectric dams, mineral deposits, and chemical plants, while the southern half was an impoverished agrarian hinterland.

Both halves shared identical language, identical genetic ancestry, identical thousands of years of shared cultural history, and identical geography.

Yet the two nations adopted opposing institutional architectures:

  • North Korea: Instituted a totalitarian command economy under Kim Il-sung. Private property was abolished; markets were outlawed; agriculture was collectivized; independent enterprise was punished as treason; and the entire society was organized to extract resources for the military and royal family.
  • South Korea: Despite enduring decades of authoritarian military regimes, South Korea established private property rights, integrated into global trade markets, heavily subsidized universal human capital (investing aggressively in public primary, secondary, and STEM education), and eventually democratized.

By 2020, South Korea had become a member of the OECD, boasting an average income per capita exceeding $35,000, world-leading semiconductor and automotive conglomerates, and a vibrant cultural economy. North Korea suffered periodic famines that killed hundreds of thousands, while its GDP per capita remained frozen under $1,500—less than 5 percent of its southern neighbor. At night, satellite imagery reveals the 38th parallel in stark visual terms: South Korea blazes with electrical light, while North Korea is an abyss of total blackness.

5. The Synthesis: The Five Pillars of National Prosperity

Synthesizing two centuries of development economics, enduring national wealth requires the simultaneous compounding of five distinct engines:

1. Impartial Rule of Law and Contract Enforcement

No society becomes wealthy without courts that enforce contracts between unrelated strangers. When property can be seized by a tyrant, a bureaucrat, or a cartel, citizens do not invest; they hide capital abroad or hoard gold.

2. State Fiscal Capacity and Public Goods

Low-tax, zero-state libertarian paradises do not exist in the real world. Every wealthy nation has high state capacity: the power to collect taxes without rampant corruption and invest those revenues in public infrastructure—clean drinking water, public sanitation, transport grids, electrical networks, and epidemic disease control. As Angus Deaton proved, clean water and sanitation contributed more to human life expectancy and workforce productivity than modern pharmaceuticals.

3. Universal Human Capital Investment

Prosperity requires that the vast majority of the population—including women and marginalized minorities—possesses high literacy, numeracy, and technical training. A nation that excludes half its talent pool from the workforce operates with one hand tied behind its back.

4. Integration with Global Markets and Specialization

Autarky and radical protectionism breed stagnation. Rich countries trade with the world, importing capital goods and technologies while specializing in high-value complex exports (the Economic Complexity Index pioneered by Ricardo Hausmann and César Hidalgo).

5. Freedom of Creative Destruction

As Joseph Schumpeter recognized, capitalism is an evolutionary process. New technologies must be allowed to obsolete old technologies. When governments protect legacy taxi companies, obsolete coal mines, or entrenched state monopolies from disruptive competitors, economic growth grinds to a halt.

6. Guided Self-Reflection

Reflect on your personal relationship to economic opportunity and systemic capital:

DIAGNOSTIC INQUIRIES

1. The Geographic Lottery Audit: How much of your current personal income and safety is a reflection of your individual grit and intellect, versus the immense unearned tailwind of being born in, or moving to, a nation with functioning water, electricity, courts, and markets?

2. The Creative Destruction Test: When a new technology (like generative AI or digital automation) threatens your current job skills or business model, is your first instinct to demand regulatory protection (extraction), or to master the new tool to deliver superior value (inclusive growth)?

3. The Public Goods Stewardship: In what ways do you actively contribute to the institutional infrastructure of your community (paying taxes with integrity, serving on school boards, mentoring young talent), rather than acting as a purely extractive consumer?

Further Reading and Primary Sources

To master development economics, macro-history, and the origins of global prosperity, read these foundational works:

  • Daron Acemoglu & James A. Robinson, Why Nations Fail: The Origins of Power, Prosperity, and Poverty (Crown, 2012). The premier institutional explanation of global inequality.
  • Angus Deaton, The Great Escape: Health, Wealth, and the Origins of Inequality (Princeton University Press, 2013). Nobel laureate analysis of health, longevity, and economic escape.
  • Robert C. Allen, The British Industrial Revolution in Global Perspective (Cambridge University Press, 2009). The economic analysis of high wages, cheap coal, and technology adoption.
  • Douglass C. North, Structure and Change in Economic History (W. W. Norton & Co., 1981). The classic foundational text on transaction costs and property rights.
