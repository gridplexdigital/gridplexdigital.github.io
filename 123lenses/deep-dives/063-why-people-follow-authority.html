Why People Follow Authority

Legitimate authority coordinates human effort, transmits specialized expertise, and preserves civil order. Yet symbolic trappings of hierarchy trigger an alarming psychological reflex: the abdication of individual moral agency and critical thought before perceived superiors.

📖 11-Minute Deep Read (2,118 words) ⚡ Short on time? Switch to the 2-minute field note summary →

In the spring of 1966, psychiatrist Charles Hofling and his colleagues orchestrated an extraordinary, covert field experiment across twenty-two separate hospital wards in the United States. A telephone rang at the nursing station on evening duty. The caller, identifying himself in a calm, authoritative voice as 'Dr. Smith' from the hospital's psychiatric staff, ordered the attending registered nurse to administer an immediate medication dose to a real patient named Mr. Jones: twenty milligrams of a drug called 'Astroten.' The order violated three explicit hospital safety regulations: first, telephone medication orders were strictly prohibited to prevent transcription disasters; second, Astroten was an unauthorized investigational drug not yet stocked on the ward formulary (the capsules had been secretly placed in the medicine cabinet by researchers); third, the label on the medicine bottle clearly warned: 'Maximum daily dose: 10 milligrams.' The nurse was being ordered to administer double the maximum daily dose of an unauthorized drug by a physician she had never met or seen. Prior to the test, hospital staff surveyed twenty-one senior nurses and graduate students; all twenty-one insisted they would refuse the order. In actual practice, twenty-one out of twenty-two nurses walked immediately to the medicine cabinet, poured the lethal dosage into a tray, and headed down the hallway to inject the patient before researchers intercepted them at the bedside.

1. The Agentic State: Relinquishing Moral Autonomy

In all mammalian species that organize in hierarchical social bands, deference to established leadership is an essential mechanism for collective survival. Without authority, groups fragment into chaotic dominance brawls; coordinated hunting, large-scale defense, agricultural irrigation, and civil logistics become impossible. Authority allows societies to scale beyond Dunbar’s tribal number by centralizing decision rights and enforcing uniform rules.

Furthermore, in complex technological civilizations, deference to specialized authority is a rational heuristic. We do not personally recalculate structural wind-shear equations every time we cross a suspension bridge; we trust civil engineers. We do not independently sequence the viral genome of every vaccine; we trust immunologists. Authority serves as an indispensable cognitive shortcut for delegating expertise.

"The disappearance of a sense of responsibility is the most far-reaching consequence of submission to authority. The person enters an agentic state; he sees himself as an agent for carrying out another person's wishes, and no longer views himself as responsible for his actions."

Stanley Milgram, Obedience to Authority: An Experimental View (1974)
Social Psychology Classic

Yet as Yale social psychologist Stanley Milgram proved in the shadow of the Nuremberg trials, this vital evolutionary adaptation harbors a terrifying vulnerability: The Agentic Shift. When an individual perceives that an order emanates from a legitimate, institutional authority figure, they undergo a psychological transformation.

In the autonomous state, a person views themselves as personally, morally accountable for the consequences of their deeds; their internal conscience adjudicates every action. In the agentic state, the individual no longer defines themselves as the author of the action. They view themselves purely as an instrument carrying out another’s will. Their moral concern shifts entirely from the consequences of the act to the technical competence with which they execute the order and please the superior.

2. Milgram's Shock Generator: The Banality of Obedience

Between 1961 and 1963 in the basement of Linsly-Chittenden Hall at Yale University, Milgram recruited everyday citizens—clerks, mechanics, salesmen, engineers, and teachers—for what was advertised as a study on 'memory and learning.'

The Anatomy of the Milgram Experiment

Participants were assigned the role of 'Teacher' alongside a friendly 47-year-old accountant named Mr. Wallace (an experimental confederate) who played the 'Learner.' Wallace was strapped into an electric chair apparatus in an adjacent room. The Teacher was seated before an imposing metal shock generator featuring thirty switches labeled from 15 volts ('Slight Shock') to 375 volts ('Danger: Severe Shock') and culminating in 450 volts marked ominously with 'XXX.'

The Teacher was instructed to administer an escalating electric shock each time Wallace failed a word-pair recall. In reality, no shocks were delivered; the machine was an elaborate theatrical prop. As the voltage escalated, recorded audio played from behind the wall: Wallace groaned at 75V, complained of heart discomfort at 150V, screamed in agony at 270V, shrieked in mortal terror at 300V, and fell completely silent and unresponsive at 330V.

When distressed Teachers turned to the experimenter—a tall, stern man dressed in a grey laboratory coat—pleading to stop, the experimenter deployed four standardized verbal prods:

  1. "Please continue."
  2. "The experiment requires that you continue."
  3. "It is absolutely essential that you continue."
  4. "You have no other choice, you must go on."

Before conducting the experiment, Milgram surveyed forty psychiatrists across prominent medical schools. The psychoanalysts confidently predicted that less than one-tenth of one percent (only clinical sadists and psychopaths) would administer the maximum 450-volt shock.

The reality horrified the scientific world: 65 percent of participants (26 out of 40) proceeded through the entire board, delivering what they believed were lethal 450-volt shocks to an unconscious human being. They did not do so out of sadism: participants sweat, trembled, bit their lips until they bled, groaned in acute moral torment, and suffered laughing fits of nervous terror. Yet they obeyed. The symbolic authority of a university scientist in a lab coat overrode their fundamental human conscience.

3. The Banality of Evil: Hannah Arendt and Bureaucratic Thoughtlessness

Milgram's laboratory findings provided empirical validation for the philosophical observations of political theorist Hannah Arendt. In 1961, Arendt attended the Jerusalem trial of Adolf Eichmann, the Nazi SS lieutenant colonel who organized the logistical transportation of millions of European Jews to extermination camps.

"The trouble with Eichmann was precisely that so many were like him, and that the many were neither perverted nor sadistic, that they were, and still are, terribly and terrifyingly normal. This normality was much more terrifying than all the atrocities put together."

Hannah Arendt, Eichmann in Jerusalem: A Report on the Banality of Evil (1963)
Political Philosophy

The world expected Eichmann to be a snarling, demonic monster consumed by rabid ideological hatred. Instead, Arendt encountered an ordinary, mediocre, uninspired bureaucrat who spoke entirely in administrative clichés. Eichmann was not motivated by pathological bloodlust; he was consumed by career ambition, punctuality, and an absolute, unthinking deference to superior orders.

Arendt coined the phrase The Banality of Evil to describe how catastrophic atrocities are committed not primarily by sadists, but by ordinary citizens who abdicate the capacity to think for themselves, taking refuge in the comforting certainty of institutional hierarchy.

4. Organizational Disasters: The Steep Authority Gradient

The danger of authority bias is not confined to authoritarian regimes or totalitarian war crimes. In modern aviation, corporate governance, and medicine, an unexamined deference to hierarchy is one of the leading causes of preventable disaster:

Case Study: The 1977 Tenerife Runway Collision

On March 27, 1977, on the fog-shrouded runway of Los Rodeos Airport in the Canary Islands, two Boeing 747s collided in the deadliest aviation accident in history, killing 583 passengers and crew.

The captain of KLM Flight 4805 was Jacob Veldhuyzen van Zanten—the chief flight instructor for KLM, a living aviation legend whose face adorned the airline's international magazine advertisements, and the man who had personally certified both his first officer and flight engineer.

Impatient to take off before weather closed the airport, Captain van Zanten advanced the throttles without receiving takeoff clearance from air traffic control. The junior first officer noticed the missing clearance, but meekly stammered: "Wait a minute, we don't have ATC clearance." Van Zanten sharply replied: "No, I know that. Go ahead and ask."

Moments later, the flight engineer heard a confusing radio transmission suggesting a Pan Am 747 was still taxiing on the runway ahead in the dense fog. The flight engineer asked: "Is he not clear then, that Pan American?" Van Zanten emphatically barked: "Oh, yes!" Intimidated by the captain's legendary status and towering authority, neither junior officer seized the controls or slammed on the brakes. KLM 4805 smashed into the Pan Am jumbo jet at 160 knots. A steep, unquestioned authority gradient silenced competent professionals when speaking up would have saved nearly six hundred lives.

5. Cockpit Resource Management and Psychological Safety

The Tenerife disaster forced commercial aviation to revolutionize its culture. In the 1980s, NASA and global airlines developed Cockpit Resource Management (CRM), fundamentally transforming the cockpit hierarchy:

  • Captains were trained to explicitly solicit input: "I expect you to challenge my decisions whenever you see a discrepancy."
  • Junior first officers were given formal assertiveness protocols (e.g., the PACE model: Probe, Alert, Challenge, Emergency Intervention). If a captain fails to respond to two consecutive safety challenges, the co-pilot is legally mandated to take physical control of the aircraft.
  • In modern high-reliability organizations (submarines, surgical suites, and nuclear facilities), Harvard Business School professor Amy Edmondson proved that Psychological Safety—the shared belief that team members will not be humiliated, punished, or ostracized for speaking up with questions, concerns, or mistakes—is the single greatest predictor of operational excellence and error prevention.

6. Actionable Heuristics: Questioning Authority with Rigor

To preserve your integrity and protect your team from the blinders of authority, cultivate these four mental habits:

Heuristic 1: Strip the Symbolic Trappings (The Naked Emperor Test)

When an expert, executive, or politician delivers an instruction or assertion, mentally strip away their designer suit, corporate title, ivory tower degree, and prestigious podium. Imagine the exact same words being spoken by a tired acquaintance in a coffee shop: Does the argument still stand on its own empirical and logical merits? If the claim collapses without the symbols of rank, it is an appeal to authority, not an argument.

Heuristic 2: The Two-Challenge Rule

Institute an explicit cultural norm in your workplace: if any subordinate, engineer, or nurse sees an ambiguous order or safety violation, they are required by protocol to challenge it twice. Repetition breaks the conversational deference reflex and forces the leader to switch from automatic pilot to conscious deliberation.

Heuristic 3: Separate Role Authority from Domain Competence

Remember that a person’s authority in one domain (e.g., a Nobel prize in physics or billions in real estate) confers zero competence in other domains (such as immunology, geopolitics, or artificial intelligence). Treat authority as strictly bounded to verifiable, specific craft.

Heuristic 4: Foster Red Teams and Appointed Dissenters

In high-stakes corporate meetings, designate an official 'Devil's Advocate' whose formal assignment is to find fatal flaws in the CEO’s plan. By institutionalizing dissent as an official duty, you eliminate the social terror of challenging the leader.

7. Guided Self-Reflection

Audit your relationship with hierarchy and obedience:

DIAGNOSTIC INQUIRIES

1. The Silence Audit: When was the last time you sat silently in a meeting while a senior executive made an assertion or strategic decision you knew was flawed? What kept you quiet—fear of retribution, or the comforting assumption that 'they must know better'?

2. The Authority Gradient in Your Team: Do junior colleagues freely interrupt and critique your ideas, or do they nod politely and wait for your cue before expressing an opinion?

3. The Milgram Line: What instruction from your organization or superiors would you refuse to execute on moral grounds today? Have you drawn that line clearly in your mind before the crisis arrives?

Further Reading and Primary Sources

To master the dynamics of obedience, organizational hierarchy, and bureaucratic power, read these seminal works:

  • Stanley Milgram, Obedience to Authority: An Experimental View (Harper & Row, 1974). The definitive account of the Yale shock experiments and the agentic state.
  • Hannah Arendt, Eichmann in Jerusalem: A Report on the Banality of Evil (Viking Press, 1963). The philosophical foundation of bureaucratic thoughtlessness.
  • Charles K. Hofling et al., "An Experimental Study of Nurse-Physician Relationships," Journal of Nervous and Mental Disease, 143(2) (1966): 171–180. The classic hospital field study on unquestioned medical orders.
  • Amy C. Edmondson, The Fearless Organization: Creating Psychological Safety in the Workplace for Learning, Innovation, and Growth (Wiley, 2018). Practical frameworks for flattening steep authority gradients.
