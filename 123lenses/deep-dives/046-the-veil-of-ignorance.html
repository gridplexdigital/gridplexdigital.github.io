The Veil of Ignorance

True fairness is not what you advocate when you already know your hand of cards: your wealth, gender, health, and social pedigree. A society is genuinely just only if you would freely agree to enter it at random, without knowing in advance whether you will be born at the apex or at the very bottom.

Every parent knows the ancient, foolproof method for settling a ferocious dispute between two children arguing over how to divide a freshly baked chocolate cake. If you allow Child A to slice the cake and also choose which piece to take, Child A will inevitably cut one gargantuan slice for themselves and leave a pathetic sliver for Child B. If you intervene and slice the cake yourself, both children will scream that the division was unfair. The solution that has survived for millennia across every human culture is the celebrated "Cake-Cutting Protocol": Child A slices the cake into two pieces, but Child B gets to choose which piece to take. The psychological mechanics of this simple rule are profound. The moment Child A knows that their sibling will choose first, Child A is placed under an immediate, brilliant constraint: any inequality they engineer will be weaponized against them. Child A now takes a ruler, kneels down to eye level with the kitchen counter, and slices the cake with microscopic, millimeter-level precision. In 1971, Harvard philosopher John Rawls transformed this simple kitchen protocol into the most celebrated thought experiment in twentieth-century political philosophy: The Veil of Ignorance. Rawls recognized that when humans design laws, tax codes, and corporate hierarchies, they are like Child A holding the knife while knowing they get the first pick: billionaires argue that capital gains taxes should be zero; corporate executives argue that CEO salaries should be unregulated; and the healthy argue that welfare should be abolished. To discover what true justice requires, you must force the designers behind a veil where they have no idea which slice of life they will receive.

1. The Original Position: John Rawls' Thought Experiment

In 1971, John Rawls published A Theory of Justice (Harvard University Press), a 600-page philosophical masterpiece that single-handedly revitalized normative political philosophy in the Anglo-American world.

Rawls asked a foundational question: What principles of social justice would free, rational, self-interested individuals agree upon if they gathered to establish the foundational constitutional rules of a society?

In real life, negotiations over the social contract are corrupted by existing advantages: the wealthy, the educated, the physically strong, and the socially connected use their leverage to rig the rules in their favor. To eliminate this structural bias, Rawls asked us to imagine a hypothetical assembly—The Original Position—conducted behind a Veil of Ignorance:

"No one knows his place in society, his class position or social status, nor does any one know his fortune in the distribution of natural assets and abilities, his intelligence, strength, and the like. I shall even assume that the parties do not know their conceptions of the good or their special psychological propensities. The principles of justice are chosen behind a veil of ignorance."

— John Rawls, A Theory of Justice (1971)

Behind the veil, you are stripped of all identity:

  • You do not know whether you will be born male or female.
  • You do not know whether you will be white, black, Asian, or Hispanic.
  • You do not know whether you will be born a mathematical prodigy with high energy, or someone with severe cognitive disabilities and chronic physical illness.
  • You do not know whether you will be born into a loving, wealthy family in Manhattan, or into a broken home in a rural Appalachian trailer park.
  • You do not know your religion, your sexual orientation, or your personal ambition.

Once the veil is lifted, you will be thrust into this society at completely random coordinates. Rawls asked: What social structure would you rationally design?

2. The Maximin Rule and the Two Principles of Justice

Rawls demonstrated that under conditions of radical uncertainty behind the veil, rational human beings would not choose utilitarianism. A utilitarian might say: "Let's create a society where 90 percent of people live like kings, while 10 percent are enslaved." Behind the veil, you would reject that society instantly, because there is a 10 percent probability that you will be one of the slaves!

Instead, rational decision-makers adopt the Maximin Rule: they maximize the minimum outcome. You design the social architecture so that if you happen to draw the short straw and land at the very bottom of the social hierarchy, your life will still be survivable, secure, and dignified.

From this original position, Rawls argued that participants would inevitably agree upon Two Foundational Principles of Justice:

  1. The Greatest Equal Liberty Principle: Every citizen has an absolute, inalienable right to the most extensive scheme of equal basic liberties compatible with a similar scheme of liberties for everyone else. These include freedom of speech, freedom of conscience and religion, the right to vote and hold public office, freedom of assembly, and freedom from arbitrary arrest. Basic civil liberties can never be traded away, compromised, or sacrificed for economic growth.
  2. The Difference Principle (with Fair Equality of Opportunity): Social and economic inequalities are permissible if and only if they satisfy two conditions:
    • Condition A (Fair Equality of Opportunity): Offices and prestigious positions must be genuinely open to all, regardless of social background. A poor child must have access to the exact same quality of education, healthcare, and training as a wealthy child.
    • Condition B (The Difference Principle): Inequalities in wealth and income are justified only if they work to the maximum benefit of the least-advantaged members of society.

Notice that Rawls does not advocate pure, flat economic equality. If paying higher salaries to neurosurgeons, software architects, and entrepreneurs incentivizes them to invent life-saving medical devices and create productive jobs that make the poorest citizens significantly healthier and wealthier than they would be under flat equality, then that inequality is not merely permitted—it is just! But inequality that serves only to lavish luxury on the elite while leaving the bottom destitute is categorically condemned.

3. The Myth of Meritocracy: The Natural Lottery

One of the most radical and challenging aspects of Rawls' philosophy is his total dismantling of the concept of moral desert in relation to natural talents.

Most successful people believe that they deserve their wealth because they worked hard. Rawls pointed out that our ability to achieve success rests entirely upon two arbitrary lotteries:

  1. The Genetic and Social Lottery: Being born with a high IQ, exceptional physical coordination, emotional stability, good health, and a supportive family is not an achievement; it is a genetic and environmental lottery ticket. You did nothing to earn your raw intelligence or your family zip code.
  2. The Cultural Appreciation Lottery: A seven-foot-tall human with exceptional athletic reflexes happens to be born in twenty-first-century America, where society awards NBA players $40 million a year to shoot a ball through a hoop. If that exact same individual had been born in medieval Europe, society would not have paid them millions; they would have spent their life digging ditches or working as an armored shock soldier. The fact that a capitalist market values your specific skills is an accident of history, not personal virtue.

Rawls concluded that society should view natural talents as a common social asset. Those blessed by the natural lottery are entitled to enjoy their success, but only on the condition that they share a portion of the harvest with those who drew unfortunate numbers in the genetic and social draw.

4. Empirical and Historical Case Studies

Case A: The Nordic Model and "Flexicurity"

The closest real-world institutional approximations of Rawlsian political philosophy are the social democratic models of the Nordic nations: Denmark, Sweden, and Norway.

Critics of the Nordic model often mischaracterize it as rigid state socialism. In reality, Denmark and Sweden maintain hyper-competitive, open-market capitalist economies: they have low corporate taxes, strong private property rights, and virtually zero trade barriers.

However, the system is grounded in the Difference Principle through a framework known as "Flexicurity":

  • Complete Flexibility for Employers: Companies can fire workers easily and rapidly with virtually zero bureaucratic friction, allowing businesses to adapt to changing global markets.
  • Absolute Security for the Least Advantaged: When a worker loses their job, they do not face catastrophic destitution, homelessness, or loss of healthcare. The state provides up to two years of robust unemployment compensation, universal high-quality healthcare, free child care, and fully subsidized vocational retraining in emerging technologies.

Behind a veil of ignorance, any rational human being would eagerly agree to live in Denmark: if you are born an entrepreneurial genius, you can build a massive global company (like Lego, Spotify, or Novo Nordisk); if you are born with a chronic illness or lose your job to automation, your family remains housed, fed, and educated. The baseline is civilized and secure.

Case B: Blind Auditions in Major Symphonies (1970s)

Until the early 1970s, the five most prestigious symphony orchestras in the United States (the New York Philharmonic, Boston Symphony, Chicago Symphony, Cleveland Orchestra, and Philadelphia Orchestra) were staffed almost exclusively by white male musicians. Women comprised less than 5 percent of professional orchestral players.

Music directors and conductors adamantly defended this disparity, asserting that women "simply lacked the lung capacity to play brass instruments" or "the physical strength to play the double bass." Conductors claimed they evaluated auditions purely on musical merit.

In the 1970s, major orchestras implemented a physical version of the Veil of Ignorance: Blind Auditions. Auditioning musicians played behind a large opaque cloth screen, identifying themselves only by an assigned number. To prevent conductors from identifying female candidates by the click of high-heeled shoes on hardwood stages, orchestras laid down thick carpet runners and required musicians to remove their shoes.

The results shattered the prejudice of the conductors. Behind the veil, when judges could evaluate only acoustic sound without knowing the musician’s gender, the probability that a female musician would advance through preliminary audition rounds surged by 50 percent, and the probability of being hired into a premier orchestra tripled. By eliminating irrelevant status cues, the veil revealed pure artistic competence.

5. Actionable Heuristics for Practicing Rawlsian Thinking

  1. The "Worst-Case Role-Reversal" Test: Whenever you design an organizational compensation structure, a customer terms-of-service contract, or a team policy, apply the veil: "If I had to enter this company tomorrow as the lowest-paid entry-level customer service representative or outsourced janitor, would I find this policy fair, respectful, and humane?" If the answer is no, your policy is not just; you are simply exploiting your management leverage.
  2. The "Cake-Cutter" Governance Architecture: In organizational management, separate the authority to design options from the authority to select them. If Department A designs the project milestones, let Department B select the resource allocation; if an executive drafts an organizational restructuring, ensure their own department is subject to the same oversight. When the cutter does not choose, fairness is guaranteed.
  3. Audit the Anonymity of Your Selection Pipelines: Strip identifying personal data (names, photos, gender, elite university pedigrees, addresses) from the early stages of hiring resumes and grant evaluations. Evaluating candidates strictly on anonymized work samples and standardized skill tests allows true excellence to emerge unhindered by unconscious pedigree bias.
  4. Dismantle Moral Arrogance Around Success: Whenever you celebrate a personal promotion, a profitable investment exit, or an academic honor, pause and mentally trace your hidden blessings: your genetic health, your early childhood stability, the public infrastructure that supported you, and the historical timing that valued your specific skills. Shift from self-righteous entitlement to humble stewardship.

6. Guided Self-Reflection

Examine your institutional and political beliefs through the veil of ignorance:

  • If I were to be reborn tomorrow with a roll of the cosmic dice into a random family in my city, what specific laws and social safety nets would I pray existed?
  • Where in my company or family am I designing rules that disproportionately benefit my own role, simply because I hold the knife?
  • Am I attributing my financial success entirely to my own grit, while ignoring the massive genetic and social lottery tickets I drew at birth?
  • How does my organization treat its least-advantaged workers—the cleaners, the interns, the contractors—and does that treatment reflect the Difference Principle?

Further Reading and Primary Sources

  • Rawls, John. A Theory of Justice (Harvard University Press, 1971). The monumental twentieth-century masterpiece on the original position, the veil of ignorance, and the difference principle.
  • Rawls, John. Justice as Fairness: A Restatement (Harvard University Press, 2001). A refined, accessible summary of Rawls' mature political philosophy.
  • Goldin, Claudia, and Cecilia Rouse. "Orchestrating Impartiality: The Impact of 'Blind' Auditions on Female Musicians." American Economic Review 90, no. 4 (2000): 715–741. The classic empirical study on blind evaluation.
  • Sandel, Michael J. Liberalism and the Limits of Justice (Cambridge University Press, 1982). A famous communitarian critique of Rawlsian individual autonomy.
  • Sen, Amartya. The Idea of Justice (Harvard University Press, 2009). A Nobel Prize-winning expansion shifting focus from ideal institutions to practical capability reduction of injustice.
