Why Technology Does Not Automatically Create Progress

Tools expand the physical and computational boundaries of human capability, but they do not automatically elevate human wisdom or distribute well-being. Technology is an amplifier: its societal consequences are dictated by the institutions, incentives, and moral governance that direct its force.

In the autumn of 1793, an ingenious young Massachusetts mechanic named Eli Whitney arrived at Mulberry Grove, a plantation outside Savannah, Georgia. Whitney observed enslaved workers painstakingly separating green seeds from the sticky fibers of short-staple upland cotton by hand—an excruciating, blistered ordeal that yielded barely a single pound of clean lint per worker per day. Moved by mechanical curiosity and early industrial philanthropy, Whitney designed a simple hand-cranked wooden cylinder studded with wire teeth that pulled cotton fibers through a wire grid, separating the seeds fifty times faster than human hands. Whitney and his Northern contemporaries sincerely believed that this labor-saving invention would deal a mortal blow to the institution of slavery by eliminating the economic need for coerced field labor. The historical reality was an apocalyptic inversion: by converting short-staple cotton into an astronomical commercial goldmine, the cotton gin rescued a dying agrarian system. Over the next six decades, the population of enslaved human beings in the American South exploded from seven hundred thousand to nearly four million, fueling a brutal domestic slave trade that ripped apart one million families and ignited the bloodiest civil war in Western history. A tool designed to liberate human labor anchored the chains of human bondage.

1. Kranzberg’s First Law: Technology Is Neither Good Nor Bad; Nor Is It Neutral

In contemporary Silicon Valley discourse, technology is frequently wrapped in a secular theology of automatic redemption. Innovators proclaim that connecting the world, increasing computational clock speeds, or releasing open-source models naturally leads to human flourishing, democracy, and liberation. When negative externalities occur—disinformation, surveillance, mental health epidemics—technologists dismiss them as mere 'user errors' or temporary growing pains.

In his 1986 presidential address to the Society for the History of Technology, historian Melvin Kranzberg formulated six laws that shattered this technological utopianism. His first law remains the supreme foundational maxim of science and technology studies:

"Technology is neither good nor bad; nor is it neutral. Technology's interaction with the social ecology is such that technical developments frequently have environmental, social, and human consequences that go far beyond the immediate purposes of the technical devices and practices themselves."

Melvin Kranzberg, Technology and History: 'Kranzberg's Laws' (1986)
History of Technology

Why did Kranzberg insist that technology is not neutral? A neutral tool would be an inert passive object, indifferent to its context. But technological systems are never neutral: they are embedded with physical architecture, capital requirements, and affordances that inherently favor specific social configurations over others.

A nuclear power plant, for instance, requires centralized military security, authoritarian command-and-control safety oversight, and massive state capital; it cannot be managed as an anarchic communal co-op. A rooftop solar panel, by contrast, naturally lends itself to decentralized, distributed local ownership. The architecture of the machine pre-structures the politics of the society that operates it.

2. Neil Postman and the Faustian Bargain of Technopoly

In his prophetic 1992 critique Technopoly: The Surrender of Culture to Technology, media theorist Neil Postman warned against the uncritical adoration of technical novelty. Postman argued that every technological transformation is a Faustian bargain:

"A new technology does not add or subtract something. It changes everything. Technological change is not additive; it is ecological. The culture always pays a price for what it incorporates: technology giveth, and technology taketh away."

Neil Postman, Technopoly: The Surrender of Culture to Technology (1992)
Media Ecology

Postman illustrated this with the myth of King Thamus and the god Theuth from Plato’s Phaedrus. When Theuth presented the invention of written letters to the King of Egypt, he boasted that writing was an elixir for memory and wisdom. King Thamus replied that writing would achieve the exact opposite: it would implant forgetfulness in men's souls, because people would rely on external marks rather than internalizing knowledge within their living minds. They would receive an abundance of information without true judgment, appearing knowledgeable while remaining ignorant.

Every technology presents winners and losers. The automobile provided breathtaking personal mobility, but it dismantled pedestrian village communities, introduced urban smog, and killed millions in highway collisions. The digital smartphone granted instantaneous access to the accumulated knowledge of human civilization, but it shredded human attention spans, created commercial surveillance monopolies, and induced an epidemic of adolescent loneliness. Technology advances along a vector of power, not necessarily along a vector of wisdom.

3. Historical Case Studies: When Innovation Deepened Misery

Case Study: Engels’ Pause and the Industrial Wage Lag

During the British Industrial Revolution between 1780 and 1840, technology advanced at a rate never before witnessed in human history: the steam engine, the mechanical spinning mule, the power loom, and modern metallurgy exploded industrial productivity.

Yet in a famous economic study, economic historian Robert Allen documented what is now known as Engels’ Pause (named after Friedrich Engels’ 1845 investigation, The Condition of the Working Class in England). For sixty continuous years of explosive output:

  • Real wages for British industrial workers remained completely stagnant, while corporate profits doubled and tripled.
  • Working hours increased to fourteen hours a day, six days a week, with widespread use of hazardous child labor in textile mills and coal pits.
  • Life expectancy in industrial cities like Manchester and Liverpool collapsed to less than twenty-five years, compared to forty years in rural agricultural districts.

Technological innovation generated colossal wealth, but that wealth was captured entirely by industrial capital owners. It was not until the mid-nineteenth century—when British workers organized trade unions, parliament passed compulsory factory acts, and municipal governments built clean public sanitation and water networks—that the benefits of industrial technology finally translated into human progress for ordinary citizens. Progress required institutional struggle, not merely mechanical invention.

Case Study: Thomas Midgley Jr. and the Miracle of Leaded Gasoline

In December 1921, an American mechanical engineer and chemist named Thomas Midgley Jr., working under Charles Kettering at General Motors, discovered that adding tetraethyllead (TEL) to petroleum gasoline eliminated violent engine 'knocking' in automobile internal combustion engines, dramatically improving engine compression and power.

Chemical manufacturers and oil companies hailed TEL as a miracle of modern chemical engineering. Advertised simply as 'Ethyl' to hide the toxic word 'lead' from consumers, leaded gasoline was sold worldwide for over half a century.

The ecological consequence was a generational public health catastrophe: billions of gallons of lead exhaust poured into global urban atmospheres, contaminating soils and bloodstreams. Epidemiologists estimate that atmospheric lead exposure lowered the IQ of hundreds of millions of children across the twentieth century and contributed to millions of premature cardiovascular deaths.

A few years later, Midgley invented chlorofluorocarbons (CFCs) to provide non-flammable, non-toxic refrigeration, unwittingly initiating the destruction of the Earth’s stratospheric ozone layer. A brilliant technologist operating within an uncritical commercial mandate had, with two inventions, damaged the cognitive health of an entire generation and weakened the planetary biological shield.

Case Study: Norman Borlaug and the Green Revolution

In the 1960s, agronomist Norman Borlaug developed semi-dwarf, high-yield, disease-resistant wheat varieties in Mexico, India, and Pakistan. Known as the Green Revolution, this technological triumph is credited with saving an estimated one billion human beings from catastrophic famine, earning Borlaug the 1970 Nobel Peace Prize.

Yet as ecological economists and agrarian sociologists documented in subsequent decades, the technology was accompanied by profound, unforeseen systemic trade-offs:

  • The miraculous dwarf crops required massive, recurring inputs of synthetic petrochemical nitrogen fertilizers, chemical pesticides, and intensive diesel-pump groundwater irrigation.
  • In regions like the Indian Punjab, water tables collapsed by hundreds of feet, soils became salinized, cancer rates spiked from chemical runoff, and millions of smallholder peasant farmers who could not afford expensive patented seeds and fertilizer inputs fell into crushing debt, accelerating rural displacement.

Borlaug’s seeds were an uncontested technological triumph of plant genetics, but they proved once again that technology cannot solve the deeper socio-economic questions of land tenure, ecological limits, and equitable water governance.

4. Brynjolfsson and McAfee: The Second Machine Age and Skill-Biased Technological Change

In their landmark analysis The Second Machine Age, MIT economists Erik Brynjolfsson and Andrew McAfee examined how digital automation and artificial intelligence interact with modern labor markets. They introduced a crucial distinction between bounty and spread:

Bounty vs. Spread in Digital Technology

  • The Bounty: The staggering increase in overall societal wealth, computing power, digital services, and consumer choice delivered by exponential technology (Moore's Law).
  • The Spread: The widening economic and social inequality that occurs when digital automation replaces routine middle-skill labor (factory workers, data clerks, paralegals) while showering astronomical financial returns on a tiny cognitive elite of capital owners and superstar software architects.

Technology is skill-biased. In the absence of proactive social contracts, modern digital automation does not lift all boats; it lifts the yachts while capsizing the lifeboats. When automation enriches capital while gutting working-class employment, the result is political polarization, democratic instability, and populist backlash.

5. Actionable Heuristics for Grounded Technologists and Citizens

To avoid the illusions of techno-utopianism, evaluate every new tool and platform through four critical filters:

Filter 1: The 'Who Loses?' Diagnostic (The Distributional Test)

Whenever a technologist or corporate leader promises that a new tool will 'revolutionize' an industry, never ask only: What does this make possible? Always demand: Who captures the profits, and who bears the uncompensated human costs? If an automation tool boosts productivity by 20% while rendering forty thousand workers unemployable without retraining, it is an extraction mechanism, not an unalloyed public triumph.

Filter 2: Beware the Solutionist Trap (Evgeny Morozov)

In To Save Everything, Click Here, critic Evgeny Morozov attacked technological solutionism: the delusional belief that complex, messy, moral and political problems (poverty, educational inequality, democratic polarization) can be neatly solved by an algorithm, an app, or a blockchain token. Recognize that political problems require political solutions: negotiation, institutional design, empathy, and compromise.

Filter 3: Measure Second-Order Ecological Consequences Early

Innovate with precautionary humility. Build red teams whose sole mission is to imagine how the tool will be weaponized by bad actors, how it will alter social norms, and what systemic waste or pollution it will generate at planetary scale. Design the governance guardrails before deploying the algorithm, not as an afterthought after damage is done.

Filter 4: Subordinate Technics to Human Flourishing (Mumford's Law)

Remind yourself that the ultimate measure of civilization is not the speed of its microprocessors or the altitude of its skyscrapers, but the character of its citizens, the justice of its institutions, and the health of its natural environment. Technology is a means; human dignity is the end.

6. Guided Self-Reflection

Audit your own relationship with modern tools and devices:

DIAGNOSTIC INQUIRIES

1. The Faustian Inventory: What modern digital tools (smartphones, algorithmic social media feeds, notification alerts) have you welcomed into your daily life for convenience, that have secretly extracted your deep focus, reading comprehension, and peace of mind?

2. The Efficiency vs. Meaning Check: Where are you using digital software to automate human relationships—sending templated messages or birthday emails—mistaking operational speed for authentic human care?

3. The Technopoly Bias: In your workplace, do you instinctively assume that a quantitative, computerized, algorithmic answer is superior to the nuanced, qualitative judgment of a seasoned human practitioner?

Further Reading and Primary Sources

To master the history, philosophy, and political economy of technology, explore these essential works:

  • Melvin Kranzberg, "Technology and History: 'Kranzberg's Laws'," Technology and Culture, 27(3) (1986): 544–560. The founding manifesto on the non-neutrality of technology.
  • Neil Postman, Technopoly: The Surrender of Culture to Technology (Vintage, 1992). The classic critique of technological ideology and cultural erosion.
  • Erik Brynjolfsson & Andrew McAfee, The Second Machine Age: Work, Progress, and Prosperity in a Time of Brilliant Technologies (W. W. Norton & Co., 2014). Landmark analysis of digital economics, bounty, and spread.
  • Lewis Mumford, Technics and Civilization (Harcourt, Brace and Co., 1934). The foundational historical treatise on the machines that shaped modern consciousness.
