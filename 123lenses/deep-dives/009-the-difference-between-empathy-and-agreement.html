The difference between empathy and agreement

Empathy is the intellectual and emotional effort to understand another person's subjective reality. It does not require endorsing their worldview, sharing their conclusions, or tolerating destructive conduct.

📖 12-Minute Deep Read (2,400 words) ⚡ Short on time? Switch to the 2-minute field note summary →

In 1983, Daryl Davis, an African-American blues pianist who had performed with Chuck Berry and Jerry Lee Lewis, sat down in a roadside bar in Frederick, Maryland. After his set, a white patron approached him to say it was the first time he had ever seen a Black man play piano like Jerry Lee Lewis. Davis explained that Lewis had learned his boogie-woogie style from Black blues pianists. The patron was skeptical, but invited Davis for a drink. As conversation progressed, the man quietly admitted that he was a dues-paying member of the Ku Klux Klan.

Rather than retreating in fear or screaming in moral indignation, Davis felt a strange surge of curiosity. For years, he had carried a single unresolved question from childhood: "How can you hate me when you don't even know me?" Over the subsequent three decades, Davis arranged private, one-on-one meetings with dozens of high-ranking Klan leaders, including Imperial Wizard Roger Kelly. Davis never concealed his identity, never validated their racist ideology, and never softened his moral opposition to white supremacy. But he listened. He asked detailed questions about their families, their childhood fears, their religious interpretations, and their economic anxieties.

Over thirty years, Davis's radical willingness to understand the mental universe of his sworn enemies caused more than two hundred Ku Klux Klan members to renounce their robes, dismantle local chapters, and hand Davis their hoods as souvenirs of their transformation. When asked by journalists how he could bear to sit in diners drinking coffee with men who openly advocated for his subjugation, Davis offered a profound philosophical distinction: "Give them a platform. You invite them, and you listen to them. You don't have to agree with what they say, but you must understand how they came to believe it. When you are fighting an enemy, you take the time to learn their tactics, their mindset. How can you expect to change someone's mind if you do not understand where that mind currently lives?"

Davis’s life demonstrates an intellectual principle that modern political discourse has almost entirely forgotten: Empathy is an instrument of perception; agreement is an act of endorsement. Confusing the two is one of the primary drivers of cultural polarization, organizational gridlock, and diplomatic catastrophe.

1. The Two Empathies: Affective Contagion vs. Cognitive Perspective

In popular psychology and corporate seminar culture, empathy is treated as an undifferentiated moral virtue—a warm, fuzzy impulse synonymous with compassion and kindness. But in neuroscience and moral philosophy, empathy is a multifaceted phenomenon containing two fundamentally distinct cognitive architectures:

  • Emotional (Affective) Empathy: The automatic neural mirroring of another person's emotional state. When a child cries and a mother feels physical tightness in her throat, or when someone flinches watching a stranger stub their toe, the brain's mirror neuron system is firing. This is an ancient, involuntary physiological contagion.
  • Cognitive Empathy (Perspective-Taking): The deliberate, conscious capacity to construct a mental model of another person's inner world—their beliefs, incentives, fears, assumptions, and constraints—without necessarily feeling their physical emotions.

In his landmark work The Age of Empathy, primatologist Frans de Waal illustrated this distinction through the Russian-doll model of empathy across primates:

"Empathy is not an all-or-nothing phenomenon. It covers all processes in which one individual's state triggers a related state in another. At its core is perception-action coupling and emotional contagion. But surrounding that core are higher cognitive layers: perspective-taking and targeted helping. Higher primates and humans can observe another's distress, understand the external cause of that distress, and determine a response that addresses the other's problem rather than merely alleviating one's own vicarious discomfort."

Frans de Waal, The Age of Empathy: Nature's Lessons for a Kinder Society (Harmony Books, 2009)
Primary Source

When people say, "I cannot empathize with that politician / coworker / rival," they usually mean they cannot feel warm affective sympathy toward them. But cognitive empathy requires no affection whatsoever. An intelligence officer interrogating a hostile operative, a defense attorney preparing a cross-examination, and an emergency room doctor triaging a violent patient must all exercise world-class cognitive empathy precisely because personal affection would cloud their professional judgment.

2. Paul Bloom: The Dark Side of Emotional Empathy

In his provocative 2016 treatise Against Empathy: The Case for Rational Compassion, Yale cognitive psychologist Paul Bloom warned that relying on emotional empathy as a moral compass regularly leads to catastrophic, discriminatory outcomes.

"Empathy is like a spotlight: it directs attention to what is right in front of us, making it vivid and emotionally compelling. But spotlights have narrow focus; they illuminate one individual while leaving the surrounding darkness untouched. Empathy is biased, pushing us in the direction of parochialism and racism. We feel far more empathy for people who look like us, speak our language, and share our values. If moral judgment is guided solely by empathy, it becomes an engine of tribal favoritism rather than universal justice."

Paul Bloom, Against Empathy: The Case for Rational Compassion (Ecco / HarperCollins, 2016)
Primary Source

Bloom demonstrates that emotional empathy is innumerate. It cares passionately about a single identifiable victim whose photograph appears in a newspaper, while ignoring statistical suffering affecting millions of faceless individuals.

Furthermore, emotional empathy easily fuels cruelty: demagogues throughout history have mobilized populations to wage horrific wars by inflaming empathy for the suffering of their own in-group, which immediately justifies merciless vengeance against the out-group.

The antidote to this parochial bias is not heartlessness, but what Bloom calls Rational Compassion paired with Cognitive Perspective-Taking: the disciplined, dispassionate effort to understand how the world looks from multiple vantage points, governed by principles of fairness and evidence rather than visceral gut resonance.

3. The Philosophical Foundation: David Hume on Sympathy and Reason

Three centuries before modern neuroimaging, Scottish Enlightenment philosopher David Hume explored this exact tension in his 1739 masterpiece A Treatise of Human Nature. Hume observed that the human mind naturally resonates with the emotions of those in close proximity:

"The minds of all men are similar in their feelings and operations; nor can any one be actuated by any affection, to which all others are not, in some degree, susceptible. As in strings wound up to the same pitch, the motion of one naturally communicates itself to the rest. ... Yet we must correct these sentiments by reflection, else our moral judgments would fluctuate with every change of distance and circumstance. We frequently bestow praise on actions that run contrary to our own immediate interests, because we survey them from a general point of view."

David Hume, A Treatise of Human Nature (1739; Oxford Philosophical Texts)
Primary Source

Hume recognized that raw sympathy is inherently local and distorted by distance. To live in a cooperative commonwealth, citizens must construct a "general point of view"—a detached, cognitive framework that separates their emotional affinity from their evaluation of justice. To understand why a person acts the way they do is an epistemic duty; whether their action conforms to justice is an ethical judgment.

Geopolitical Case Study

Llewellyn Thompson and the Cuban Missile Crisis

In October 1962, during the height of the Cuban Missile Crisis, the American National Security Council was deliberating how to respond to two contradictory letters from Soviet Premier Nikita Khrushchev. The first letter, emotional and private, suggested that the Soviets would remove their nuclear missiles from Cuba in exchange for a public American guarantee not to invade the island. The second letter, published twelve hours later over Radio Moscow, was aggressive and uncompromising, demanding the immediate withdrawal of American Jupiter missiles from Turkey.

The Joint Chiefs of Staff and most of President Kennedy's advisors urged immediate airstrikes and an amphibious invasion of Cuba. They argued that Khrushchev was negotiating in bad faith and seeking to humiliate the United States.

The one man who altered history was Llewellyn Thompson, a quiet former American ambassador to the Soviet Union who had spent years socializing with Khrushchev. Thompson urged Kennedy to ignore the second, belligerent letter and respond exclusively to the first. Thompson explained Khrushchev's internal psychological reality:

"Khrushchev is in deep trouble with his hardline military generals in the Kremlin. He knows he has made a catastrophic blunder by placing missiles in Cuba. He wants a way out, but he cannot afford to look weak in front of his politburo. If you publicly humiliate him, his generals will depose him and escalate. You must give him a face-saving victory: promise never to invade Cuba, and secretly agree to remove the obsolete missiles from Turkey later on. Make him look like the peacemaker who saved the world."

Kennedy adopted Thompson’s perspective-taking strategy. Khrushchev accepted the terms, the Soviet freighters turned around, and the world stepped back from the brink of nuclear war. Thompson did not agree with Soviet communism; he detested it. But his ability to see the world through Khrushchev's eyes was the single factor that prevented global annihilation.

4. The Modern Pathology: Moral Equivocation and Outrage Culture

In contemporary public discourse, this distinction has suffered catastrophic collapse. In media, academia, and social networks, attempting to explain the motives, incentives, or historical context behind an adversary’s action is frequently attacked as a moral betrayal:

  • If an analyst explains the geopolitical anxieties driving Russian or Chinese foreign policy, critics accuse them of being a "puppet" or "apologist."
  • If an executive explains the financial incentives causing union leaders to reject a compromise, colleagues accuse them of "siding with the opposition."
  • If a manager tries to understand why an underperforming employee is burned out, peers complain that they are "making excuses for incompetence."

This confusion stems from a primitive cognitive error: the belief that explanation equals exoneration.

To explain why an engine failed is not to approve of the failure. To understand the psychological pathology of an authoritarian dictator is not to endorse tyranny. In fact, refusing to understand the adversary's cognitive world guarantees that you will remain blind to their future moves, incapable of negotiating effectively, and helpless to prevent their aggression.

5. The Intellectual Discipline: Rapoport’s Rules

How can an individual practice radical cognitive empathy without succumbing to moral surrender?

In his book Intuition Pumps and Other Tools for Thinking, philosopher Daniel Dennett codified the four rules of intellectual engagement originally formulated by mathematical psychologist Anatol Rapoport:

"How to compose a successful critical commentary: 1. You should attempt to re-express your target's position so clearly, vividly, and fairly that your target says: 'Thanks, I wish I'd thought of putting it that way.' 2. You should list any points of agreement (especially if they are not matters of general or widespread agreement). 3. You should mention anything you have learned from your target. 4. Only then are you permitted to say so much as a single word of rebuttal or criticism."

Daniel C. Dennett, Intuition Pumps and Other Tools for Thinking (W. W. Norton, 2013)
Primary Source

When you master Rapoport's Rules, an astonishing psychological transformation occurs. When your opponent hears you articulate their perspective with greater clarity, nuance, and generosity than they themselves could muster, their defensive walls drop. They no longer perceive you as a threat; they perceive you as a discerning listener. Only then do they become psychologically capable of hearing your rebuttal.

6. Practical Application: Cultivating Cognitive Perspective

1. The "Steel-Manning" Protocol

Never attack a "straw man"—a caricature of the opponent's argument designed for easy demolition. Instead, steel-man their position: construct the strongest, most coherent version of their thesis. If you cannot articulate why an intelligent, morally serious person could hold the view you oppose, you do not understand the issue yet.

2. The Boundary of Accountability

Empathy governs how you listen; accountability governs how you act. You can listen to a colleague's personal stress with genuine empathy, acknowledging that their exhaustion is real, while firmly stating: "I understand how overwhelming this week has been for you, and I sympathize. At the same time, this client deliverable must meet our security standards by 5 PM, so we cannot ship this incomplete code. Let's figure out what emergency resources we can reassign to help you."

3. The Roman Krznaric "Outspective" Habit

In his book Empathy, philosopher Roman Krznaric recommends the practice of "conversational out-spection": when encountering someone whose views bewilder or disgust you, do not ask "What is wrong with them?" Ask: "What lived experiences, historical fears, or social incentives make this behavior appear completely rational from the inside of their skull?"

A QUESTION TO CARRY

Who in your life or public sphere do you disagree with most vehemently, and could you describe their internal world so faithfully that they would say: "Yes, that is exactly how I feel"?
